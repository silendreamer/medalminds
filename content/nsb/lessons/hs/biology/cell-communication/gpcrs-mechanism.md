#### GPCR Structure and Activation
G-protein coupled receptors are transmembrane proteins with seven hydrophobic helices that span the lipid bilayer. When a ligand binds the extracellular face, the receptor changes conformation, activating the associated heterotrimeric G-protein on the cytoplasmic face. The G-protein has three subunits: Gα, Gβ, and Gγ. In the resting state, Gα is bound to GDP. Ligand binding causes Gα to swap GDP for GTP and dissociate from Gβγ.

#### The cAMP Second Messenger Pathway
Activated Gα (bound to GTP) diffuses along the membrane and activates **adenylyl cyclase**, which converts ATP into **cyclic AMP (cAMP)**. cAMP is a second messenger — it carries the signal deeper into the cell. cAMP binds and activates **protein kinase A (PKA)**, which phosphorylates serine and threonine residues on target proteins, changing their activity. PKA can activate enzymes, regulate ion channels, or enter the nucleus to phosphorylate transcription factors.

#### Signal Termination
The G-protein is self-inactivating: Gα has intrinsic GTPase activity, converting GTP back to GDP, which returns it to the inactive state. Phosphodiesterase degrades cAMP back to AMP, shutting off PKA. This built-in "off switch" prevents signals from running indefinitely.

#### Examples of GPCR Signaling
Epinephrine (adrenaline) binds β-adrenergic receptors (GPCRs) on heart muscle, raising cAMP and increasing heart rate. The same hormone binds α-adrenergic receptors (different GPCRs) on blood vessels, causing constriction. The diversity of GPCR types allows one hormone to have different effects in different tissues depending on which GPCR type is expressed.

#### Pharmacology of GPCRs
Over 30% of all FDA-approved drugs target GPCRs. Beta-blockers (like propranolol) block β-adrenergic GPCRs, slowing heart rate in hypertension. Opioid painkillers activate GPCR-coupled receptors in the nervous system. Antihistamines block histamine GPCRs. GPCRs are among the most druggable targets in medicine.
