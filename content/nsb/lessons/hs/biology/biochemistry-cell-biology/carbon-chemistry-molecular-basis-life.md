#### Carbon's Unique Bonding Properties
Carbon (C) has 4 valence electrons and forms 4 covalent bonds — to other carbons, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus. This allows carbon to form chains, branches, and rings of virtually unlimited length and complexity. Carbon-carbon bonds are strong (C-C = 347 kJ/mol; C=C = 614 kJ/mol) yet numerous enough to store large amounts of chemical energy. No other element approaches carbon's structural diversity: silicon, carbon's periodic table neighbor, forms rigid silica frameworks rather than flexible organic molecules.

#### Functional Groups
Functional groups are specific arrangements of atoms that confer consistent chemical properties regardless of the carbon skeleton they're attached to. Key groups: hydroxyl (-OH) makes compounds polar and reactive; carbonyl (C=O) as aldehyde (-CHO) or ketone (C=O in chain) affects reactivity and classification of sugars; carboxyl (-COOH) makes compounds acidic (ionizes to -COO⁻ + H⁺); amino (-NH₂) makes compounds basic (accepts H⁺ to form -NH₃⁺); phosphate (-OPO₃²⁻) allows energy transfer and ionization; sulfhydryl (-SH) forms disulfide bonds in proteins.

#### Isomers
Isomers are molecules with the same molecular formula but different arrangements. Structural isomers have different bonding arrangements (e.g., butane vs. isobutane, both C₄H₁₀). Stereoisomers have the same bonds but different spatial arrangements. Enantiomers are mirror images (like left and right hands) — biologically important because enzymes are stereospecific: L-amino acids are used in proteins, D-sugars in carbohydrates. Cis-trans isomers (geometric isomers) differ in arrangement around a double bond — cis-fatty acids are kinked (healthier); trans-fatty acids are straight (more like saturated fats, associated with cardiovascular disease).

#### Dehydration Synthesis and Hydrolysis
Monomers join into polymers via dehydration synthesis (condensation reactions): each new bond releases a water molecule. Hydrolysis breaks polymers back into monomers by adding water across the bond. These reactions are catalyzed by enzymes. Dehydration synthesis builds carbohydrates (glycosidic bonds), proteins (peptide bonds), nucleic acids (phosphodiester bonds), and triglycerides (ester bonds). Hydrolysis is how your digestive system breaks down food: amylase hydrolyzes starch, proteases hydrolyze proteins, lipases hydrolyze triglycerides.
