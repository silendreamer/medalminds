#### Nucleotide Structure and Polymer Assembly
Nucleotides are the monomers of nucleic acids, each consisting of: a pentose sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), a nitrogenous base, and one to three phosphate groups. Nitrogenous bases: purines (two rings) = adenine and guanine; pyrimidines (one ring) = cytosine, thymine (DNA only), and uracil (RNA only). Nucleotides join via phosphodiester bonds between the 3' hydroxyl of one sugar and the 5' phosphate of the next, creating a backbone with directionality: synthesis always proceeds 5' → 3'. The sequence of bases encodes genetic information.

#### DNA Double Helix
DNA consists of two antiparallel strands wound into a right-handed helix. The sugar-phosphate backbone is on the outside; the bases face inward and pair via hydrogen bonds: A-T (2 hydrogen bonds), G-C (3 hydrogen bonds). G≡C pairs are stronger and higher G+C content raises the melting temperature (Tm) of the duplex — relevant for PCR primer design. Base pairing rules (Chargaff's rules): [A]=[T] and [G]=[C] in any double-stranded DNA. The major groove (wider) and minor groove (narrower) are where DNA-binding proteins make specific contact with base sequences. The helix has a pitch of 3.4 nm per 10 base pairs, with each base pair separated by 0.34 nm.

#### RNA Structure and Types
RNA is single-stranded and uses ribose and uracil. Three major types: messenger RNA (mRNA) carries protein-coding sequence from DNA in the nucleus to ribosomes in the cytoplasm; ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is the catalytic and structural component of ribosomes (23S/16S in prokaryotes, 28S/18S in eukaryotes) — the ribosome is fundamentally an RNA enzyme (ribozyme); transfer RNA (tRNA) carries amino acids to the ribosome during translation, with an anticodon loop that pairs with mRNA codons. RNA's single-stranded nature allows it to fold into complex secondary and tertiary structures via intramolecular base pairing (hairpin loops, stem-loops), enabling its catalytic functions.

#### ATP: The Energy Currency
ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a nucleotide with special energy-storage function. The two phosphoanhydride bonds (connecting the three phosphates) are high-energy bonds releasing ~30.5 kJ/mol when hydrolyzed. ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ + energy. This energy drives endergonic reactions, active transport, muscle contraction, and cell signaling. ATP is not a long-term storage molecule (cells turn over their entire ATP supply every minute during exercise); it is a momentary energy currency coupling catabolic (energy-releasing) to anabolic (energy-requiring) reactions.
