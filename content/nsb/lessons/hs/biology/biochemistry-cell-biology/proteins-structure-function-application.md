#### Amino Acid Structure and Peptide Bonds
All 20 standard amino acids share a central α-carbon bonded to: an amino group (-NH₂), a carboxyl group (-COOH), a hydrogen, and a variable R-group (side chain) that determines identity and chemical properties. Amino acids are classified by R-group: nonpolar/hydrophobic (alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, proline, phenylalanine, tryptophan, methionine), polar/uncharged (serine, threonine, cysteine, tyrosine, asparagine, glutamine), charged/basic (lysine, arginine, histidine), and charged/acidic (aspartate, glutamate). Peptide bonds form between the carboxyl of one amino acid and the amino of the next via dehydration synthesis, creating the peptide backbone (N-terminus → C-terminus).

#### Four Levels of Protein Structure
Primary structure: the linear amino acid sequence, determined by DNA. All higher structure derives from this sequence. Secondary structure: local regular folding patterns stabilized by hydrogen bonds between backbone atoms (N-H and C=O). α-helices are coiled; β-pleated sheets are extended strands connected by hydrogen bonds laterally. Tertiary structure: the overall 3D shape of a polypeptide, stabilized by interactions among R-groups: hydrophobic interactions (nonpolar R-groups cluster in the interior away from water), disulfide bonds (covalent bonds between cysteines), ionic bonds (between oppositely charged R-groups), and hydrogen bonds. Quaternary structure: assembly of two or more polypeptide subunits (e.g., hemoglobin: 2α + 2β subunits).

#### Structure Determines Function
Hemoglobin demonstrates structure-function relationships beautifully. Its quaternary structure allows cooperative O₂ binding: binding of one O₂ causes conformational changes that increase affinity for subsequent O₂ molecules (sigmoidal binding curve). Sickle cell disease results from a single amino acid substitution (glutamic acid → valine at position 6 of β-globin) — this one change creates a hydrophobic patch that causes deoxygenated hemoglobin to polymerize into rigid fibers, deforming red blood cells. One amino acid = one disease = millions affected. This illustrates how critical primary structure is.

#### Protein Denaturation
Denaturation is the disruption of secondary, tertiary, and/or quaternary structure without breaking peptide bonds. Causes: heat (disrupts non-covalent interactions by increasing kinetic energy), extreme pH (alters ionization of R-groups, disrupts ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds), organic solvents (disrupt hydrophobic interactions), heavy metals (bind sulfhydryl groups, preventing or disrupting disulfide bonds). Denaturation is usually irreversible in vivo. The classic demonstration: a fried egg — heat denatures albumin, turning it from clear (soluble) to white (insoluble aggregates). Prions are misfolded proteins that catalyze the misfolding of normal proteins, causing neurodegenerative diseases (BSE, CJD) — denaturation with catastrophic consequences.

#### Review Questions
1. A mutation changes a hydrophobic leucine to a charged lysine in the interior of a globular protein. Predict what happens to protein stability and function.
2. Explain why an α-helix is a type of secondary structure and what type of chemical interaction stabilizes it.
3. Compare denaturation by heat vs. extreme pH at the molecular level. Which types of bonds/interactions does each disrupt?
