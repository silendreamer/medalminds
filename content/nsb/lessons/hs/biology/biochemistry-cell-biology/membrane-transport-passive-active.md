#### Selective Permeability
The plasma membrane is selectively permeable — it allows some substances to cross freely while restricting others. Small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, N₂, fatty acids) diffuse freely across the lipid bilayer. Small polar molecules (H₂O, urea) cross slowly. Large polar molecules (glucose, amino acids) and ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺) cannot cross the lipid bilayer without assistance. This selectivity is essential for maintaining concentration gradients that drive cellular processes.

#### Passive Transport
Passive transport requires no energy (no ATP) — it moves substances down their electrochemical gradient (from high to low concentration for neutral solutes; for ions, the gradient combines concentration and electrical potential). Simple diffusion: nonpolar molecules directly diffuse through the bilayer. Facilitated diffusion uses membrane proteins: channel proteins (ion channels) form pores — gated (open in response to voltage, ligand, or mechanical stimulus) or ungated (always open, like aquaporins for H₂O). Carrier proteins (transporters) bind specific solutes, undergo conformational change, and release them on the other side — slower than channels. Aquaporins are water channels that increase water permeability ~100×; they explain why some cells are highly water-permeable (red blood cells, renal tubules) while others are not.

#### Active Transport
Active transport moves substances against their electrochemical gradient, requiring energy (usually ATP hydrolysis). Primary active transport directly couples ATP hydrolysis to transport. The Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase (sodium-potassium pump) exports 3 Na⁺ out and imports 2 K⁺ in per ATP hydrolyzed, maintaining low intracellular Na⁺ and high intracellular K⁺. This gradient drives the resting membrane potential (~−70 mV) and is used for secondary active transport. The Ca²⁺ ATPase pumps Ca²⁺ into the ER lumen or out of the cell, maintaining very low cytoplasmic [Ca²⁺] (~100 nM at rest vs. ~1 mM outside). Secondary active transport couples the movement of one ion (usually Na⁺) down its gradient to drive another solute against its gradient (cotransport/symport: both in same direction; antiport: opposite directions).

#### Bulk Transport
Endocytosis and exocytosis move large particles or volumes in membrane-bounded vesicles. Phagocytosis ("cell eating"): pseudopods engulf large particles (bacteria, dead cells) → phagosome → fuses with lysosome for digestion. Pinocytosis ("cell drinking"): invagination of the membrane creates small vesicles of extracellular fluid. Receptor-mediated endocytosis: clathrin-coated pits bind specific extracellular ligands via surface receptors → internalize the receptor-ligand complex → early endosome → late endosome → lysosomal degradation or receptor recycling. LDL (cholesterol) is taken up by receptor-mediated endocytosis; defects cause familial hypercholesterolemia.

2. Explain why the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase is described as an electrogenic pump and state its stoichiometry.
3. Compare pinocytosis and receptor-mediated endocytosis in terms of specificity and mechanism.
