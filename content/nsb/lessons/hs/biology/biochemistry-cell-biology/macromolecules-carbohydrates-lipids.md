#### Carbohydrates: Structure
Carbohydrates follow the formula (CH₂O)n. Monosaccharides are the monomers — glucose, fructose, galactose (hexoses, 6C) and ribose, deoxyribose (pentoses, 5C). Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) exists predominantly in ring form in aqueous solution. Disaccharides form by glycosidic bonds between two monosaccharides: sucrose (glucose + fructose), maltose (glucose + glucose), lactose (glucose + galactose). Polysaccharides are long chains: starch (α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic bonds, energy storage in plants), glycogen (more highly branched, energy storage in animals/liver/muscle), cellulose (β-1,4 glycosidic bonds, structural in plant cell walls — humans cannot digest due to lack of cellulase).

#### Carbohydrates: Function
Carbohydrates serve as: (1) primary energy source — glucose is the preferred fuel for cellular respiration; (2) structural materials — cellulose in plant walls, chitin (N-acetylglucosamine polymer) in fungal walls and insect exoskeletons; (3) cell recognition — glycoproteins and glycolipids on cell surfaces serve as identification tags (e.g., ABO blood type antigens are carbohydrate chains). The differences between starch and cellulose demonstrate how monomer orientation (α vs. β glucose) creates completely different macroscopic properties from the same monomer.

#### Lipids: Structure and Diversity
Lipids are defined not by polymer structure (they are not polymers) but by their hydrophobicity — insolubility in water. Major classes: triglycerides (one glycerol + three fatty acid chains joined by ester bonds), phospholipids (glycerol + two fatty acids + phosphate group + polar head), steroids (four fused carbon rings with functional groups), waxes (long-chain fatty acid + long-chain alcohol). Fatty acids can be saturated (no double bonds, straight, tightly packed, solid at room temperature — e.g., lard, butter) or unsaturated (one or more C=C double bonds, kinked, loosely packed, liquid at room temperature — e.g., olive oil). Polyunsaturated fats have multiple double bonds.

#### Phospholipids and the Lipid Bilayer
Phospholipids are amphipathic: the phosphate-containing head is polar and hydrophilic; the two fatty acid tails are nonpolar and hydrophobic. In aqueous solution, phospholipids spontaneously form bilayers: heads face out (toward water), tails face in (away from water). This is thermodynamically driven by the hydrophobic effect — the ordered hydrogen bond network around exposed nonpolar tails is released when tails cluster together, increasing entropy. The bilayer is self-healing and fluid at physiological temperature. Cholesterol inserts between phospholipids, reducing fluidity at high temperatures and preventing solidification at low temperatures — a "fluidity buffer."

#### Steroids and Waxes
Steroids share a four-ring carbon skeleton. Cholesterol is the precursor to all steroid hormones (testosterone, estrogen, cortisol, aldosterone) and bile salts. Despite its cardiovascular disease associations, cholesterol is essential for membrane fluidity and hormone synthesis. Waxes are highly hydrophobic (very long hydrocarbon chains) and serve as waterproofing: leaf cuticle (cutin), insect exoskeleton, earwax (cerumen), whale oil (spermaceti). Wax melts at higher temperatures than most lipids due to its more regular structure.
