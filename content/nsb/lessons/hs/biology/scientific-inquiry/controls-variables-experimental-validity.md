#### Why Controls Matter
The control group is the backbone of experimental design. It establishes a baseline against which the experimental group is compared. Without a control, any observed effect could be attributed to dozens of unknown factors. In a drug trial, if patients given a treatment improve, is it the drug or the act of receiving care? A placebo-controlled study answers this by giving one group an inert substance while keeping all other variables identical. Only the presence or absence of the active compound differs.

#### Types of Variables
The independent variable (IV) is deliberately manipulated by the researcher. The dependent variable (DV) is measured and expected to respond to the IV. Controlled variables are kept constant. Confounding variables are uncontrolled factors that could affect the DV, potentially producing false conclusions. If you test whether fertilizer improves plant growth but use different amounts of water in each group, watering is a confounding variable — you cannot tell whether growth differences came from fertilizer or water.

#### Internal and External Validity
Internal validity asks: does the experiment actually test what it claims to test? Threats include selection bias (groups differ before treatment begins), experimenter bias (researcher unconsciously influences results), and the placebo effect (belief in treatment causes improvement). External validity asks: do results generalize beyond the lab? A study using only college students may not apply to elderly populations. Double-blind designs — where neither participants nor researchers know group assignments — control for both experimenter and participant bias simultaneously.

#### Correlation vs. Causation
A correlation is a statistical relationship between two variables. Causation means one variable directly produces changes in another. Correlation does not imply causation: ice cream sales and drowning rates both rise in summer, but ice cream does not cause drowning (both are driven by hot weather). In biology, confusing correlation with causation leads to incorrect medical and ecological conclusions. Controlled experiments are the gold standard for establishing causation; observational studies can only identify correlations.

#### Peer Review and Reproducibility
Before scientific findings are published, they undergo peer review: independent experts scrutinize methodology, data, and conclusions for errors and bias. Even published results must be reproduced by independent labs to be considered reliable. The "replication crisis" — where many psychology and biology studies failed to reproduce — highlighted that sample sizes were often too small and publication bias (favoring positive results) distorted the literature. Modern biology increasingly requires pre-registration of study designs and open data sharing.
