### Introduction to Genetic Mutations  
Genetic mutations are alterations in the DNA sequence that can lead to changes in phenotype. These mutations can occur in various forms, including point mutations, insertions, deletions, and duplications. Understanding how these mutations are inherited is crucial for predicting their occurrence in future generations and for understanding genetic diseases.

### Types of Genetic Mutations  
Mutations can be classified into several categories based on their effects on the organism. Autosomal dominant mutations require only one copy of the mutated gene to express the trait, while autosomal recessive mutations require two copies for the trait to manifest. X-linked mutations are located on the X chromosome and can exhibit unique inheritance patterns, particularly affecting males more severely than females due to the presence of only one X chromosome in males.

### Autosomal Dominant Inheritance  
In autosomal dominant inheritance, an affected individual has a 50% chance of passing the mutated gene to each offspring. This pattern often results in the trait appearing in every generation, as the mutation can be transmitted from an affected parent to both sons and daughters. Examples of autosomal dominant disorders include Huntington's disease and Marfan syndrome, which illustrate how a single mutated allele can lead to significant health implications.

### Autosomal Recessive Inheritance  
Autosomal recessive inheritance requires both parents to carry one copy of the mutated gene for their child to express the trait. If both parents are carriers, there is a 25% chance with each pregnancy that the child will inherit the disorder. Conditions such as cystic fibrosis and sickle cell anemia exemplify autosomal recessive traits, often skipping generations if carriers do not have affected offspring.

### X-Linked Inheritance  
X-linked inheritance involves genes located on the X chromosome and can lead to distinct patterns of inheritance. Males, having one X and one Y chromosome, are more likely to express X-linked recessive traits, as they do not have a second X chromosome to mask the effect of a mutated allele. Examples include hemophilia and Duchenne muscular dystrophy, which predominantly affect males and can be passed from carrier mothers to their sons.

### Pedigree Analysis  
Pedigree analysis is a valuable tool for tracking the inheritance of genetic traits through generations. By constructing a family tree, geneticists can visualize how traits are passed down and identify patterns of inheritance. Symbols in pedigrees represent individuals and their phenotypes, allowing for the identification of carriers and affected individuals, which is crucial for genetic counseling and understanding the risk of passing on genetic disorders.

2. Name one genetic disorder that follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern.
