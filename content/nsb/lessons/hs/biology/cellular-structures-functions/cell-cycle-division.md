### Overview of the Cell Cycle

The cell cycle is a series of phases that a cell goes through to grow and divide. It consists of interphase and the mitotic phase. Interphase is further divided into three stages: G1 (gap 1), S (synthesis), and G2 (gap 2). During interphase, the cell grows, duplicates its DNA, and prepares for division, while the mitotic phase includes mitosis and cytokinesis, where the cell divides into two daughter cells.

### Phases of the Cell Cycle

Interphase is the longest phase of the cell cycle, accounting for about 90% of the total cycle time. In G1, the cell grows and synthesizes proteins necessary for DNA replication. The S phase is critical as the cell's DNA is replicated, ensuring that each daughter cell will receive an identical set of chromosomes. G2 is the final preparation phase, where the cell continues to grow and produces proteins required for mitosis.

### Mitosis: Process and Stages

Mitosis is the process by which a single cell divides to produce two genetically identical daughter cells. It is divided into four main stages: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase. During prophase, chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes, and the nuclear envelope begins to break down. In metaphase, chromosomes align at the cell's equatorial plane, while in anaphase, sister chromatids are pulled apart to opposite poles. Telophase involves the reformation of the nuclear envelope around each set of chromosomes, followed by cytokinesis, where the cytoplasm divides.

### Meiosis: A Unique Form of Cell Division

Meiosis is a specialized type of cell division that occurs in gametes (sperm and eggs) and results in four genetically diverse daughter cells, each with half the number of chromosomes of the parent cell. It consists of two rounds of division: meiosis I and meiosis II. In meiosis I, homologous chromosomes are separated, while meiosis II resembles mitosis, separating sister chromatids. This reduction in chromosome number is crucial for maintaining genetic stability across generations during sexual reproduction.

### Importance of Cellular Structures in Division

Cellular structures play vital roles in the processes of mitosis and meiosis. The centrosome, which organizes microtubules, is essential for the formation of the mitotic spindle that segregates chromosomes. The cell membrane and cytoskeleton are involved in the physical division of the cell during cytokinesis. Additionally, structures such as the nucleolus and endoplasmic reticulum contribute to the synthesis of proteins and RNA necessary for cell division.

### Regulation of the Cell Cycle

The cell cycle is tightly regulated by a series of checkpoints that ensure the integrity of the cell's DNA and the proper timing of division. Key proteins known as cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) control progression through the cycle. Checkpoints occur at G1, G2, and metaphase, where the cell assesses whether conditions are favorable for division. Failure in these regulatory mechanisms can lead to uncontrolled cell division, a hallmark of cancer.

### Review Questions

2. During which phase of mitosis do sister chromatids separate?
3. How does meiosis differ from mitosis in terms of the number of daughter cells produced?
