### Introduction to Action Potentials

Action potentials are rapid, temporary changes in the membrane potential of a neuron that allow for the transmission of electrical signals. These changes occur due to the movement of ions across the neuron's membrane, primarily sodium (Na⁺) and potassium (K⁺). When a neuron is stimulated, voltage-gated sodium channels open, allowing Na⁺ to flow into the cell, causing depolarization. If the depolarization reaches a certain threshold, an action potential is triggered, propagating along the axon to communicate with other neurons or muscles.

### Phases of Action Potential

The action potential consists of several distinct phases: depolarization, repolarization, and hyperpolarization. During depolarization, the influx of Na⁺ ions causes the membrane potential to become more positive. Following this, repolarization occurs as voltage-gated potassium channels open, allowing K⁺ to exit the cell, restoring the negative membrane potential. Hyperpolarization may occur as K⁺ channels remain open slightly longer than needed, making the neuron temporarily more negative than its resting state.

### The Role of Ion Channels

Ion channels are crucial for the generation and propagation of action potentials. Voltage-gated sodium channels are responsible for the rapid depolarization phase, while voltage-gated potassium channels facilitate repolarization. Additionally, leak channels for K⁺ maintain the resting membrane potential by allowing a small amount of potassium to flow out of the cell. The coordinated opening and closing of these channels create the characteristic "all-or-nothing" response of action potentials, ensuring that signals are transmitted efficiently along the axon.

### Synaptic Transmission Overview

Once an action potential reaches the axon terminal, it triggers synaptic transmission, the process by which signals are communicated between neurons. The arrival of the action potential causes voltage-gated calcium (Ca²⁺) channels to open, allowing Ca²⁺ ions to enter the presynaptic neuron. This influx of calcium ions prompts synaptic vesicles filled with neurotransmitters to fuse with the presynaptic membrane, releasing their contents into the synaptic cleft.

### Neurotransmitter Functions

Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that transmit signals across the synaptic cleft to the postsynaptic neuron. Common neurotransmitters include acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine, each playing distinct roles in neural communication. Upon release, neurotransmitters bind to specific receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, leading to either excitatory or inhibitory responses, depending on the type of neurotransmitter and receptor involved. This modulation of neuronal activity is essential for processes such as muscle contraction, mood regulation, and sensory perception.

### Synaptic Integration and Plasticity

The postsynaptic neuron integrates signals from multiple presynaptic neurons, a process known as synaptic integration. This integration can result in either excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs) or inhibitory postsynaptic potentials (IPSPs), influencing whether the postsynaptic neuron will fire an action potential. Synaptic plasticity, the ability of synapses to strengthen or weaken over time, is crucial for learning and memory. Long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD) are two mechanisms that illustrate how synaptic strength can change in response to activity levels.
