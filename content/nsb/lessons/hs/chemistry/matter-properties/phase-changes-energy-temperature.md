#### The Heating Curve
A heating curve plots temperature vs. heat added for a pure substance starting as a solid and ending as a gas. It has five distinct regions: (1) Solid heating: temperature rises as heat increases particle KE. (2) Melting (solid → liquid): temperature stays CONSTANT while heat overcomes IMFs to break the crystal lattice. (3) Liquid heating: temperature rises again. (4) Vaporization (liquid → gas): temperature stays CONSTANT while heat overcomes IMFs to free particles entirely. (5) Gas heating: temperature rises as KE increases.

#### Why Temperature Holds Constant During Phase Changes
During a phase change, the heat added goes into potential energy (breaking intermolecular bonds), not kinetic energy. Since temperature measures average kinetic energy, temperature doesn't rise. This is called **latent heat**. Latent heat of fusion (Hf) = heat to melt 1 mol or 1 g of solid. Latent heat of vaporization (Hv) = heat to vaporize 1 mol or 1 g of liquid. For water: Hf = 6.01 kJ/mol; Hv = 40.7 kJ/mol — vaporization requires ~6× more energy than melting because ALL intermolecular bonds must be broken.

#### Calculating Heat for Phase Changes
q = mHf or q = mHv (using specific latent heat per gram). For temperature changes within a single phase: q = mcΔT (where c = specific heat capacity). For water: c(ice) = 2.09 J/g·°C; c(liquid) = 4.18 J/g·°C; c(steam) = 2.01 J/g·°C. Note: the specific heat of liquid water is higher than ice or steam — water holds more heat.

#### Boiling Point vs. Melting Point
Normal boiling point: temperature at which vapor pressure equals 1 atm. Normal melting point: temperature at which solid and liquid coexist at 1 atm. Increasing pressure raises boiling point (pressure cookers); decreasing pressure lowers boiling point (water boils at lower temperature at high altitude). Pressure has minimal effect on melting point for most substances.
