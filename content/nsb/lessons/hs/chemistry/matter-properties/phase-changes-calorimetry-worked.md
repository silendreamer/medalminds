#### Strategy for Multi-Step Problems
When matter changes from one temperature to another and crosses a phase boundary, break the problem into segments: one segment per temperature-change region and one segment per phase change. Calculate q for each segment, then sum. Always check that you're in the right phase at each step.

#### Worked Example: Ice to Steam
How much heat (kJ) is needed to convert 25.0 g of ice at −20°C to steam at 125°C?
- Step 1 (ice warming): q = 25.0 g × 2.09 J/g·°C × 20°C = 1045 J = 1.045 kJ
- Step 2 (melting): q = 25.0 g × 334 J/g = 8350 J = 8.35 kJ
- Step 3 (water warming): q = 25.0 g × 4.18 J/g·°C × 100°C = 10,450 J = 10.45 kJ
- Step 4 (vaporization): q = 25.0 g × 2260 J/g = 56,500 J = 56.50 kJ
- Step 5 (steam warming): q = 25.0 g × 2.01 J/g·°C × 25°C = 1256 J = 1.26 kJ
- **Total = 77.6 kJ**

Note: vaporization (step 4) dominates — accounts for 73% of the total energy. This is why steam burns are more severe than boiling-water burns of equal temperature.

#### Cooling Calculation
How much heat is released when 100 g of steam at 150°C cools to ice at −5°C? Reverse the process: cooling is exothermic (negative q). Sum of all steps → approximately −310 kJ released.

#### Science Bowl Mental Math Shortcut
For quick comparisons: vaporization of water dominates all other terms. If a problem asks "which step requires the most energy," the answer is almost always vaporization, not melting or sensible heating.

#### Review Questions
1. How much heat (in kJ) is released when 50.0 g of steam at 100°C condenses to liquid water and cools to 25°C?
2. A 10.0 g piece of ice at 0°C is added to 100 g of water at 35°C in an insulated cup. Does all the ice melt? Show the calculation.
3. Why does water's anomalously high specific heat make it an excellent coolant in both engineering and biology?
