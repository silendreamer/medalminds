#### Electrolytic Cells

An **electrolytic cell** uses an external power source to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction. Unlike a galvanic cell (which generates electricity from a spontaneous reaction), an electrolytic cell consumes electricity to force the reaction in the non-spontaneous direction. The **anode** remains the site of oxidation and the **cathode** the site of reduction, but both are connected to an external voltage supply. Common examples include the electrolysis of water, brine (molten NaCl), and aqueous copper sulfate solutions.

#### Faraday's First and Second Laws

**Faraday's First Law** states that the mass of substance deposited or dissolved at an electrode is directly proportional to the total charge passed. **Faraday's Second Law** states that for the same charge, the masses of different substances deposited are proportional to their molar masses divided by the number of electrons required (their **equivalent weights**). These are unified in the quantitative formula:

moles of product = Q / (n · F) = (I · t) / (n · F)

where Q is total charge (coulombs), I is current (amperes), t is time (seconds), n is the number of electrons per formula unit, and F = 96,485 C/mol is **Faraday's constant**. Mass deposited = moles × molar mass.

#### Electroplating

**Electroplating** applies Faraday's laws to deposit a thin metal layer onto an object. The object to be plated is the **cathode**; the plating metal is typically the **anode**, which dissolves to replenish ions in solution. The thickness and mass of the deposited layer are controlled by the current and time. For example, to deposit 1.00 g of copper (M = 63.55 g/mol, n = 2) requires: moles Cu = 1.00/63.55 = 0.01573 mol; charge = 0.01573 × 2 × 96,485 ≈ 3,036 C.

#### Predicting Electrode Products

When multiple ions are present, the product at each electrode is determined by **discharge potential**. At the cathode, the species with the most positive (least negative) reduction potential is reduced preferentially. At the anode in aqueous solution, water may be oxidized rather than anions if the anion oxidation potential is very unfavorable (e.g., SO₄²⁻ is not oxidized; O₂ is evolved instead). This explains why electrolysis of dilute H₂SO₄ produces H₂ and O₂, not sulfur species.

#### Review Questions
1. A current of 2.50 A is passed through a CuSO₄ solution for 30.0 minutes. How many grams of copper (M = 63.55 g/mol, n = 2) are deposited at the cathode?
2. During the electrolysis of molten NaCl, sodium metal forms at the cathode and chlorine gas at the anode. If 0.500 mol of Cl₂ is produced, how many moles of electrons were transferred?
3. Explain why electroplating a piece of jewelry with gold requires the jewelry to act as the cathode rather than the anode.
