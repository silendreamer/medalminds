### Introduction to Chemical Reactions

Chemical reactions are processes in which substances, known as reactants, undergo a transformation to form new substances called products. These transformations involve the breaking and forming of chemical bonds and can be represented by chemical equations. Understanding how to balance these equations is crucial, as it reflects the law of conservation of mass, which states that matter cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.

### The Law of Conservation of Mass

The law of conservation of mass asserts that the total mass of reactants in a chemical reaction must equal the total mass of products. This principle implies that the number of atoms of each element must remain constant throughout the reaction. Consequently, when writing chemical equations, it is essential to ensure that the same number of each type of atom appears on both sides of the equation.

### Components of a Chemical Equation

A chemical equation consists of reactants on the left side and products on the right side, separated by an arrow that indicates the direction of the reaction. Coefficients are used to indicate the number of moles of each substance involved in the reaction. For example, in the equation 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the coefficient '2' in front of H₂ and H₂O indicates that two moles of hydrogen react with one mole of oxygen to produce two moles of water.

### Techniques for Balancing Chemical Equations

There are several techniques for balancing chemical equations, including the inspection method and the algebraic method. The inspection method involves adjusting coefficients systematically to achieve balance, starting with the most complex molecule. The algebraic method uses variables to represent coefficients and sets up a system of equations to solve for the unknowns, ensuring that the number of atoms for each element is equal on both sides.

### Common Challenges in Balancing Equations

Students often encounter challenges when balancing equations, particularly with polyatomic ions and compounds that contain multiple elements. It is helpful to treat polyatomic ions as single units when they appear unchanged on both sides of the equation. Additionally, balancing hydrogen and oxygen atoms last can simplify the process, as these elements are often found in multiple reactants and products.

### Practice Problems and Application

To reinforce the concepts learned, students should practice balancing a variety of chemical equations. Start with simple equations, such as H₂ + O₂ → H₂O, and gradually progress to more complex ones, such as C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Working through these problems will enhance students' understanding of stoichiometry and the relationships between reactants and products in chemical reactions.

### Review Questions

1. What does the law of conservation of mass state regarding chemical reactions?
2. In the equation 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl, how many atoms of sodium are present on each side of the equation?
3. When balancing the equation for the combustion of propane (C₃H₈), which elements should be balanced last, and why?
