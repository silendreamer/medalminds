#### Alkanes: Saturated Hydrocarbons

**Alkanes** are hydrocarbons that contain only single C–C and C–H bonds and follow the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Because every carbon is bonded to the maximum number of hydrogens, alkanes are called **saturated** hydrocarbons. Carbon atoms in alkanes are **sp³ hybridized** with tetrahedral geometry (~109.5° bond angles). Alkanes are relatively unreactive but undergo **combustion** and **halogenation** (via free-radical substitution). The first four members are methane, ethane, propane, and butane.

#### Alkenes and Alkynes: Unsaturated Hydrocarbons

**Alkenes** contain one or more C=C double bonds (general formula CₙH₂ₙ for one double bond) and are **unsaturated** because they contain fewer hydrogens than the corresponding alkane. Carbon atoms at a double bond are **sp² hybridized** with trigonal planar geometry (120°). **Alkynes** contain one or more C≡C triple bonds (general formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ for one triple bond) and are even more unsaturated; the involved carbons are **sp hybridized** with linear geometry (180°). The degree of **unsaturation** (index of hydrogen deficiency) is calculated as: DoU = (2C + 2 - H + N - X) / 2, where each ring or pi bond contributes one degree.

#### Structural and Geometric Isomers

Hydrocarbons with the same molecular formula can differ in connectivity (**structural isomers**) or spatial arrangement. Alkenes with internal double bonds can exhibit **cis-trans (geometric) isomerism** when each sp² carbon bears two different substituents. For example, cis-2-butene and trans-2-butene are distinct compounds with different physical properties. Alkynes and terminal alkenes lack this isomerism. **Branching** in alkanes lowers boiling point compared to unbranched isomers of the same molecular formula.

#### Reactions of Alkenes and Alkynes

Unlike alkanes, alkenes and alkynes readily undergo **addition reactions** because the pi bond is weaker than the sigma bond and acts as an electron donor. Key reactions include:
- **Hydrogenation** (H₂, catalyst): converts C=C to C–C (reduces unsaturation)
- **Hydrohalogenation** (HX): follows **Markovnikov's rule** — H adds to the less-substituted carbon
- **Halogenation** (X₂): adds across the double bond; bromine water decolorization is a classic test for unsaturation
- **Hydration** (H₂O, acid catalyst): produces an alcohol per Markovnikov's rule
