## Subtopic: States of Matter

### Kinetic Molecular Theory and the Three States
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** kinetic-molecular-theory-states
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** KMT · intermolecular forces · phase properties
**Summary:** Understand why matter exists in different states based on the competition between kinetic energy and intermolecular attraction.

#### The Kinetic Molecular Theory Framework
The Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT) explains the behavior of matter at the particle level. Five key postulates: (1) Matter is composed of tiny particles (atoms, molecules, or ions). (2) These particles are in constant, random motion. (3) Collisions between particles are perfectly elastic (no energy lost). (4) There are no attractive or repulsive forces between gas particles (ideal gas assumption). (5) The average kinetic energy of particles is directly proportional to absolute temperature (in Kelvin). This last postulate explains why heating a substance increases particle speed.

#### Comparing the Three States
**Solids**: particles close together, held in fixed positions by strong intermolecular forces, vibrate in place. Definite shape, definite volume, very low compressibility. **Liquids**: particles close together but able to move past each other. No definite shape (takes the shape of its container), definite volume. **Gases**: particles far apart, moving rapidly in all directions, intermolecular forces negligible. No definite shape, no definite volume (expands to fill container), highly compressible. The fourth state, **plasma**, is an ionized gas at extremely high temperatures; KMT breaks down.

#### The Role of Intermolecular Forces
The state of matter at a given temperature depends on the competition between kinetic energy (which drives particles apart) and intermolecular forces (which pull particles together). At low temperatures, IMFs dominate → solid. At intermediate temperatures, particles have enough energy to overcome fixed positions but not IMFs completely → liquid. At high temperatures, kinetic energy overwhelms IMFs → gas. This is why substances with strong IMFs have high melting and boiling points.

#### Density Relationships
In most substances, density: solid > liquid > gas. Water is the famous exception: ice is LESS dense than liquid water (density of ice ≈ 0.917 g/cm³; liquid water ≈ 1.000 g/cm³) because the hexagonal hydrogen-bond network in ice space the molecules farther apart than in liquid water. This anomaly makes ice float, allowing aquatic life to survive under frozen lakes.

#### Science Bowl Key Fact
At the same temperature and pressure, all ideal gas samples have the same number of particles per unit volume (Avogadro's law). The KMT predicts that at 0°C, gas particles move at hundreds to thousands of meters per second — much faster than solid particles vibrating in fixed positions.

#### Review Questions
1. Which state of matter has the highest density, and why? Name the one common exception and explain it.
2. According to KMT, what happens to the average kinetic energy of gas particles when temperature doubles (in Kelvin)?
3. Why are gases highly compressible while liquids are nearly incompressible?

### Phase Changes: Energy and Temperature
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** phase-changes-energy-temperature
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** heating curves · latent heat · phase change temperatures
**Summary:** Master the heating curve — one of the most heavily tested diagrams in AP Chemistry and Science Bowl.

#### The Heating Curve
A heating curve plots temperature vs. heat added for a pure substance starting as a solid and ending as a gas. It has five distinct regions: (1) Solid heating: temperature rises as heat increases particle KE. (2) Melting (solid → liquid): temperature stays CONSTANT while heat overcomes IMFs to break the crystal lattice. (3) Liquid heating: temperature rises again. (4) Vaporization (liquid → gas): temperature stays CONSTANT while heat overcomes IMFs to free particles entirely. (5) Gas heating: temperature rises as KE increases.

#### Why Temperature Holds Constant During Phase Changes
During a phase change, the heat added goes into potential energy (breaking intermolecular bonds), not kinetic energy. Since temperature measures average kinetic energy, temperature doesn't rise. This is called **latent heat**. Latent heat of fusion (Hf) = heat to melt 1 mol or 1 g of solid. Latent heat of vaporization (Hv) = heat to vaporize 1 mol or 1 g of liquid. For water: Hf = 6.01 kJ/mol; Hv = 40.7 kJ/mol — vaporization requires ~6× more energy than melting because ALL intermolecular bonds must be broken.

#### Calculating Heat for Phase Changes
q = mHf or q = mHv (using specific latent heat per gram). For temperature changes within a single phase: q = mcΔT (where c = specific heat capacity). For water: c(ice) = 2.09 J/g·°C; c(liquid) = 4.18 J/g·°C; c(steam) = 2.01 J/g·°C. Note: the specific heat of liquid water is higher than ice or steam — water holds more heat.

#### Boiling Point vs. Melting Point
Normal boiling point: temperature at which vapor pressure equals 1 atm. Normal melting point: temperature at which solid and liquid coexist at 1 atm. Increasing pressure raises boiling point (pressure cookers); decreasing pressure lowers boiling point (water boils at lower temperature at high altitude). Pressure has minimal effect on melting point for most substances.

#### Review Questions
1. How many kJ are needed to convert 50.0 g of ice at −10°C to steam at 110°C? (Use: c(ice) = 2.09 J/g·°C, Hf = 334 J/g, c(water) = 4.18 J/g·°C, Hv = 2260 J/g, c(steam) = 2.01 J/g·°C)
2. On a heating curve, why are the plateau regions horizontal?
3. Why is the latent heat of vaporization of water much greater than its latent heat of fusion?

### Vapor Pressure and Phase Diagrams
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** vapor-pressure-phase-diagrams
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** vapor pressure · phase diagram · triple point · critical point
**Summary:** Understand how pressure and temperature together determine which phase a substance adopts — the phase diagram.

#### Vapor Pressure
Vapor pressure is the partial pressure of a substance's vapor in equilibrium with its liquid at a given temperature. More precisely: in a closed container, liquid molecules constantly evaporate while vapor molecules condense. Equilibrium is reached when evaporation rate = condensation rate. The pressure of vapor at this equilibrium = vapor pressure. Key relationships: (1) VP increases with temperature (more particles have enough KE to escape). (2) VP depends on IMF strength — weaker IMFs → higher VP (more volatile). (3) Boiling occurs when VP = atmospheric pressure.

#### The Phase Diagram
A phase diagram shows which state of matter is stable at any combination of temperature and pressure. Key features: **Triple point**: the one unique temperature and pressure where all three phases coexist. For water: 273.16 K, 611.7 Pa. **Critical point**: the temperature and pressure above which liquid and gas become indistinguishable (supercritical fluid). For water: 374°C, 218 atm. **Solid-liquid boundary**: for most substances, slopes positively (higher pressure favors solid). For water, slopes negatively (higher pressure favors liquid — explains pressure melting of ice).

#### Water's Unique Phase Diagram
Water's solid-liquid boundary has a negative slope (unusual). This means applying pressure at 0°C converts ice to water — explaining ice skating (though the primary mechanism is actually frictional heat). Carbon dioxide's phase diagram is more typical, with a positive solid-liquid slope. At 1 atm pressure, CO₂ has no stable liquid phase — dry ice sublimes directly to gas (sublimation point: −78.5°C).

#### Supercritical Fluids
Above the critical point, a substance is a supercritical fluid — it has the density of a liquid but diffuses like a gas. Supercritical CO₂ is used industrially as a solvent for coffee decaffeination and pharmaceutical extraction. It's environmentally friendly (no organic solvent residue) and the properties can be tuned by adjusting P and T.

#### Review Questions
1. On a water phase diagram, mark the triple point, critical point, and normal boiling point. Explain what happens when you increase pressure at 200°C.
2. Why does CO₂ sublimate at 1 atm rather than melting?
3. A liquid has a higher vapor pressure than water at 25°C. What can you conclude about its intermolecular forces and boiling point compared to water?

### Phase Changes: Worked Calorimetry Problems
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** phase-changes-calorimetry-worked
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** multi-step heat calculations · heating curve math · phase change energy
**Summary:** Work through complete multi-step heating/cooling problems that combine phase change and sensible heat calculations.

#### Strategy for Multi-Step Problems
When matter changes from one temperature to another and crosses a phase boundary, break the problem into segments: one segment per temperature-change region and one segment per phase change. Calculate q for each segment, then sum. Always check that you're in the right phase at each step.

#### Worked Example: Ice to Steam
How much heat (kJ) is needed to convert 25.0 g of ice at −20°C to steam at 125°C?
- Step 1 (ice warming): q = 25.0 g × 2.09 J/g·°C × 20°C = 1045 J = 1.045 kJ
- Step 2 (melting): q = 25.0 g × 334 J/g = 8350 J = 8.35 kJ
- Step 3 (water warming): q = 25.0 g × 4.18 J/g·°C × 100°C = 10,450 J = 10.45 kJ
- Step 4 (vaporization): q = 25.0 g × 2260 J/g = 56,500 J = 56.50 kJ
- Step 5 (steam warming): q = 25.0 g × 2.01 J/g·°C × 25°C = 1256 J = 1.26 kJ
- **Total = 77.6 kJ**

Note: vaporization (step 4) dominates — accounts for 73% of the total energy. This is why steam burns are more severe than boiling-water burns of equal temperature.

#### Cooling Calculation
How much heat is released when 100 g of steam at 150°C cools to ice at −5°C? Reverse the process: cooling is exothermic (negative q). Sum of all steps → approximately −310 kJ released.

#### Science Bowl Mental Math Shortcut
For quick comparisons: vaporization of water dominates all other terms. If a problem asks "which step requires the most energy," the answer is almost always vaporization, not melting or sensible heating.

#### Review Questions
1. How much heat (in kJ) is released when 50.0 g of steam at 100°C condenses to liquid water and cools to 25°C?
2. A 10.0 g piece of ice at 0°C is added to 100 g of water at 35°C in an insulated cup. Does all the ice melt? Show the calculation.
3. Why does water's anomalously high specific heat make it an excellent coolant in both engineering and biology?

## Subtopic: Physical & Chemical Properties

### Identifying Physical and Chemical Properties
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** identifying-physical-chemical-properties
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** physical vs. chemical properties · intensive/extensive · property identification
**Summary:** Learn to classify every observable characteristic as physical or chemical, and understand why the distinction matters.

#### Physical Properties
A physical property can be measured or observed without changing the substance's chemical composition. Examples: color, odor, density, melting point, boiling point, hardness, conductivity, malleability, ductility, solubility, viscosity, mass, volume. These properties describe how the substance looks or behaves as itself — not how it reacts. You can measure water's boiling point (100°C) without converting it to something else.

#### Chemical Properties
A chemical property describes how a substance reacts to form different substances. Examples: flammability (does it burn?), reactivity with acids, oxidation tendency (does iron rust?), toxicity, acidity, ability to corrode. To observe a chemical property, you must carry out a chemical change. You can't determine flammability without actually burning — and after burning, you no longer have the same substance.

#### Intensive vs. Extensive Properties
**Intensive** properties don't depend on the amount of substance: density, color, melting point, boiling point, specific heat, conductivity. These are used to identify substances — density = 0.789 g/mL identifies ethanol regardless of how much you have. **Extensive** properties depend on amount: mass, volume, heat content, length. Doubling the amount of water doubles its mass and volume but not its boiling point or density. Ratio of two extensive properties = intensive property (mass/volume = density).

#### Why This Classification Matters
Chemical analysis uses physical properties to identify substances without destroying them (non-destructive testing). Chemical properties require testing reactivity — necessarily consuming or altering the sample. Forensic scientists, geologists, and quality-control chemists exploit physical properties first. Only when physical properties are insufficient do they perform chemical tests.

#### Science Bowl Classification Drill
Classify each as physical (P) or chemical (C): melting point (P), flammability (C), density (P), corrosion (C), color (P), solubility (P), reactivity with water (C), ductility (P), ability to tarnish (C).

#### Review Questions
1. Classify each as physical or chemical property: (a) gold's yellow color, (b) sulfur's odor, (c) iron's tendency to rust, (d) water's boiling point at 100°C, (e) gasoline's flammability.
2. Which is an intensive property: density, mass, volume, or heat energy? Explain why.
3. Why can you determine a substance's melting point without performing a chemical reaction?

### Physical and Chemical Changes
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** physical-chemical-changes
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** change classification · evidence of chemical change · reversibility
**Summary:** Distinguish physical changes from chemical changes using observable evidence and molecular-level reasoning.

#### Physical Changes
A physical change alters the form or phase of a substance but NOT its chemical composition. The substance can be recovered unchanged: melting ice → liquid water (same molecules, H₂O, just differently arranged); crushing a rock (smaller pieces, same mineral composition); dissolving salt in water (recoverable by evaporation). Physical changes are generally reversible, though "reversible" is not the defining criterion (some physical changes are hard to reverse, like cutting a wire).

#### Chemical Changes
A chemical change produces one or more new substances with different compositions. Evidence of chemical change: (1) color change (copper → green patina), (2) gas production (bubbling in a reaction), (3) precipitate formation (solid forms from clear solutions), (4) temperature change (exothermic/endothermic reactions), (5) light emission (combustion, bioluminescence). Note: these are EVIDENCE of a chemical change, not proof — some physical changes also show these signs (dissolving in water can be exothermic).

#### Tricky Cases
**Dissolving** is physical — the substance can be recovered. **Combustion** is chemical — the substance is consumed. **Melting** is physical. **Burning** is chemical. **Rusting** is chemical. **Tearing paper** is physical. **Digesting food** is chemical. **Boiling water** is physical. **Electrolysis of water** is chemical (2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂).

#### Molecular-Level Interpretation
At the molecular level: physical changes rearrange or separate molecules without breaking bonds (phase changes, dissolving). Chemical changes break and form bonds, creating new substances. The bonds in H₂O are NOT broken when ice melts — only the hydrogen bonds between water molecules are disrupted. The covalent O–H bonds within each molecule remain intact.

#### Review Questions
1. Classify each as physical or chemical change: (a) melting butter, (b) cooking an egg, (c) burning wood, (d) dissolving sugar, (e) electrolysis of water.
2. A student adds vinegar to baking soda and observes bubbling. Is this physical or chemical change? Identify two pieces of evidence.
3. Why is dissolving NaCl in water considered a physical change, even though the ions separate?

### Pure Substances, Mixtures, and Separation Techniques
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** pure-substances-mixtures-separation
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** pure substance · mixture · separation methods
**Summary:** Master the classification of matter and the separation techniques that exploit physical differences between components.

#### Classification Hierarchy
All matter divides into: **Pure substances** (fixed, uniform composition throughout) and **Mixtures** (two or more substances physically combined). Pure substances further divide into: **Elements** (cannot be broken down by chemical means; one type of atom; e.g., gold, oxygen) and **Compounds** (two or more elements chemically combined in fixed ratios; can be broken down by chemical means; e.g., water, table salt). Mixtures divide into: **Homogeneous** (uniform composition throughout; also called solutions; e.g., saltwater, air, brass) and **Heterogeneous** (non-uniform composition; visibly different phases; e.g., salad, sand and water, concrete).

#### Separation Techniques and the Property Each Exploits
| Technique | Property Exploited | Example |
|-----------|---------------------|---------|
| Filtration | Particle size | Remove sand from water |
| Distillation | Boiling point | Separate ethanol from water |
| Evaporation | Volatility | Recover salt from saltwater |
| Chromatography | Polarity/solubility | Separate ink dyes |
| Magnetic separation | Magnetic susceptibility | Separate iron from sulfur |
| Centrifugation | Density | Separate blood components |
| Crystallization | Solubility vs. temperature | Purify salts |

#### Chromatography in Detail
In paper chromatography, components travel different distances based on their affinity for the stationary phase (paper) vs. mobile phase (solvent). The Rf (retention factor) = distance spot traveled / distance solvent front traveled. More polar components travel less far on polar stationary phase (they "stick" more). Rf values are characteristic of each compound under specific conditions — used for identification.

#### Why Mixtures Can Be Separated by Physical Means
Because mixtures are held together by physical forces (not chemical bonds), you can exploit differences in physical properties to separate components without breaking any intramolecular bonds. Separation of compounds requires chemical reactions (you can't separate water into hydrogen and oxygen by filtration or distillation).

#### Review Questions
1. Classify each as element, compound, homogeneous mixture, or heterogeneous mixture: (a) oxygen gas (O₂), (b) salt water, (c) table salt (NaCl), (d) granite.
2. You have a mixture of sand, salt, and iron filings. Describe a sequence of separation techniques to isolate each pure component.
3. What property does chromatography exploit? Why do different components travel different distances?

### Classification and Separation Applied
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** classification-separation-applied
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** separation design · chromatography · distillation
**Summary:** Apply matter classification and separation techniques to realistic laboratory scenarios.

#### Scenario 1: Wine Production — Fractional Distillation
Fermented grape juice contains water (bp 100°C), ethanol (bp 78.4°C), and hundreds of other compounds. To concentrate ethanol, winemakers use distillation (commercial spirits use fractional distillation). A fractionating column allows vapors to repeatedly condense and re-evaporate, enriching the ethanol fraction. The vapor exiting the column is richer in ethanol than the original mixture because ethanol has the lower boiling point.

Pure ethanol cannot be obtained by simple distillation from water due to azeotrope formation at 95.6% ethanol (the azeotrope boils at 78.1°C, lower than pure ethanol, and co-distills). Obtaining 100% ethanol requires molecular sieves or chemical dehydration.

#### Scenario 2: Drug Identification via Chromatography
A forensic chemist suspects a white powder contains three substances. She dissolves it and runs thin-layer chromatography (TLC). Four spots appear; three match known standards. The Rf values: aspirin = 0.45, caffeine = 0.25, unknown compound = 0.67. The unknown's Rf doesn't match the standards in this solvent system. She switches to a different solvent (changes the mobile phase polarity) and reruns. This changes all Rf values, but their relative relationships can still reveal the compound's identity by comparison to a reference database.

#### Scenario 3: Separating a Ternary Mixture
Given: a mixture of iron filings, sand (SiO₂), and salt (NaCl). Optimal separation sequence: (1) Magnetic separation — magnet removes iron (exploits magnetic property). (2) Add water — salt dissolves, sand doesn't (exploits differential solubility). (3) Filter — separates sand from saltwater (exploits particle size). (4) Evaporate water from filtrate — recovers salt. Four pure components from four physical techniques; no chemical reactions needed.

#### Review Questions
1. Why can't you separate a compound like water (H₂O) into hydrogen and oxygen using physical separation techniques?
2. Design a separation procedure for a mixture of large silica beads, sugar, and cooking oil.
3. Two compounds have Rf values of 0.30 and 0.85 in ethyl acetate on a silica TLC plate. Which is more polar? Which has more affinity for the silica stationary phase?

## Subtopic: Physical & Chemical Changes

### Evidence and Mechanism of Chemical Change
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** evidence-mechanism-chemical-change
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** chemical change indicators · bond breaking/forming · conservation of mass
**Summary:** Deep-dive into what actually happens during a chemical change at the molecular level, and how to recognize chemical changes from macroscopic evidence.

#### What Defines a Chemical Change
A chemical change produces new substances with different chemical formulas. At the molecular level: chemical bonds between atoms are broken, and new bonds form, creating products with different arrangements of atoms. Example: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. In this combustion, C–H and O=O bonds break; C=O and O–H bonds form. The products CO₂ and H₂O have completely different properties than the reactants methane and oxygen.

#### Five Observable Signs of Chemical Change
(1) **Temperature change**: exothermic reactions release heat (combustion, neutralization, rusting); endothermic reactions absorb heat (photosynthesis, dissolving ammonium nitrate). (2) **Color change**: copper → blue in CuSO₄ solution; silver tarnish; litmus paper pH indicator. (3) **Gas production**: CO₂ from acid + carbonate (bubbles); H₂ from acid + metal. (4) **Precipitate formation**: mixing clear Pb(NO₃)₂ and KI solutions produces yellow PbI₂ precipitate. (5) **Light emission**: burning magnesium emits brilliant white light; chemiluminescence in glow sticks.

#### The Conservation of Mass
In any chemical change, mass is conserved (Lavoisier's Law). The total mass of reactants = total mass of products. This is because no atoms are created or destroyed — they are only rearranged. Example: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Reactants: 2(2) + 32 = 36 g. Products: 2(18) = 36 g. ✓ This law is what allows us to balance chemical equations.

#### When "Evidence" Isn't Enough
Some signs that look like chemical change are physical: dissolving sodium hydroxide in water releases heat (feels hot) — but it's a physical change. Dissolving ammonium nitrate absorbs heat (cold packs) — physical. These are physical changes because the solute can be recovered by evaporation. True chemical change produces substances that cannot be recovered by simple physical means.

#### Review Questions
1. Identify the chemical change(s): (a) salt dissolving in water, (b) milk souring, (c) ice melting, (d) iron rusting, (e) photosynthesis.
2. A student mixes two clear solutions and observes a yellow solid forming. What type of change is this? What is the solid called?
3. How does the law of conservation of mass apply to combustion? If 100 g of wood produces 60 g of ash, does mass disappear? Explain.

#### The Five Major Reaction Types
**Synthesis (combination)**: A + B → AB. Two or more reactants combine to form one product. Example: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Clue: multiple reactants → one product.

**Decomposition**: AB → A + B. One compound breaks into two or more simpler substances. Example: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. Clue: one reactant → multiple products. Often requires heat, light, or electricity.

**Single replacement (displacement)**: A + BC → AC + B. An element displaces another element from a compound. Example: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂. Clue: element + compound → new compound + different element. Driven by activity series.

**Double replacement (metathesis)**: AB + CD → AD + CB. Ions exchange partners. Example: Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂↓ + 2KNO₃. Clue: two ionic compounds → two new compounds; often produces precipitate or water.

**Combustion**: Fuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (complete) or CO + H₂O (incomplete). Example: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. Clue: hydrocarbon + oxygen.

#### Activity Series for Single Replacement
The activity series ranks metals from most reactive (Li at top) to least reactive (Au at bottom). A metal can only replace a metal BELOW it in the series. Na can replace Cu (Na is above Cu), but Cu CANNOT replace Na. Similarly, metals above H₂ in the series will react with dilute acids to produce H₂ gas.

#### Review Questions
1. Classify each reaction: (a) 2Al + 3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃; (b) CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂; (c) Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu; (d) BaCl₂ + Na₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + 2NaCl.
2. Will copper react with silver nitrate solution (AgNO₃)? Write the reaction if yes; explain if no. (Cu is above Ag in the activity series.)
3. Write the complete combustion reaction for butane (C₄H₁₀). Balance it.

#### Reaction Type Classification (Quick-Fire)
1. 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ → **Decomposition**
2. 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl → **Synthesis**
3. CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O → **Combustion**
4. 2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂ → **Single replacement**
5. AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃ → **Double replacement** (precipitation)
6. N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ → **Synthesis**

#### Conservation of Mass Problems
1. 4 g H₂ + 32 g O₂ → ? g H₂O → **36 g** (mass conserved)
2. CaCO₃ (100 g) → CaO (56 g) + CO₂ → CO₂ = **44 g**
3. 112 g Fe reacts with Cl₂ to form 325 g FeCl₃. How many grams of Cl₂ were consumed? → 325 − 112 = **213 g Cl₂**

#### Evidence Identification
Which piece of evidence suggests a chemical change (not just physical)?
(A) The temperature of the solution rises when NaOH dissolves in water → possibly physical
(B) A yellow precipitate forms when Pb²⁺ and I⁻ solutions are mixed → **chemical** (new substance)
(C) Gas bubbles form when vinegar meets baking soda → **chemical** (CO₂ produced)
(D) Iron turns reddish-brown over days in moist air → **chemical** (iron oxide formed)

### Science Bowl: Matter & Properties High-Yield Facts
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** science-bowl-matter-properties-facts
**Estimated time:** 7 min
**Key concepts:** classification facts · reaction types · bowl recognition patterns
**Summary:** The matter classification and chemical change facts that appear most often in Science Bowl Chemistry toss-ups.

#### Top Bowl Facts: Changes and Reactions
1. Physical change: composition unchanged; substance recoverable
2. Chemical change: new substances formed; bonds broken and formed
3. Law of conservation of mass: mass reactants = mass products
4. Synthesis: A + B → AB (two things make one)
5. Decomposition: AB → A + B (one thing makes multiple)
6. Single replacement: driven by the activity series (more reactive displaces less reactive)
7. Double replacement: ion exchange; often produces precipitate, gas, or water
8. Complete combustion: products are CO₂ + H₂O
9. Incomplete combustion: products include CO and/or soot (C)
10. Precipitation reaction: double replacement producing an insoluble product
11. Electrolysis: uses electricity to decompose compounds (H₂O → H₂ + O₂)
12. Photosynthesis and combustion are chemical changes

#### Common Bowl Traps
- Dissolving ≠ chemical change (even if temperature changes)
- Physical signs (bubbles, color change) can occur in physical changes — not 100% proof of chemical change
- Sublimation of dry ice = PHYSICAL change (CO₂ doesn't become a different substance)

#### Practice Toss-Up Stems
1. "For 10 points — what type of reaction takes one compound and produces multiple products?" → **decomposition**
2. "For 10 points — in a single replacement reaction, what determines whether the reaction will occur?" → **the activity series** (more active metal displaces less active)
3. "For 10 points — what law states that the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products in a chemical reaction?" → **Law of Conservation of Mass** (accept: Lavoisier's law)

#### Review Questions
1. Is photosynthesis (6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂) a physical or chemical change? What type of reaction is it?
2. Magnesium burns in nitrogen gas to form Mg₃N₂. Is this synthesis or single replacement?
3. A student mixes two clear ionic solutions and a white solid forms. What type of reaction is this? What is the white solid called?
