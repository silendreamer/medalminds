#### What the Scientific Method Actually Is
The scientific method is a structured process for answering questions about the natural world: observe, question, hypothesize, experiment, analyze, conclude. In chemistry, it means you never just "mix things and see what happens" — you design a controlled experiment with a clear hypothesis. A hypothesis is a testable, falsifiable prediction in the form "If [independent variable changes], then [dependent variable will change] because [reasoning]."

#### Variables: What You Change, Measure, and Hold Constant
Every experiment has three types of variables. The **independent variable** is what you deliberately change (e.g., temperature). The **dependent variable** is what you measure (e.g., reaction rate). **Controlled variables** are everything else you hold constant (same concentration, same container, same chemist). Changing multiple independent variables simultaneously makes it impossible to determine which one caused the observed effect.

#### Designing a Valid Experiment
A valid experiment must have a **control group** (no treatment, baseline comparison), adequate **sample size** (multiple trials reduce random error), and **reproducibility** (another chemist can repeat it). In chemistry, this means running each condition at least 3 times, using calibrated equipment, and documenting every procedural step precisely enough that a stranger could replicate it exactly.

#### Interpreting Results vs. Drawing Conclusions
Results are raw data; conclusions are interpretations. "The reaction rate doubled when temperature increased from 25°C to 35°C" is a result. "Temperature increases reaction rate because higher kinetic energy leads to more frequent effective collisions" is a conclusion connecting data to underlying theory. A good conclusion also acknowledges limitations: what sources of error existed, and how might they have affected results?

#### Science Bowl Connection
Science Bowl questions frequently test whether students can identify the independent vs. dependent variable in a described experiment, or recognize a flaw in experimental design (missing control group, insufficient trials, uncontrolled variable). Key phrase: "which variable is the scientist deliberately changing?" = independent variable.

#### Review Questions
1. A chemist tests whether salt concentration affects the boiling point of water, using identical pots, identical stove settings, and three trials at each concentration. Identify the independent, dependent, and two controlled variables.
2. Why must a chemistry experiment include multiple trials rather than a single measurement?
3. What is the difference between a result and a conclusion? Give an example from a chemistry context.
