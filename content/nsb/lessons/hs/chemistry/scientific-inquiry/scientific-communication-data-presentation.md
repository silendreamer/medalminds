#### Data Tables: Structure and Conventions
A proper data table has: a descriptive title, column headers with units in parentheses or brackets, independent variable in the first column, dependent variable(s) in subsequent columns, and values aligned consistently. Never omit units from headers — a column of numbers without units is meaningless. Example: "Temperature (°C)" not just "Temperature" or "Temp."

#### Choosing the Right Graph Type
**Line graph**: for continuous data (temperature over time, concentration vs. time in kinetics). **Bar graph**: for discrete categories (comparing yields from different catalysts). **Scatter plot**: for examining correlation between two variables. Line graphs are most common in chemistry. Always label both axes with the variable name AND units. Include a title. If multiple datasets appear, include a legend.

#### Scientific Notation in Data Reporting
Chemistry data often spans many orders of magnitude. 0.00000602 is awkward; 6.02 × 10⁻⁶ is clear. Rules: the coefficient must be between 1 and 10; the exponent shows how many places the decimal moved. Positive exponent = large number; negative exponent = small number. 6.02 × 10²³ ≠ 6.02²³ — the × 10ⁿ is the notation, not multiplication separately.

#### Writing a Lab Report
Standard sections: Title, Abstract (summary of purpose, method, results, conclusion — ~150 words), Introduction (background, purpose, hypothesis), Methods (detailed enough to replicate), Results (data tables, graphs, sample calculations), Discussion (analysis, error sources, connections to theory), Conclusion (restate hypothesis, state whether supported, summarize findings). References cite only peer-reviewed sources.
