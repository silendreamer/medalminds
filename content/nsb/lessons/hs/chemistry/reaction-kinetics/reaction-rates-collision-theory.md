#### Reaction Rate Defined

The **reaction rate** measures how quickly reactants are consumed or products are formed, expressed as the change in concentration per unit time: rate = -Δ[A]/Δt (for a reactant A) or rate = Δ[P]/Δt (for a product P). Rates are typically reported in mol/L·s (molarity per second). For a balanced equation, each species' rate is divided by its stoichiometric coefficient to yield a single unified rate.

#### Collision Theory Fundamentals

**Collision theory** states that chemical reactions occur when reactant particles collide with sufficient energy and proper orientation. Not every collision leads to a reaction; only **effective collisions** — those meeting both the energy and geometry requirements — produce products. The **activation energy (Ea)** is the minimum kinetic energy that colliding particles must possess for the collision to be effective.

#### Factors Affecting Reaction Rate

Several variables control how often effective collisions occur:

- **Concentration**: More dissolved particles increase collision frequency, raising the rate.
- **Temperature**: Higher temperature increases average kinetic energy, so a larger fraction of collisions exceed Ea and orientation constraints.
- **Surface area**: For heterogeneous reactions, finer particle size exposes more reactive surface, increasing collision frequency.
- **Catalysts**: Lower Ea without being consumed, dramatically increasing the fraction of effective collisions (see the Arrhenius lesson).
- **Nature of reactants**: Ionic species in solution react nearly instantaneously; covalent bond-breaking steps are inherently slower.

#### Orientation and Steric Factor

Even energetically sufficient collisions may fail if molecules approach with the wrong geometry. The **steric factor (p)**, a value between 0 and 1, captures the fraction of collisions that have the correct spatial orientation. The overall rate of effective collisions = p × Z × f, where Z is the total collision frequency and f is the Boltzmann fraction of molecules exceeding Ea.

#### Review Questions

3. If two molecules collide with energy greater than Ea but in the wrong orientation, what is the outcome, and what term describes this geometric requirement?
