#### Strong Acids and Bases
**Strong acids** ionize completely in water — there is essentially no reverse reaction. The six common strong acids are HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, H2SO4 (first proton), and HClO4. For a 0.10 M HCl solution, [H3O+] = 0.10 M directly. **Strong bases** also dissociate fully; Group 1 hydroxides (NaOH, KOH) and heavy Group 2 hydroxides (Ca(OH)2, Ba(OH)2) are the principal examples. Because ionization is complete, Ka and Kb expressions are not meaningful for strong acids/bases — equilibrium lies entirely on the product side.

#### Weak Acids and the Ka Expression
**Weak acids** only partially ionize. Their equilibrium is described by the **acid dissociation constant**: Ka = [H3O+][A-] / [HA]. A smaller Ka means weaker acid. For acetic acid (Ka = 1.8 × 10-5), a 0.10 M solution produces [H3O+] = sqrt(Ka × C) ≈ sqrt(1.8 × 10-6) ≈ 1.34 × 10-3 M, giving pH ≈ 2.87. The **percent ionization** = ([H3O+]eq / [HA]initial) × 100%, which increases as the solution is diluted (a consequence of Le Chatelier's principle).

#### Weak Bases and the Kb Expression
**Weak bases** partially accept protons from water. The **base dissociation constant** is Kb = [BH+][OH-] / [B]. Ammonia (Kb = 1.8 × 10-5) is the canonical example. For a weak base, [OH-] = sqrt(Kb × C), then pH = 14 - pOH. The relationship **Ka × Kb = Kw = 1.0 × 10-14** links a weak acid to its conjugate base: knowing Ka for acetic acid immediately gives Kb for acetate ion.

#### Conjugate Strength Relationship
A fundamental principle: **the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base**, and vice versa. Strong acids (large Ka) have essentially inert conjugate bases (Cl-, NO3-). Weak acids (small Ka) have relatively strong conjugate bases that noticeably hydrolyze water. This explains why a solution of sodium acetate (CH3COO- Na+) is basic — the acetate ion is a moderately strong conjugate base that accepts protons from water to raise pH.

#### Comparing Acid Strength
Acid strength trends: bond polarity and bond strength both matter. Across a period, electronegativity increases O-H bond polarity, making acids stronger (HF < HCl). Down a group, bond strength decreases and dominates, making acids stronger (HF < HCl < HBr < HI). For oxyacids (HxEOy), more oxygen atoms around the central element E increase acid strength by withdrawing electron density, stabilizing the conjugate base anion.

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate the pH and percent ionization of a 0.050 M solution of hypochlorous acid (HClO, Ka = 3.0 × 10-8).
2. Using Ka × Kb = Kw, determine Kb for the fluoride ion (F-) given that the Ka of HF is 7.2 × 10-4, and explain whether a NaF solution is acidic, basic, or neutral.
3. Rank the following in order of increasing acid strength and justify your ranking: HClO4, HClO3, HClO2, HClO.
