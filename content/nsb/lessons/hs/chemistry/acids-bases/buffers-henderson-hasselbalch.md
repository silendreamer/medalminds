#### What Is a Buffer?

A **buffer** is a solution that resists significant changes in pH when small amounts of strong acid or strong base are added. A buffer consists of a **weak acid (HA)** and its **conjugate base (A-)** (or equivalently, a weak base and its conjugate acid) present in comparable concentrations. Common biological buffers include the carbonate system (H2CO3 / HCO3-) in blood and phosphate buffers in cells.

#### How Buffer Action Works

When a small amount of strong acid (H+) is added to a buffer, the conjugate base consumes it: A- + H+ → HA. When strong base (OH-) is added, the weak acid neutralizes it: HA + OH- → A- + H2O. In both cases the pH shifts only slightly because the equilibrium concentrations adjust without a large change in [H+]. The buffer fails — **buffer capacity** is exceeded — when the added acid or base consumes virtually all of one component.

#### The Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation

The pH of a buffer is calculated with the **Henderson-Hasselbalch equation**:

pH = pKa + log([A-] / [HA])

where pKa = -log(Ka) of the weak acid, [A-] is the molar concentration of the conjugate base, and [HA] is the molar concentration of the weak acid. This equation is derived directly from the Ka expression. At the **half-equivalence point** of a titration [A-] = [HA], so log(1) = 0 and pH = pKa — a key fact in both buffer design and titration analysis.

#### Buffer Capacity and Optimal Range

**Buffer capacity** is the amount of acid or base a buffer can absorb before its pH changes substantially. Capacity is greatest when [A-] ≈ [HA] (i.e., pH ≈ pKa) and increases with higher total concentrations of the buffer components. A buffer is effective within approximately **pKa ± 1** pH unit. To prepare a buffer at a target pH, choose a weak acid whose pKa is within 1 unit of that target, then adjust the [A-]/[HA] ratio using Henderson-Hasselbalch.
