## Subtopic: U.S. Department of Energy & National Labs

### DOE Structure and National Laboratory System
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** doe-structure-national-labs
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** DOE organization · National laboratories · Mission areas · Scientific research
**Summary:** Understand the structure and role of the U.S. Department of Energy in energy innovation.

#### Department of Energy Organization
The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), established 1977, operates under the Secretary of Energy (Cabinet-level position). Major organizational units:

1. **Office of Science:** Operates the largest physics, chemistry, and materials research enterprise in the U.S.; operates 10 national laboratories (Argonne, Berkeley, Brookhaven, Fermilab, Jefferson Lab, Lawrence Livermore, NREL, Oak Ridge, Pacific Northwest, SLAC). Focus: basic research in energy, physics, chemistry, materials; supercomputing (leadership computing facilities).

2. **Energy Efficiency & Renewable Energy (EERE):** Develops and deploys renewable energy technologies (solar, wind, geothermal, bioenergy); energy efficiency in buildings and industry. ~$4 B/year budget supporting technology development, demonstration, and deployment.

3. **Fossil Energy & Carbon Management:** Manages coal, natural gas, and carbon capture research; NETL (National Energy Technology Laboratory) in Pennsylvania and West Virginia. Focus: improving fossil fuel efficiency, reducing emissions, carbon capture.

4. **Nuclear Energy:** Manages nuclear power research, waste management (Yucca Mountain repository program, in limbo), advanced reactor development (small modular reactors, SMRs).

5. **Electricity, Delivery & Energy Reliability:** Oversees grid modernization, energy storage, power systems research.

6. **Defense Programs:** Nuclear weapons stewardship (Los Alamos, Lawrence Livermore); separate from civilian energy programs but shares some research infrastructure.

Annual DOE budget is ~$45–50 B; roughly 1/3 goes to national labs R&D, 1/3 to nuclear weapons stewardship, 1/3 to energy programs (renewables, efficiency, nuclear, fossil, grid).

#### National Laboratories and Research Areas
**Argonne National Laboratory (Illinois):** ~6,000 employees; focus on transportation electrification, battery research, advanced computing for energy applications. Operates Advanced Photon Source (APS, synchrotron X-ray facility, ~$32 M capital upgrade to diffraction-limited APS underway).

**Oak Ridge National Laboratory (Tennessee):** ~5,000 employees; largest DOE lab. Focus on advanced manufacturing, computational modeling for energy materials, neutron science (Spallation Neutron Source). Operates Summit (world's most powerful supercomputer as of 2024, 1.2 exaFLOPS).

**Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (California):** ~3,200 employees. Research: energy efficiency, renewable energy, materials science, climate modeling. Operates Advanced Light Source (ALS), another synchrotron X-ray facility.

**National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL, Colorado):** ~3,000 employees. Mission: renewable energy and energy efficiency R&D. Focus: photovoltaics (record lab efficiency ~22% silicon), bioenergy, wind, geothermal, energy storage, grid integration modeling. Provides technical support to EERE deployment programs.

**Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (California):** ~7,000 employees; primary mission is nuclear weapons stewardship. Also conducts energy research: inertial confinement fusion (NIF, National Ignition Facility), advanced manufacturing, climate modeling.

**Los Alamos National Laboratory (New Mexico):** ~4,000 employees. Nuclear security and stewardship, advanced reactor concepts (molten salt, fast reactors), materials research.

**Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (Washington):** ~4,500 employees. Energy efficiency in buildings, grid modernization, atmospheric chemistry relevant to climate.

#### Research Focus Areas
DOE labs concentrate on long-term research (5–20 year timescales) too risky or capital-intensive for industry:

- **Materials science:** Advanced materials for solar cells, batteries, fuel cells, fusion reactors.
- **Energy storage:** Battery chemistry, flow batteries, thermal storage, supercapacitors.
- **Nuclear science:** Fusion energy (ITER, NIF), advanced fission reactors (SMRs, fast reactors), waste transmutation.
- **Computing:** Supercomputers for energy modeling, machine learning for grid optimization, molecular dynamics simulations.
- **Bioenergy:** Biofuels from algae, lignocellulosic biomass, synthetic biology for bioengineering.

#### Review Questions
1. List the 10 national laboratories under the DOE Office of Science and their primary research areas.
2. Explain the difference between DOE's role in energy R&D (EERE) and its role in nuclear weapons stewardship.
3. Describe how a DOE national laboratory contributes to renewable energy technology commercialization (e.g., from basic research to deployment).

## Subtopic: Energy Efficiency Standards & Building Science

### Energy Codes and Appliance Standards
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** energy-codes-appliance-standards
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** CAFE standards · Appliance labeling · Building codes · Energy audits
**Summary:** Understand regulatory frameworks that improve energy efficiency across sectors.

#### Vehicle Efficiency Standards (CAFE)
Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards, established 1975 (post-oil crisis), mandate that automotive manufacturer fleets meet minimum efficiency targets. Current U.S. standards (2024): 49 MPG equivalent (combined fleet average) for model year 2026, increasing to 56 MPG by 2030 (electric vehicles count as zero-emission, inflating MPG equivalent). The standard is applied as:

Average fuel consumption (gal/100 km) = 235.2 / (weighted average MPG)

Manufacturers must average across all models and variants they sell; larger vehicles (SUVs, trucks) are permitted lower efficiency in exchange for increased cost and technological innovation penalties. Fuel consumption standards account for vehicle size (two standards: footprint-based for cars, light trucks); size categories allow trucks to be less efficient than cars.

Penalties: manufacturers exceeding the standard by >10% pay $55 per vehicle per 0.1 MPG shortfall, imposed as fuel consumption fees. This creates incentive for efficiency technologies: (1) engine downsizing + turbocharging; (2) transmission efficiency (8–10 speed automatics vs. 4–6 speed); (3) lightweight materials (aluminum, composite body panels); (4) electrification (hybrid, plug-in hybrid, all-electric).

#### Appliance Standards
DOE appliance standards regulate efficiency of refrigerators, washing machines, air conditioners, furnaces, water heaters, and lights. Example refrigerator standard (2014 update): maximum energy consumption 730 kWh/year (vs. ~1,800 kWh/year for 1970s appliances). Standard is enforced by: (1) manufacturer testing per DOE protocol; (2) verification testing at independent labs; (3) fines for violations ($432 per unit sold above standard, 2024). Standards save consumers money (lower operating cost outweighs higher purchase price) and reduce peak grid load (aggregated savings ~3–5% of US electricity).

LED lighting standards (effective 2020–2022): all general-service lamps must be LED-equivalent efficiency. Incandescent and CFL (compact fluorescent) production largely phased out. LEDs achieve 90–100 lumens/watt (vs. 60 lumens/watt for incandescent, 60 lumens/watt for CFL). Annual savings per household: ~$100 on lighting electricity.

#### Building Energy Codes
Model energy code: International Energy Conservation Code (IECC), updated every 3 years. Adoption varies by state/municipality; California Title 24 is most stringent, requiring solar-ready new construction. Codes specify:

- **Insulation levels:** R-value (thermal resistance) requirements vary by climate zone (higher R in cold climates).
- **HVAC efficiency:** Minimum SEER (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio) for air conditioning, HSPF (Heating Seasonal Performance Factor) for heat pumps, AFUE (Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency) for furnaces.
- **Envelope air tightness:** Blower door test limits infiltration (air leakage) to <7 air changes per hour (ACH) at 50 Pa pressure (new construction).
- **Renewable energy:** Increasingly required (e.g., California requires solar PV or fuel cells on new single-family homes since 2020).

Building energy codes reduce consumption by ~30–40% vs. pre-code practice; enforced by building inspectors and third-party IECC-certified verifiers.

#### Energy Auditing and Commissioning
An energy audit identifies opportunities for efficiency improvements:

1. **Walk-through audit:** Qualitative inspection, no instruments; identifies obvious issues (leaks, poor insulation). Cost: ~$200–500.
2. **Standard audit:** Measurements (thermography, blower door, power meter); historical energy bills analyzed; recommendations with estimated savings. Cost: ~$500–1,500.
3. **Investment-grade audit:** Detailed energy modeling, equipment testing, financial analysis. Cost: ~$1,500–5,000. Justifies retrofits with measured savings guarantees (Energy Service Agreements).

Commissioning (Cx) is verification that building systems operate as designed. Retrocommissioning (identifying operational issues in existing buildings) can achieve 5–15% energy savings with minimal capital cost, often through control software adjustments and operator training.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain the CAFE standard for automobiles, and calculate the average fleet fuel consumption needed to meet 50 MPG equivalent.
2. Compare the efficiency of incandescent (60 lumens/watt), CFL (60 lumens/watt), and LED (95 lumens/watt) lighting. Calculate annual electricity cost for 10,000 light-hours/year at $0.12/kWh.
3. Describe how energy auditing and commissioning save energy in buildings, and give examples of low-cost measures (non-capital).

## Subtopic: Energy Economics & LCOE

### Levelized Cost of Energy Analysis
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** energy-economics-lcoe-analysis
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** Capital cost · Capacity factor · LCOE formula · Technology comparison
**Summary:** Analyze the economic competitiveness of energy technologies.

#### LCOE Formula and Inputs
Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) is the average cost per unit energy over a project lifetime, accounting for all capital, operating, and decommissioning costs discounted to present value. Formula:

LCOE = (ΣCapital + ΣO&M + Decommissioning) / (Σ Energy_produced) [$/kWh]

More precisely, using net present value discounting at rate r over project lifetime T years:

LCOE = Σ_{t=0}^{T} [(Capex_t + O&M_t) / (1+r)^t] / Σ_{t=0}^{T} [Energy_t / (1+r)^t]

Simplified version (constant annual costs, perpetual life):

LCOE = (Capex × CRF + O&M) / (Energy/year) ≈ (Capex × 0.067 + O&M) / (8.76 × CF × Prated)

where CRF = 0.067 is capital recovery factor (for 20-year life, 7% discount rate), O&M is annual operating cost ($/kW/year), Energy/year = 8.76 MWh/kW/year × Capacity Factor (CF).

#### LCOE Comparison Across Technologies
2024 LCOE estimates (U.S., $/kWh, including transmission connection, capacity reserves):

| Technology | Capex ($/kW) | O&M ($/kW/yr) | CF | LCOE ($/kWh) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Utility solar PV | 800–1,200 | 10–15 | 0.25 | 0.02–0.04 |
| Onshore wind | 1,200–1,600 | 40–50 | 0.35 | 0.03–0.06 |
| Offshore wind | 2,500–4,000 | 80–120 | 0.45 | 0.07–0.12 |
| Natural gas (new) | 500–800 | 30–40 | 0.50 | 0.04–0.08 |
| Coal (new, rare) | 2,500–3,500 | 50–70 | 0.80 | 0.08–0.15 |
| Nuclear (new) | 6,000–9,000 | 100–150 | 0.90 | 0.12–0.19 |
| Hydropower | 1,500–5,000 | 20–50 | 0.40 | 0.04–0.15 |
| Geothermal | 2,500–4,000 | 100–200 | 0.80 | 0.07–0.12 |

Solar and wind have achieved parity with fossil fuels on LCOE; however, LCOE does not account for system costs (storage, grid, reserve capacity), which favor dispatchable sources (nuclear, hydro, natural gas).

#### Sensitivity and Scenarios
LCOE is sensitive to key assumptions:

1. **Capacity factor:** 10% change in CF changes LCOE by ~10% (inverse relationship). Wind LCOE depends strongly on site quality (good sites CF=0.40, poor sites CF=0.20).
2. **Discount rate:** 7% → 8% increases LCOE by ~15% for capital-intensive technologies (nuclear, wind). Low discount rates favor high-capital projects.
3. **Lifetime:** 20 years vs. 30 years for wind reduces LCOE by ~15% (amortization over longer period). Actual plant life may be 25–50 years; book value vs. true economic cost matters for policy.
4. **Learning and cost reduction:** Solar PV LCOE declined 90% from 2010–2023 ($0.40/kWh → $0.04/kWh); continued cost reduction expected as markets scale. Conversely, nuclear cost has increased (regulation, labor) and is unfavorable to LCOE improvement.

#### System Costs and Grid Integration
LCOE compares busbar cost (plant-gate); system costs add transmission, interconnection, grid balancing, storage. For high wind/solar penetration (>50%), system costs increase significantly:

- **Transmission:** $50–200/kW to connect remote wind sites to load centers.
- **Storage:** $100–300/kWh battery (capital) + ~$0.05/kWh (annual O&M for 4-hour storage).
- **Overbuilding and curtailment:** Excess generation during low-demand hours may be curtailed (wasted) if storage is insufficient. In high-renewable grids, ~5–20% renewable generation is curtailed.

Fully-loaded system LCOE (including storage for firm power) for 100% renewable is ~$0.08–0.15/kWh (2024 estimates, declining). This exceeds fossil baseline but improves with cost reduction and grid efficiency innovations.

#### Worked Example: Wind vs. Gas Generation Comparison
Compare a 100 MW onshore wind farm vs. a 100 MW natural gas plant. Assume 20-year project life, 7% discount rate.

**Wind inputs:**
- Capex: $1,400/kW → $140 M total
- O&M: $45/kW/year → $4.5 M/year
- CF: 0.35
- Energy/year: 100 MW × 8.76 MWh/MW/year × 0.35 = 306.6 GWh/year

**Gas inputs:**
- Capex: $650/kW → $65 M total
- O&M: $35/kW/year → $3.5 M/year (fixed) + variable fuel cost (see below)
- CF: 0.50 (economic operation at 50% capacity on average)
- Fuel cost: Natural gas $5/MMBtu, efficiency 45% → fuel cost $11/MWh (variable)
- Energy/year: 100 MW × 8.76 × 0.50 = 438 GWh/year

**LCOE calculation:**

Wind LCOE:
- Capital cost amortized: $140 M × 0.067 = $9.38 M/year
- O&M: $4.5 M/year
- Total cost: $13.88 M/year
- LCOE = $13.88 M / 306.6 GWh = **$0.0453/kWh** (~$45.3/MWh)

Gas LCOE:
- Capital cost amortized: $65 M × 0.067 = $4.36 M/year
- O&M (fixed): $3.5 M/year
- O&M (fuel variable): 438 GWh × $11/MWh = $4.82 M/year
- Total cost: $12.68 M/year
- LCOE = $12.68 M / 438 GWh = **$0.0290/kWh** (~$29.0/MWh)

**Conclusion:** Gas has lower LCOE on busbar basis (~$29/MWh vs. $45/MWh for wind). However, if natural gas price increases to $7/MMBtu, fuel cost rises to ~$15.4/MWh, increasing gas LCOE to ~$0.0380/kWh, approaching wind. And wind has zero fuel cost risk.

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate LCOE for a 50 MW solar farm: Capex $900/kW, O&M $12/kW/year, CF 0.25, 20-year life, 7% discount rate.
2. A new coal plant costs $3,000/kW, $60/kW/year O&M, CF 0.80. Calculate LCOE and compare to your solar result from Q1. What explains the difference?
3. Explain why system costs (storage, transmission, balancing) are not included in LCOE, and why system LCOE is relevant for policy decisions on high-renewable scenarios.

## Subtopic: Hydrogen Economy & Fuel Cells

### Hydrogen Production and Fuel Cell Technology
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hydrogen-production-fuel-cells
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** Hydrogen production · Fuel cell chemistry · Efficiency · Hydrogen economy
**Summary:** Understand hydrogen as an energy vector and fuel cell technology.

#### Hydrogen Production Routes
Hydrogen (H₂) is an energy carrier, not a primary energy source; it must be produced from another energy source.

1. **Steam methane reforming (SMR):** CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3 H₂ (at 700–900°C, Ni catalyst). Current dominant route (~95% global H₂). ΔH = +206 kJ/mol (endothermic, requires heat input). Efficiency ~75–80% (energy in H₂ product / energy in methane feed + heat). CO is further oxidized to CO₂: CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂ (water-gas shift reaction, exothermic). Net: CH₄ + 2 H₂O → CO₂ + 4 H₂.

2. **Electrolysis:** 2 H₂O + electricity → 2 H₂ + O₂. Current: ~5% of global H₂, growing. Electrolyzers: alkaline (30–50 MW units, $400–600/kW), PEM (proton exchange membrane, <10 MW units, $500–1,000/kW), solid oxide (experimental, <1 MW). Efficiency ~65–75% (electrical energy in / chemical energy in H₂). At $60/MWh electricity, H₂ cost ~$1.5–2.5/kg (competitive with blue hydrogen at high carbon prices ~$100/ton CO₂).

3. **Thermochemical cycles:** Sulfur-iodine cycle, calcium-bromine cycle use high-temperature heat (>800°C, from concentrated solar or nuclear) to split water without electricity. Efficiency ~40–50%; still experimental. Advantage: if driven by renewable heat (concentrated solar, geothermal), H₂ is fully renewable.

4. **Photoelectrochemical:** Direct splitting of water using semiconductors (band-gap ~2–2.5 eV for visible light absorption) + catalyst. Efficiency potential ~15–20%, but lab-stage technology.

#### Fuel Cell Operation and Efficiency
Fuel cells electrochemically oxidize hydrogen in a controlled manner, producing electricity without combustion. A proton exchange membrane (PEM) fuel cell:

Anode: 2 H₂ → 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ (oxidation, loses electrons)
Cathode: O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ → 2 H₂O (reduction, gains electrons)
Overall: 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O

Electrode potential: Ecell = Eocell − (RT/nF) ln(Q), where Eocell ≈ 1.23 V (theoretical). At 1 A/cm² current density (practical operation), voltage drops to ~0.6–0.7 V due to overpotential losses. Stack voltage = Ecell × N_cells; 80 cells (80 V) is typical for automotive fuel cells.

**Efficiency:** Theoretical efficiency (at Eocell, 1.23 V) is ηth = (ΔG)/(ΔH) ≈ 83% (ratio of Gibbs free energy to enthalpy for H₂ oxidation). Practical efficiency at full load is 50–60% (automotive); at part-load, efficiency improves to 70–75%. Compare to internal combustion engine (25–30% efficiency) or battery electric vehicle (85–90% drivetrain efficiency but sourced from grid electricity, which is 30–50% efficient from fossil fuels, net 15–45%).

#### Hydrogen Storage and Distribution
Hydrogen is challenging to store due to low density:

- **Gaseous H₂:** 0.08 kg/m³ at 1 atm, 20°C. Requires high-pressure tanks (350 bar, 70 MPa for vehicles); 1 liter stores ~0.006 kg H₂ (vs. ~0.8 kg gasoline). Tank cost ~$2–3/liter usable H₂.
- **Liquid H₂:** 71 kg/m³ at −253°C (boiling point). Requires cryogenic insulation; boil-off losses ~0.3–1%/day. Used for spacecraft; impractical for vehicles/grid.
- **Solid storage:** Metal hydrides (MgH₂, LaNi₅H₆) store 5–10% H₂ by weight; release H₂ when heated. Reversibility and cost remain challenges; lab-stage.
- **Synthetic storage:** Convert H₂ to liquid (methanol, ammonia, synthetic hydrocarbons) for dense storage and existing fuel infrastructure. Efficiency loss: H₂ → X → electricity or heat, ~60–70% round-trip for methanol.

**Distribution:** Hydrogen is produced at large reformers (~100 MW scale) and transported by pipeline (~1,600 km pipeline in North America/Europe). Delivery cost ~$3–5/kg H₂. Fueling stations (~500 globally, mostly Japan, Germany, California) dispense compressed H₂ at 350–700 bar to vehicles at cost ~$8–14/kg H₂ (2024).

#### Hydrogen Economy Projections
"Hydrogen economy" envisions H₂ replacing fossil fuels in hard-to-decarbonize sectors (heavy industry, aviation, shipping). Global H₂ production is ~120 million tons/year (~$170 B market); ~50% goes to ammonia production (fertilizer, explosives), ~30% to refining (hydrocracking), ~20% to chemicals (methanol, etc.). Energy applications (fuel cells, power generation) are <1% currently.

Projections (IEA Net Zero 2050 scenario): H₂ grows to 300 million tons/year by 2050, with 80% from electrolysis (driven by renewable electricity). Cost target: $1.5–2.5/kg H₂ (goal: parity with fossil fuels on carbon-adjusted basis). Challenges: building electrolyzer capacity (5–10 TW by 2050, equivalent to current global electricity), sourcing renewable electricity, developing storage/transport infrastructure.

#### Review Questions
1. Compare steam methane reforming (SMR) vs. electrolysis for hydrogen production in terms of energy input, efficiency, and CO₂ emissions.
2. Calculate the efficiency of a fuel cell operating at 0.65 V per cell, 80 cells, 50 A current, with hydrogen energy content 286 kJ/mol.
3. Estimate the cost of hydrogen delivered to an automotive fuel station ($60/MWh electricity, $3.5/kg green H₂ production + $5/kg transport). Compare to gasoline at $3/gallon (0.8 kg/gallon).

## Subtopic: Emerging Technologies (Fusion, Advanced Nuclear, Grid Storage)

### Fusion Energy and Advanced Fission Reactors
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** fusion-advanced-fission
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** Fusion reactions · Plasma confinement · Inertial confinement · Advanced reactor designs
**Summary:** Understand next-generation nuclear technologies and their potential.

#### Nuclear Fusion
Fusion combines light nuclei to form heavier nuclei, releasing energy (mass difference). Practical fusion reaction: deuterium + tritium (D-T fusion): ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV. Reaction requires nuclei to overcome Coulomb repulsion, necessitating temperatures >100 million Kelvin (kT > MeV) for thermal fusion. At these temperatures, matter is plasma (ionized gas, mobile electrons and ions). Fusion power density (power per unit volume) = n_D × n_T × σv × E, where n is number density, σv is reaction cross-section × velocity (∝ temperature), E is energy per reaction. At 150 million K, fusion power density is ~10 MW/m³ (very high, 1 m³ plasma could power a city; challenge is confinement).

**Magnetic confinement:** Strong magnetic field (>10 Tesla, generated by superconducting coils) confines plasma by lorentz force F = q v × B. Tokamaks (toroidal chamber) and stellarators (twisted toroid) are leading designs. ITER (International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor, France, under construction) is designed for Q ≥ 10 (10 MW output per 1 MW heating input). **NIF (National Ignition Facility, California)** achieved fusion ignition (2022): laser-driven inertial confinement fusion produced 3.15 MJ energy output from 2.05 MJ laser input (Q ≈ 1.5 for laser, but laser efficiency ~1%, so net Q ≈ 0.015 to the electrical grid).

**Challenge:** Commercial fusion requires Q >> 1 (>10) for net power generation, and sustained reaction (continuous operation, not pulse). Current status: closest to commercialization are private companies (Commonwealth Fusion, TAE Technologies) targeting compact, high-field tokamaks with $1–2B funding. Time to electricity: 2030s (optimistic) to 2050s (realistic). Cost forecast: $1–2/W for fusion plant (competitive with solar/wind).

#### Advanced Fission Reactors
**Small modular reactors (SMRs):** <300 MW capacity (vs. conventional ~1,000 MW), designed for factory manufacturing, simple cooling (no active pumps; passive natural convection). Advantages: lower upfront capital (enables small utility, island grids), simplicity reduces operator cost. Disadvantages: /MW cost higher (~$5–8/W vs. $4–6/W for large reactors) due to reduced economies of scale. Examples: NuScale (U.S., 12×77 MW units), X-energy (U.S., high-temperature gas reactor), Rolls-Royce (U.K., light-water SMR). Near-term deployment: 2027–2030s (pilot plants under construction). Global SMR pipeline: ~70 designs, ~20 in construction/licensing phase.

**Advanced fission designs:** (1) **Fast reactors:** Burn uranium-238 (99.3% of natural uranium vs. 0.7% U-235 in thermal reactors), enabling much higher burnup and reducing long-lived waste. Russia operates BN-600/800 fast reactors; China building CEFR fast reactor. (2) **Molten salt reactors (MSRs):** Liquid fuel (salt with fissile/fertile nuclei dissolved) instead of solid fuel. Advantages: simplified cooling (salt is coolant), online refueling, high-temperature operation (>600°C, suitable for industrial process heat or hydrogen production). Disadvantages: materials challenges (corrosion, tritium breeding control). (3) **High-temperature gas reactors (HTGRs):** Helium cooling, TRISO fuel (coated particles), inherent safety (can shutdown without active cooling, meltdown-proof). Low power density (small size for given power) increases cost. Deployment: demonstration units (China HTR-PM, Germany AVR) showing promise; commercial reactors expected 2030s.

#### Grid-Scale Energy Storage
Battery energy storage (BESS): lithium-ion dominates (98% of grid storage as of 2024). Cost: $100–200/kWh (2024, declining to $50–100/kWh by 2030). 4-hour discharge duration is typical for utility BESS (264 GWh global capacity deployed, growing ~50% per year). Constraints: lithium resource limits (enough for ~1–2 TW renewable capacity), supply chain concentration (60% processing in China). Alternative chemistries in development: (1) **Flow batteries:** Iron-vanadium, zinc-bromine; longer duration (8–12 hour), lower cost/kWh (~$60–120/kWh) but more volumetric area required. (2) **Compressed air energy storage (CAES):** Compressed air in caverns (salt domes, depleted gas fields); 70–75% round-trip efficiency, cost ~$30–50/kWh (capital-heavy, geological site-dependent). Global capacity: ~3 GW, limited by suitable geology. (3) **Thermal storage:** Molten salt (see geothermal section) or hot water tanks for district heating; cost ~$10–50/kWh. (4) **Mechanical storage:** Flywheels (5–30 MWh units, cost ~$100–300/kWh, useful for frequency regulation), gravitational (Energy Vault gravity blocks, cost competitive but limited by height), pumped hydro (only 170 GW global, limited by geography).

Future grid storage requirement (100% renewable scenarios, 2050): ~50–150 TWh stored capacity (net), requiring 10–100× current capacity and proportional cost reduction (~$20–30/kWh achievable with scale and recycling).

#### Review Questions
1. Explain why fusion requires 100+ million Kelvin temperature, and compare magnetic vs. inertial confinement approaches.
2. Calculate the Q (energy output / input) needed for commercially viable fusion power, assuming electricity cost $50/MWh and thermal efficiency 40%.
3. Compare grid-scale battery (lithium-ion, $150/kWh) vs. flow battery ($100/kWh) vs. pumped hydro ($80/kWh) for 4-hour storage. Which is most economical for a 100 MW system? Consider capital cost and round-trip efficiency (80%, 70%, 85% respectively).
