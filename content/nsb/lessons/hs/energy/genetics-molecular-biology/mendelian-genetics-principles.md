### Introduction to Mendelian Genetics

Mendelian genetics is the foundation of classical genetics, established by Gregor Mendel in the mid-19th century through his experiments with pea plants. Mendel's work revealed how traits are inherited from one generation to the next, leading to the formulation of key principles that govern genetic inheritance. His findings laid the groundwork for modern genetics, influencing our understanding of heredity, variation, and the role of genes in living organisms.

### Dominant and Recessive Traits

In Mendelian genetics, traits are classified as dominant or recessive based on their expression in offspring. A dominant trait is one that is expressed in the phenotype even when only one copy of the allele is present, while a recessive trait requires two copies of the allele for expression. For example, in pea plants, the allele for tall stems (T) is dominant over the allele for short stems (t). Thus, a plant with either TT or Tt genotype will exhibit the tall phenotype, while only the tt genotype will result in a short phenotype.

### The Law of Segregation

The law of segregation states that during the formation of gametes, the two alleles for a trait segregate from each other so that each gamete carries only one allele for each gene. This principle explains how offspring inherit one allele from each parent, resulting in genetic variation. For instance, if a plant with the genotype Tt undergoes meiosis, it will produce gametes with either the T or t allele, ensuring that the genetic information is passed on in a predictable manner.

### Punnett Squares

Punnett squares are a valuable tool for predicting the genotypic and phenotypic ratios of offspring resulting from a genetic cross. By arranging the alleles of each parent along the top and side of a grid, students can visualize the possible combinations of alleles in the offspring. For example, crossing a homozygous tall plant (TT) with a homozygous short plant (tt) using a Punnett square will yield all Tt offspring, demonstrating that all progeny will be tall due to the dominance of the tall allele.

### Monohybrid and Dihybrid Crosses

Monohybrid crosses involve a single trait, while dihybrid crosses examine the inheritance of two traits simultaneously. In a monohybrid cross, the focus is on one gene, such as the height of pea plants, while a dihybrid cross considers two genes, such as seed color and seed shape. The phenotypic ratio for a monohybrid cross of two heterozygous parents (Tt x Tt) is typically 3:1, while the phenotypic ratio for a dihybrid cross (RrYy x RrYy) is 9:3:3:1, illustrating the complexity of inheritance when multiple traits are involved.

### Extensions of Mendelian Genetics

While Mendel's principles provide a solid foundation for understanding inheritance, there are extensions and exceptions to his laws. Incomplete dominance, codominance, and polygenic inheritance are examples of how traits can exhibit more complex inheritance patterns. For instance, in incomplete dominance, the phenotype of heterozygotes is an intermediate blend of the dominant and recessive traits, such as red and white flowers producing pink offspring. Understanding these variations is crucial for a comprehensive grasp of genetics.
