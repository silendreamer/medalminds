#### What Is Reprocessing?
Reprocessing chemically separates spent fuel into: (1) usable materials — uranium and plutonium; (2) fission product waste. The dominant technology is PUREX (Plutonium-Uranium Reduction Extraction), using tributyl phosphate solvent in nitric acid. France reprocesses ~1,700 tonnes of spent fuel per year at La Hague; Russia and UK also reprocess. The US banned civilian reprocessing in 1977 (Carter administration, proliferation concerns); the ban was lifted in 1981 but economics prevent it.

#### Mixed Oxide (MOX) Fuel
Separated plutonium is mixed with uranium oxide to make MOX fuel, usable in standard LWRs. France uses MOX in ~20 reactors, reducing fresh uranium demand by ~15%. Japan planned widespread MOX use (Rokkasho reprocessing plant) but the program has faced repeated delays since 1997.

#### Proliferation Concerns
Separated civilian plutonium is weapons-usable — less than 10 kg can make a nuclear weapon. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) safeguards monitor civilian plutonium inventories, but perfect accounting is difficult. The US opposes reprocessing in many countries precisely because of proliferation risk. Advanced reprocessing concepts (UREX+, pyroprocessing) aim to keep plutonium mixed with other actinides — weapons-unusable without further separation.

#### Waste Volume Reduction
Reprocessing reduces the volume of HLW requiring geological disposal by ~80% (fission products are a much smaller volume than spent fuel assemblies). The remaining HLW is vitrified — immobilized in borosilicate glass — which is chemically stable for millions of years. France's HLW from 50 years of nuclear power would fit under the Eiffel Tower.
