#### Nuclear Composition
A nucleus contains protons (charge +1e) and neutrons (charge 0), collectively called nucleons. The atomic number Z gives the number of protons; the mass number A gives the total nucleons (A = Z + N, where N is neutrons). Isotopes share the same Z but differ in N. The nuclear radius scales as r = r₀A^(1/3), where r₀ ≈ 1.2 fm (femtometers, 10⁻¹⁵ m) — the nucleus is ~10⁵ times smaller than the atom.

#### The Strong Nuclear Force
Protons repel each other via the Coulomb force. The nucleus would instantly fly apart if not for the strong nuclear force — one of the four fundamental forces. The strong force is: (1) attractive between nucleons; (2) charge-independent (acts equally on proton-proton, proton-neutron, neutron-neutron); (3) short-range (effective only within ~2 fm); (4) much stronger than electrostatic repulsion at short distances. Beyond ~2 fm, the strong force vanishes and electrostatic repulsion dominates.

#### The N/Z Ratio and Stability
For light nuclei (Z < 20), stable nuclei have roughly equal numbers of protons and neutrons (N ≈ Z). As Z increases, more neutrons are needed to dilute proton-proton repulsion — the N/Z ratio rises above 1. Above Z = 82 (lead), no stable nuclei exist; all heavier nuclei are radioactive. The "valley of stability" on a nuclide chart maps all stable and unstable isotopes.

#### Nuclear Density
Nuclear density is roughly constant across all nuclei: ~2 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³. This extraordinary density — a teaspoon of nuclear matter would weigh 2 billion tons — arises because nucleons pack together at nuclear distances. This near-incompressibility is one reason the strong force saturates (each nucleon interacts only with its nearest neighbors).
