#### Why Nuclei Decay
Some nuclei have too many neutrons, too many protons, or too much internal energy to remain stable. These unstable nuclei lower their energy by emitting particles or radiation — a process called radioactive decay. Decay is spontaneous and random at the level of individual atoms; you cannot predict when any single nucleus will decay. However, for large collections of atoms, decay follows a precise statistical law.

#### Types of Radioactive Decay
Alpha decay ejects a helium-4 nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons), reducing the atomic number by 2 and mass number by 4. Beta-minus decay converts a neutron to a proton, emitting an electron and antineutrino — the atomic number increases by 1. Beta-plus decay converts a proton to a neutron, emitting a positron. Gamma decay releases high-energy photons without changing the nucleus's composition — it often follows alpha or beta decay.

#### Activity and the Becquerel
The activity of a radioactive sample is the number of decays per second, measured in becquerels (Bq): 1 Bq = 1 decay/s. The curie (Ci), an older unit, equals 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq. Activity depends on both the number of atoms present and the decay constant (λ): A = λN. As atoms decay, N decreases, so activity drops over time.

#### Decay Equations
A nuclear decay equation must conserve both mass number (A) and atomic number (Z). For uranium-238 alpha decay: ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He. For carbon-14 beta-minus decay: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e + antineutrino. Always check that the top numbers (mass) and bottom numbers (charge) balance on both sides.

#### Real-World Context
Natural radioactivity is everywhere: potassium-40 in bananas, radon-222 in basements, carbon-14 in all living things. Medical PET scans use positron-emitting isotopes (fluorine-18, t½ = 110 min). Smoke detectors use americium-241. Understanding decay types and rates is fundamental to nuclear medicine, power generation, and waste management.
