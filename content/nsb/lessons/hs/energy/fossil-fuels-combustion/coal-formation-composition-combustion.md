#### Coal Formation and Rank
Coal forms from plant material (peat) buried and subjected to heat and pressure over millions of years. The process is called coalification: peat → lignite → subbituminous → bituminous → anthracite, representing increasing rank (carbon content and energy density). Peat has ~60% carbon; lignite ~70%; bituminous coal ~80%; anthracite ~90%. Higher rank coals formed under greater pressure and heat. The degree of coalification is irreversible—once peat becomes anthracite, increasing pressure will not revert it. Global coal reserves are ~1 trillion tonnes; coal is the largest fossil fuel reserve by energy content and is found on every continent.

#### Coal Composition and Properties
Coal is a complex heterogeneous mixture of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and sulfur, plus trace minerals (silica, alumina, iron oxide). Bituminous coal typically contains 75–90% carbon, 4–5% hydrogen, 5–15% oxygen, 1–2% nitrogen, and 1–3% sulfur. The oxygen and hydrogen content is lower than peat because coalification removes these elements preferentially. Sulfur exists as pyrite (FeS₂, "fool's gold"), organic sulfur, or sulfate. Mineral matter (ash) ranges from 3–15% by weight; upon burning, ash fuses into clinkers (liquid slag) that damage boiler surfaces. Premium coals have low ash and sulfur content.

#### Proximate and Ultimate Analysis
Proximate analysis measures moisture, ash, volatile matter, and fixed carbon. Volatiles (15–40% for bituminous coal) are organic compounds that vaporize at ~120°C without combustion; they ignite easily and produce flame. Fixed carbon (40–80%) is solid residue that burns after volatiles are driven off; it burns slowly and with little flame. Ultimate analysis determines elemental composition: %C, %H, %N, %S, %O. Together, these analyses predict coal's combustion properties—high volatile content indicates fast ignition but smoky flame; high fixed carbon indicates slower burn but higher energy density.

#### Coal Combustion and Reaction Sequence
Coal combustion proceeds through stages. First, moisture evaporates (endothermic). Second, volatile matter is driven off and ignites, producing flame and contributing 20–40% of total heat. Third, fixed carbon burns as a solid at the surface: C + O₂ → CO₂ (main reaction); C + ½ O₂ → CO (if oxygen is limited). The reaction rate depends on temperature, oxygen concentration, and coal particle size. Finely ground coal (70 μm) used in power plants combusts in 0.1–1 second; larger lumps take minutes. Combustion temperatures reach 1,400–1,600°C in furnaces; coal ash melts and collects as slag or fly ash (fine particles carried by exhaust gas).

#### Sulfur Emissions and Control
Sulfur in coal oxidizes to SO₂ during combustion: S + O₂ → SO₂. SO₂ is a precursor to acid rain (SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃; 2 H₂SO₃ + O₂ → 2 H₂SO₄). Coal combustion is the largest anthropogenic SO₂ source. Flue gas desulfurization (FGD) removes SO₂ by contacting exhaust gas with calcium oxide or hydroxide: 2 CaO + 2 SO₂ + O₂ → 2 CaSO₄. Selective catalytic reduction (SCR) removes NOₓ by reaction with ammonia over vanadium catalysts: 4 NO + 4 NH₃ + O₂ → 4 N₂ + 6 H₂O. Modern coal plants employ both technologies to meet air quality standards, but these add 3–5% to electricity generation costs.
