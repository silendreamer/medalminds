#### Nitrogen Oxides (NOₓ) Formation
Nitrogen oxides (NO and NO₂, collectively NOₓ) form during high-temperature combustion when nitrogen in air is oxidized. Two pathways:

1. **Thermal NOₓ** (Zeldovich mechanism): N₂ + O ↔ NO + N (at >1,200°C). Rate increases exponentially with temperature; doubling temperature increases NO formation ~10-fold. This is the dominant source in flames and power plant furnaces.

2. **Fuel NOₓ**: Nitrogen in the fuel (organic N compounds) oxidizes directly: fuel-N + O₂ → NOₓ. This occurs at lower temperatures and is significant in coal and heavy oil combustion.

3. **Prompt NOₓ**: Hydrocarbon radicals (CH, CN) react with N₂ near the flame front (in first few milliseconds); contributes ~5–10% of total NO.

In a typical gasoline engine, exhaust contains ~500–1,000 ppm NO (colorless); NO oxidizes in air to NO₂ (brown, pungent gas): 2 NO + O₂ → 2 NO₂. Further oxidation yields nitric acid (HNO₃), an acidic precursor to acid rain.

#### Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning
Carbon monoxide forms under fuel-rich conditions (λ < 1) or incomplete combustion at low temperatures. Mechanism: at high temperatures, CO₂ is reduced: CO₂ + C → 2 CO (Boudouard reaction). At low temperatures, kinetic limitations prevent complete oxidation.

CO is toxic because it binds hemoglobin with affinity 200–300 times higher than O₂, forming carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) and reducing oxygen delivery to tissues. Exposure to 100 ppm for 2 hours produces mild headache; 400 ppm produces dizziness within 1–2 hours; 800 ppm causes collapse and death within 2 hours. In urban areas with heavy traffic, CO concentrations occasionally exceed 10 ppm, a level that can impair cognition and cardiovascular function in sensitive populations. Modern three-way catalytic converters oxidize CO to CO₂: 2 CO + O₂ → 2 CO₂ over Pt-Pd catalyst at ~300°C.

#### Particulate Matter (PM) and Soot
Particulate matter is a mixture of solid and liquid particles suspended in air. Sources include soot (elemental carbon) from incomplete combustion, sulfate and nitrate aerosols from NOₓ and SOₓ, and dust. Size categories: PM10 (diameter <10 μm, enters upper airway), PM2.5 (diameter <2.5 μm, penetrates deep lung), ultrafine particles (<0.1 μm, reach alveoli). Diesel engines produce ~100× more particulate matter than gasoline engines due to diffusion-controlled combustion and locally fuel-rich zones forming soot.

Soot forms through polymerization of aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) at temperatures >1,000°C. Particle size is 10–100 nm; many PM2.5 particles are aggregates of soot. Soot surfaces catalyze NO₂ formation (NO + O₃ → NO₂ on soot surface). Particulate matter is associated with cardiovascular and respiratory disease; epidemiological studies link PM2.5 to premature mortality, with an increase of 10 μg/m³ PM2.5 corresponding to a 4–6% increase in all-cause mortality.

#### Health Effects Summary
WHO air quality guidelines (2021) set annual average limits: PM2.5 ≤ 5 μg/m³, PM10 ≤ 15 μg/m³, NO₂ ≤ 10 μg/m³. U.S. EPA National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) are less stringent: PM2.5 ≤ 12 μg/m³ (annual), NO₂ ≤ 53 ppb (1-hour). Exposure to concentrations above these levels increases risks of asthma exacerbation, bronchitis, reduced lung function, and premature death, particularly in children and the elderly.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain the Zeldovich mechanism for thermal NOₓ formation, and why NOₓ increases exponentially with temperature.
2. Why does CO form in fuel-rich combustion, and what is its mechanism of toxicity in the human body?
3. Compare the sources and health impacts of NO₂, CO, and PM2.5 in urban air pollution.
