#### Diagram Axes and Orientation
The H-R diagram plots luminosity (intrinsic brightness, Y-axis, in solar luminosities L☉) against surface temperature (X-axis, in Kelvin — but increasing to the left, so hot stars are on the left). Color/spectral class (OBAFGKM — "Oh Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me") correlates with temperature: O stars are blue-white (~30,000 K), G stars yellow (~5,800 K like the Sun), M stars red-orange (~3,500 K). Luminosity spans ~10 orders of magnitude (from 10⁻⁴ to 10⁶ L☉).

#### The Main Sequence
Most stars lie on the main sequence — a diagonal band from upper-left (hot, luminous) to lower-right (cool, dim). Stars on the main sequence are fusing hydrogen to helium in their cores. The Sun (G2V, 1 L☉, 5,778 K) sits roughly in the middle. More massive stars are hotter, more luminous, and shorter-lived. The mass-luminosity relation: L ∝ M⁴ approximately. A 10 M☉ star is ~10,000 times more luminous — and lives only ~1/1,000 as long (~10 million years vs 10 billion for the Sun).

#### Giants, Supergiants, and White Dwarfs
Giants (upper right): cool but extremely luminous because they are very large. Red giants have expanded after leaving the main sequence — their cores contract while the outer layers expand dramatically. Supergiants (e.g., Betelgeuse: M2Iab, ~700 R☉, ~10⁵ L☉) are extremely massive evolved stars. White dwarfs (lower left): hot but dim — small (Earth-sized), dense remnants of low-to-medium mass stars after they shed their outer layers. They are off the main sequence because they no longer fuse hydrogen.

#### Reading Stellar Evolution on the H-R Diagram
A star's life is a track on the H-R diagram. A solar-mass star spends ~10 billion years on the main sequence, then expands to a red giant (upper right), loses its outer layers as a planetary nebula, and leaves behind a white dwarf (lower left). A 20 M☉ star evolves faster: main sequence → red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole. The main sequence turnoff point of a star cluster indicates the cluster's age: older clusters have turnoffs at lower luminosity (less massive stars remain).

#### Luminosity Classes
The MK system adds a Roman numeral luminosity class: I = supergiant, II = bright giant, III = giant, IV = subgiant, V = main sequence dwarf. The Sun is G2V. Sirius is A1V. Aldebaran is K5III. Rigel is B8Ia. Luminosity class is read from subtle spectral line widths — narrower lines in giants indicate lower surface gravity (lower pressure broadening).

#### Review Questions
1. On the H-R diagram, which direction is hotter — left or right?
2. Why do high-mass stars evolve off the main sequence faster than low-mass stars?
3. Where on the H-R diagram would you find a star that is very hot but very dim? What type of star is this?
