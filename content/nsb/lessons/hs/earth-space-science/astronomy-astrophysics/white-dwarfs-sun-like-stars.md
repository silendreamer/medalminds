#### The Red Giant Phase and Mass Loss
When a solar-mass star exhausts core hydrogen, the core contracts and heats while the outer layers expand — the star becomes a red giant. On the asymptotic giant branch (AGB), helium shell burning causes thermal pulses that dredge up carbon to the surface. Strong stellar winds expel the outer layers at up to 10–15 km/s, forming an expanding shell of gas and dust — the planetary nebula (named by William Herschel because they appeared disk-like in early telescopes). Famous examples: Ring Nebula (M57), Cat's Eye Nebula, Helix Nebula ("Eye of God").

#### White Dwarf Structure
After the planetary nebula disperses, the exposed stellar core remains as a white dwarf — a compact object roughly Earth-sized (~0.01 R☉) but with ~0.6 M☉. With no nuclear fusion, white dwarfs are supported by electron degeneracy pressure — a quantum mechanical effect: electrons resist being compressed into the same quantum states (Pauli exclusion principle). White dwarfs have densities ~10⁶ g/cm³ — a teaspoon weighs ~5 tons. Surface temperatures can exceed 100,000 K initially, but they cool gradually over billions of years (no new energy source). The oldest white dwarfs serve as cosmic clocks for the age of the galaxy.

#### The Chandrasekhar Limit
Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar (1931) showed that the maximum mass of a white dwarf is ~1.4 M☉. Above this limit, electron degeneracy cannot support the star against gravity. A white dwarf accreting mass in a binary system that exceeds this limit undergoes a Type Ia supernova — a thermonuclear explosion of consistent intrinsic brightness used as a standard candle for cosmological distance measurements.
