#### Core Collapse and the Supernova
A massive star (8–20+ M☉) fuses elements up to iron (Fe) in its core over successive burning stages: H → He → C → Ne → O → Si → Fe. Iron fusion requires energy rather than releasing it, so when the iron core exceeds ~1.4 M☉ (Chandrasekhar mass), it collapses in less than a second. Core density reaches nuclear density (~10¹⁴ g/cm³). The infalling material bounces off the rigid neutron-degenerate core, sending a shockwave outward — a Type II supernova. The visible explosion releases ~10⁴⁴ J of kinetic energy (and ~100 times more in neutrinos). SN 1987A (in the Large Magellanic Cloud) was the nearest supernova since 1604 and confirmed neutrino emission predictions.

#### Neutron Star Properties
The core remnant is a neutron star: ~1.4–3 M☉, radius ~10 km, density ~10¹⁴–10¹⁵ g/cm³ (similar to atomic nuclei). A teaspoon weighs ~10⁸ tons. Supported by neutron degeneracy pressure (analogous to electron degeneracy in white dwarfs). Surface gravity ~2 × 10¹¹ m/s² (20 billion g's). Magnetic fields can reach 10¹² gauss (ordinary refrigerator magnets ~100 gauss). The maximum mass for a neutron star is ~2–3 M☉ (less well-determined than the Chandrasekhar limit).

#### Pulsars: Cosmic Lighthouses
When a neutron star rotates and has its magnetic axis misaligned with the rotation axis, it beams radio waves (and sometimes X-rays or gamma rays) from its magnetic poles. If a beam sweeps past Earth, we detect periodic radio pulses — a pulsar. The first pulsar (PSR B1919+21) was discovered by Jocelyn Bell Burnell in 1967. Periods range from ~1 second (young pulsars) down to ~milliseconds (millisecond pulsars, "recycled" by accretion in binaries). The Crab Pulsar (33 ms period, remnant of the 1054 CE supernova observed by Chinese astronomers) was the first confirmed pulsar-supernova remnant association. Pulsars are extraordinarily stable timekeepers — some rival atomic clock precision.

#### Magnetars and Exotic States
Magnetars are neutron stars with extreme magnetic fields (10¹³–10¹⁵ gauss). They produce soft gamma repeaters (SGR) — periodic bursts of gamma rays from starquakes on their solid crusts. The December 2004 SGR 1806-20 flare briefly outshone the rest of the Milky Way in gamma rays and was detectable even from ~50,000 ly away.
