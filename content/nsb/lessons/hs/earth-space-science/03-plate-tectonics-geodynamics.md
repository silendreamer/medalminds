## Subtopic: Plate Boundaries (Divergent, Convergent, Transform)

### The Theory of Plate Tectonics: A Unified Framework
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hs-ess-plate-tectonics-overview
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** lithosphere · tectonic plates · mantle convection · seafloor spreading · subduction
**Summary:** Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of geology, explaining the distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain ranges, and ocean basins as consequences of moving lithospheric plates.

#### The Structure of the Lithosphere
Earth's outer rigid layer — the **lithosphere** — consists of the crust plus the uppermost mantle. It is broken into ~15 major tectonic plates and several minor ones. Oceanic lithosphere is denser (basaltic composition, ~3.0 g/cm³) and thinner (5–10 km of crust). Continental lithosphere is less dense (granitic composition, ~2.7 g/cm³) and thicker (25–70 km of crust, up to 100 km in cratons). Plates move over the **asthenosphere**, a partially molten, ductile layer of the upper mantle that flows on geologic timescales.

#### Driving Mechanisms
The primary driver of plate motion is **slab pull**: as cold, dense oceanic lithosphere sinks at subduction zones, it drags the rest of the plate behind it like a tablecloth being pulled off a table. **Ridge push** (the weight of elevated mid-ocean ridges pushing material away) is secondary. Mantle convection — driven by heat from Earth's interior (radioactive decay + residual heat of accretion) — moves material that transfers heat to the base of plates, but is not the simple conveyor belt once imagined. GPS measurements show plates move 1–15 cm/yr (fingernail growth rate to a few inches/year).

#### Three Types of Boundaries
Plates interact at three types of boundaries: **divergent** (plates move apart), **convergent** (plates move toward each other), and **transform** (plates slide horizontally past each other). Each produces characteristic geological features and seismicity patterns. Most of Earth's seismic and volcanic activity occurs at plate boundaries.

#### The Wilson Cycle
The **Wilson cycle** describes the birth, growth, and closure of ocean basins over hundreds of millions of years: continental rifting → mid-ocean ridge formation → seafloor spreading → subduction initiation → ocean closure → continental collision. The Atlantic Ocean is currently in its opening phase; the Pacific is closing. This cycle recycles oceanic crust (maximum age ~200 Ma) but preserves ancient continental crust (up to 4.4 Ga).

#### Review Questions
1. Why is oceanic lithosphere denser than continental lithosphere, and what consequences does this have at convergent boundaries?
2. What is slab pull, and why is it considered the primary driver of plate motion?
3. Describe the Wilson cycle in four stages.

### Divergent Boundaries: Where Earth's Surface is Born
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hs-ess-divergent-boundaries
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** mid-ocean ridge · seafloor spreading · continental rifting · East African Rift
**Summary:** At divergent boundaries, plates move apart and new oceanic crust is created by magma upwelling at mid-ocean ridges, or continents are torn apart by rift zones.

#### Mid-Ocean Ridges
Mid-ocean ridges (MORs) are the longest mountain ranges on Earth — the **Mid-Atlantic Ridge** extends ~16,000 km and rises 2–3 km above the surrounding seafloor. Where plates separate, decompression melting of the asthenosphere produces basaltic magma that erupts on the seafloor as pillow lavas, forming new oceanic crust. The ridge is continuously renewed as older crust moves away symmetrically on both sides — **seafloor spreading**, confirmed by symmetric magnetic anomaly stripes (paleomagnetic evidence from the Vine-Matthews-Morley hypothesis, 1963).

#### Magnetic Anomaly Stripes: Key Evidence
As new basaltic oceanic crust forms at a ridge, magnetic minerals align with Earth's magnetic field — which has reversed polarity hundreds of times in geologic history. This creates symmetric stripes of normal and reversed polarity on either side of the ridge. The width of each stripe records how fast spreading occurred (faster = wider stripe). Mapping these stripes in the 1960s provided definitive evidence for seafloor spreading and confirmed Wegener's continental drift hypothesis.

#### Continental Rifting: The East African Rift
When a divergent boundary forms within a continent, it creates a **rift valley** — a graben (down-dropped block) bounded by normal faults. The **East African Rift System** stretches ~6,000 km from the Afar Triangle (where three plates meet) through Kenya and Tanzania. Associated features: elongated lakes (Tanganyika, the world's second deepest), active volcanoes (Kilimanjaro, Nyiragongo), and earthquakes. If rifting continues, East Africa will eventually separate from the continent, creating a new ocean basin — as happened when South America split from Africa ~130 Ma.

#### Review Questions
1. What process creates new oceanic crust at a mid-ocean ridge?
2. How do magnetic anomaly stripes provide evidence for seafloor spreading?
3. What geological features characterize a continental rift zone, and give a modern example.

### Convergent Boundaries: Collision and Subduction
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hs-ess-convergent-boundaries
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** subduction · oceanic-continental convergence · oceanic-oceanic convergence · continental collision · accretionary wedge
**Summary:** At convergent boundaries, one plate descends beneath another (subduction) or continents collide to build mountain ranges; both settings produce intense seismicity and volcanism.

#### Oceanic-Continental Convergence
When dense oceanic lithosphere converges with less dense continental lithosphere, the oceanic plate **subducts** beneath the continent. The descending slab carries water-saturated sediments and hydrated minerals into the mantle. As the slab sinks and warms, water is driven off (dehydration), lowering the melting point of the overlying mantle wedge. This produces silica-rich (andesitic to rhyolitic) magmas that rise to form a **volcanic arc** on the overriding continent — the Andes, the Cascades, Japan's volcanoes, and the Aleutians all formed this way. A deep **oceanic trench** forms where the slab bends downward (Mariana Trench, 11 km deep, is the world's deepest).

#### Oceanic-Oceanic Convergence
When two oceanic plates converge, the denser/older plate subducts. This produces **island arcs** — chains of volcanic islands parallel to the trench. The Lesser Antilles arc (Caribbean), the Aleutian Islands, and the Mariana Islands are examples. The trench-arc system has the same deep seismicity as continent-ocean convergence.

#### Continental Collision
When oceanic lithosphere between two continents is fully subducted, the continents eventually collide. Neither can subduct (both are too buoyant), so the collision zone compresses and thickens the crust, building massive mountain ranges. The **Himalayas and Tibetan Plateau** formed when India (moving northward after splitting from Gondwana ~130 Ma) collided with Asia ~50 Ma ago — collision continues today, raising the Himalayas ~5 mm/yr. The **Appalachians** and **European Alps** also record ancient and more recent continental collisions.

#### Accretionary Wedge and Mélange
As sediments on the subducting slab are scraped off against the overriding plate, they pile up in a deformed **accretionary wedge** (or accretionary prism). The jumbled, chaotically mixed rock produced in subduction zones is called **mélange** — diagnostic of ancient subduction zones preserved in mountain belts.

#### Review Questions
1. Why does subduction of oceanic crust produce arc volcanism, and what makes the magmas silica-rich?
2. Why can't continental crust subduct when two continents collide?
3. How do the Himalayas and the Appalachians differ in terms of their current tectonic activity, and why?

### Transform Boundaries and Hotspots
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** hs-ess-transform-hotspots
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** transform fault · strike-slip · San Andreas Fault · hotspot · mantle plume
**Summary:** Transform boundaries produce shallow earthquakes as plates slide horizontally, while hotspots create volcanic chains unrelated to plate boundaries.

#### Transform Faults and Their Seismicity
At **transform boundaries**, plates slide past each other horizontally with no creation or destruction of crust. The **San Andreas Fault** in California is a transform boundary between the Pacific Plate (moving northwest) and the North American Plate (moving southeast) — relative motion ~46 mm/yr. Transform faults produce shallow, often powerful earthquakes but no volcanism. The 1906 San Francisco earthquake (M~7.9) and 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake resulted from San Andreas system faults. Most oceanic transform faults connect offset segments of mid-ocean ridges — they form because ridges don't always grow in straight lines.

#### Hotspots: Mantle Plumes Beneath Plates
**Hotspots** are volcanic centers fed by **mantle plumes** — narrow upwellings of hot mantle material from deep within the Earth (possibly from the core-mantle boundary). Unlike plate boundary volcanoes, hotspots are stationary relative to the deep mantle while the tectonic plate above moves over them, creating a **chain of progressively older volcanic islands** in the direction of plate motion. The **Hawaiian-Emperor seamount chain** records the Pacific Plate moving over the Hawaiian hotspot: Hawaii (youngest, active) → Maui → Oahu (older, eroded) → extinct seamounts extending to the Aleutian Trench. The bend in the chain at ~43 Ma records a change in Pacific Plate motion direction.

#### Yellowstone Hotspot
The **Yellowstone hotspot** is a continental hotspot that has produced a series of supervolcanic calderas across the Snake River Plain. The hotspot has been stationary while the North American Plate moves southwest over it, leaving a track of caldera complexes. Yellowstone's magma system today feeds geysers (Old Faithful), hot springs, and ongoing seismicity. Its last supereruption was ~640,000 years ago.

#### Review Questions
1. Why does the San Andreas Fault produce earthquakes but not volcanoes?
2. How does the Hawaiian island chain serve as evidence for the direction and speed of Pacific Plate motion?
3. What distinguishes a hotspot volcano from a subduction zone volcano in terms of magma composition and tectonic setting?

### Plate Boundary Synthesis: Mapping Features to Processes
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** hs-ess-plate-boundary-synthesis
**Estimated time:** 10 min

**Summary:** Given a set of geological features, identify the plate boundary type and predict associated hazards and landforms.

#### Feature-to-Process Mapping
Given: a deep ocean trench (11 km), adjacent volcanic island arc, Benioff zone seismicity (earthquakes deepening away from the trench). → This is oceanic-oceanic convergence (subduction zone). Example: Mariana Trench / Mariana island arc.

Given: a symmetric pattern of magnetic anomaly stripes, central rift valley, submarine volcanic activity, no deep earthquakes. → This is a mid-ocean ridge (divergent boundary). Example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge.

Given: shallow right-lateral strike-slip earthquakes, no volcanism, linear fault scarp. → Transform boundary. Example: San Andreas Fault.

Given: extremely thick crust (~70 km), fold-and-thrust belt, no active volcanism, convergent GPS motion. → Continental collision. Example: Himalayas.

## Subtopic: Earthquakes — Causes, Measurement & Seismic Hazards

### What Causes Earthquakes: Elastic Rebound Theory
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hs-ess-earthquake-causes
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** elastic rebound theory · focus · epicenter · fault rupture · seismic waves
**Summary:** Earthquakes occur when stress accumulated in rock exceeds the frictional strength of a fault, releasing elastic strain energy as seismic waves.

#### Elastic Rebound Theory (Reid, 1906)
After studying the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, H.F. Reid proposed **elastic rebound theory**: rocks on either side of a fault accumulate elastic strain as tectonic forces move the plates — like stretching a rubber band. When the accumulated strain exceeds the frictional resistance of the fault, the fault ruptures suddenly, releasing elastic energy as **seismic waves**. The two sides snap to new positions, rebounding past their original unstressed state.

#### Focus and Epicenter
The **focus** (or hypocenter) is the point within the Earth where the earthquake rupture initiates. The **epicenter** is the point on Earth's surface directly above the focus. Shallow earthquakes (0–70 km depth) are most damaging because seismic waves don't attenuate as much before reaching the surface. Deep earthquakes (300–700 km) occur in subducting slabs — the cold, brittle slab material can still rupture at these depths.

#### Seismic Waves: P, S, and Surface
Earthquakes generate body waves (travel through Earth) and surface waves (travel along Earth's surface). **P-waves (Primary/Compressional):** fastest (~6–8 km/s in crust), travel through solids, liquids, and gases; compress and expand rock in the direction of propagation. **S-waves (Secondary/Shear):** slower (~3.5–4.5 km/s), travel only through solids; shake rock perpendicular to propagation. **Surface waves (Love and Rayleigh):** slowest but largest amplitude; cause most structural damage. The time difference between P and S wave arrivals at a seismic station is used to calculate the distance to the epicenter — three stations are needed to triangulate location.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain elastic rebound theory in your own words.
2. Why can't S-waves travel through Earth's liquid outer core?
3. If a seismic station receives P-waves 30 seconds before S-waves, and P-wave speed is 6 km/s and S-wave speed is 4 km/s, how far is the station from the epicenter?

### Measuring Earthquakes: Magnitude and Intensity Scales
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hs-ess-earthquake-measurement
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** Richter scale · moment magnitude · Modified Mercalli Intensity · logarithmic scale
**Summary:** Earthquake magnitude (an intrinsic property of the earthquake) and intensity (effects at a location) are measured by different scales with different purposes.

#### The Richter Scale (Local Magnitude, Mₗ)
Charles Richter (1935) developed a magnitude scale based on the maximum amplitude of seismic waves recorded on a specific seismograph (Wood-Anderson torsion seismograph) at a standard distance of 100 km. The scale is **logarithmic**: each integer increase represents a 10× increase in ground motion amplitude and roughly a 31.6× increase in energy released. A M6 earthquake releases ~31.6× more energy than a M5. The Richter scale works well for local California earthquakes but saturates for large earthquakes (M > 7) because it doesn't account for fault area and slip.

#### Moment Magnitude (Mw)
The **moment magnitude scale (Mw)** is now the standard for earthquake science. It is based on the **seismic moment**: M₀ = μ × A × D, where μ is rock rigidity, A is the fault rupture area, and D is average slip. This scale doesn't saturate and works for all earthquake sizes. The 2011 Tōhoku earthquake (Japan) was Mw 9.0; the 1960 Valdivia earthquake (Chile) was Mw 9.5 — the largest ever recorded. Each 1-unit increase in Mw = 10× ground amplitude = ~31.6× energy; 2 units = 1,000× energy.

#### Modified Mercalli Intensity Scale (MMI)
**Intensity** measures the effects of shaking at a specific location and varies with distance from the epicenter, local geology, and building type. The **Modified Mercalli Intensity (MMI) scale** runs from I (not felt) to XII (total destruction). Soft sediments (bay mud, river deposits) amplify shaking dramatically — a phenomenon called **site amplification**. The 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake preferentially damaged buildings on bay mud in San Francisco's Marina District, 100 km from the epicenter.

#### Review Questions
1. Why was the moment magnitude scale developed to replace the Richter scale for large earthquakes?
2. A M8 earthquake releases how many times more energy than a M6 earthquake?
3. What is the difference between magnitude and intensity, and why do they require different scales?

### Seismic Hazard Assessment: From Science to Engineering
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** hs-ess-seismic-hazard
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** probabilistic seismic hazard · liquefaction · tsunami generation · building codes
**Summary:** Seismic hazard assessment combines fault geology, historical seismicity, and soil data to inform building codes and emergency planning.

#### Probabilistic Seismic Hazard Analysis (PSHA)
PSHA calculates the probability of exceeding a given level of ground shaking at a site within a specified time period (e.g., 10% probability of exceedance in 50 years — the standard for building codes). It integrates: (1) identification of all faults and seismic sources within the region; (2) historical earthquake data (frequency-magnitude relationships, Gutenberg-Richter law: log N = a − bM); (3) attenuation relationships (how shaking decreases with distance); (4) site amplification factors. PSHA maps produced by the USGS show that the highest hazard in the U.S. is in coastal California, Pacific Northwest, and portions of the New Madrid Seismic Zone (central U.S.).

#### Liquefaction: When Soil Behaves Like a Liquid
**Liquefaction** occurs when saturated, loosely packed sand momentarily loses strength and behaves like a liquid during strong shaking. Water pressure in pore spaces spikes, reducing effective stress and shear strength. Buildings sink, tilt, or overturn; buried structures (pipes, tanks) float up. Liquefaction was catastrophic in the 2011 Christchurch, New Zealand earthquake (M6.2): neighborhoods built on former river channels saw homes sink and sewage systems float to the surface.

#### Tsunami Generation
Large submarine earthquakes (M > 7.5) involving significant vertical seafloor displacement (typical of subduction zone megathrust earthquakes) can generate **tsunamis**. The 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake (Mw 9.1, Sumatra-Andaman subduction zone) displaced the seafloor up to 10 m vertically, generating tsunamis with 30-m run-up heights that killed ~230,000 people across 14 countries. The 2011 Tōhoku tsunami (run-up to 40 m) overwhelmed seawalls and inundated Japan's Sendai coast within 30–40 minutes of the earthquake.

#### Review Questions
1. What is liquefaction, and what soil conditions favor it?
2. Why do large subduction zone earthquakes generate tsunamis more often than transform fault earthquakes of similar magnitude?
3. What is the Gutenberg-Richter relationship, and what does it predict about the relative frequency of small vs. large earthquakes?

### Earthquake Hazard Synthesis
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** hs-ess-earthquake-synthesis
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** seismicity pattern · hazard zone · mitigation · early warning
**Summary:** Connect earthquake science to practical hazard reduction, integrating knowledge of fault types, seismic waves, magnitude scales, and site effects.

#### Earthquake Early Warning Systems
**Earthquake early warning (EEW)** systems detect P-waves (which arrive first but cause little damage) and send alerts before the more destructive S-waves and surface waves arrive. The warning time ranges from seconds (nearby) to roughly 60 seconds (far away). Japan's J-Alert system and the USGS ShakeAlert system (West Coast U.S.) automatically stop trains, open fire station doors, and alert phones. Even a 10-second warning allows surgeons to pause, people to drop-cover-hold, and gas lines to shut.

## Subtopic: Volcanism & Magma Formation

### The Origin of Magma: Three Ways to Melt Rock
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hs-ess-magma-formation
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** decompression melting · flux melting · heat transfer melting · magma composition · Bowen's reaction series
**Summary:** Magma forms by three distinct mechanisms — decompression melting, flux melting, and heat transfer — each producing magmas of different compositions at different tectonic settings.

#### Why Rocks Melt
Rock melts when temperature exceeds the **solidus** — the temperature at which melt first appears at a given pressure and composition. Three mechanisms achieve melting in different tectonic settings:

1. **Decompression melting:** At mid-ocean ridges, upwelling asthenosphere rises without significant cooling. As pressure decreases, the solidus temperature drops faster than the rock's actual temperature, crossing into the melt field. No extra heat is needed — just reduced pressure. Produces **tholeiitic basalt** (mafic, low silica, MOR typical).

2. **Flux melting:** At subduction zones, the descending slab releases water and other volatiles (CO₂, fluorine) that lower the melting point of the overlying mantle wedge. Water acts as a "flux" — it breaks Si-O bonds in silicate minerals. Produces water-rich, silica-enriched (andesitic) magmas.

3. **Heat transfer melting:** At continental hotspots (Yellowstone), mantle plume heat conducts into overlying continental crust, partially melting it. Produces silica-rich rhyolitic magmas capable of supervolcanic eruptions.

#### Bowen's Reaction Series
As a basaltic magma cools, minerals crystallize in a predictable order described by **Bowen's Reaction Series** (Norman Bowen, 1920s). High-temperature minerals (olivine, pyroxene, Ca-plagioclase) crystallize first, removing Mg, Fe, and Ca from the melt. The remaining melt becomes progressively enriched in Si, Al, Na, K — evolving from mafic (basalt) toward intermediate (andesite) and ultimately felsic (rhyolite) composition through **fractional crystallization**. This is why subduction zone magmas are more silica-rich than mid-ocean ridge magmas, even when both start from mantle material.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between decompression melting and flux melting in terms of mechanism and tectonic setting?
2. How does Bowen's Reaction Series explain why arc volcanoes produce andesitic rather than basaltic magma?
3. Why is rhyolitic magma more viscous and explosive than basaltic magma?

### Volcanic Eruption Styles and Products
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hs-ess-eruption-styles
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** viscosity · explosive vs effusive · pyroclastic flow · VEI · volcanic hazards
**Summary:** Eruption style — from gentle lava flows to catastrophic explosions — is determined primarily by magma viscosity and volatile content.

#### Viscosity Controls Eruption Style
**Viscosity** (resistance to flow) in magma depends on: SiO₂ content (higher Si → more polymerization → higher viscosity); temperature (higher temp → lower viscosity); dissolved volatiles (water, CO₂). Basaltic magma (low Si, high temp): low viscosity → **effusive eruptions** — lava fountains and fast-moving lava flows (Hawaiian shield volcanoes). Rhyolitic magma (high Si, lower temp, volatile-rich): high viscosity → **explosive eruptions** — gases can't escape gradually; pressure builds until catastrophic fragmentation (Plinian eruptions, supervolcanoes).

#### Pyroclastic Material
Explosive eruptions fragment magma into **pyroclastic material**: ash (< 2 mm), lapilli (2–64 mm), and volcanic bombs (> 64 mm). **Pyroclastic flows** (also called pyroclastic density currents) are fast-moving (100–700 km/hr), hot (300–800°C), turbulent avalanches of gas and pyroclasts — the most dangerous volcanic phenomenon. The 79 CE eruption of Vesuvius killed Pompeii's residents by pyroclastic surges reaching ~300°C. The 1980 Mount St. Helens eruption lateral blast was a pyroclastic density current.

#### Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI)
The **VEI** scale (0–8) rates eruption size by volume of ejecta and eruption column height. Each unit represents a 10× increase in ejecta volume. VEI 8 = "super-eruption" (≥ 1,000 km³ tephra); examples: Toba (~74,000 years ago, VEI 8, nearly drove humans to extinction with a volcanic winter), Yellowstone Huckleberry Ridge (~2.1 Ma, VEI 8). For comparison, the 1980 Mount St. Helens eruption was VEI 5; the 1991 Pinatubo (Philippines) was VEI 6 and cooled global temperatures by ~0.5°C for 1–2 years.

#### Review Questions
1. Why do basaltic eruptions tend to be effusive while rhyolitic eruptions tend to be explosive?
2. What is a pyroclastic flow, and why is it more deadly than a lava flow?
3. The 1991 Pinatubo eruption cooled global temperatures. What mechanism caused this, and what aerosol was responsible?

### Volcanic Landforms and Their Origins
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** hs-ess-volcanic-landforms
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** shield volcano · stratovolcano · caldera · cinder cone · flood basalt
**Summary:** Different magma compositions and eruption styles produce distinctive volcanic landforms, from gently sloping shields to steep composite cones to vast lava plateaus.

#### Shield Volcanoes
**Shield volcanoes** are built from successive basaltic lava flows that spread widely, producing broad, gently sloping edifices (resembling a warrior's shield lying flat). They form over hotspots (Hawaii's Mauna Loa, tallest volcano on Earth from base — 9 km from seafloor to summit) and at some MOR settings. Eruptions are frequent, effusive, and non-explosive. Mauna Loa has erupted 33 times since 1843 and is expected to erupt again within years to decades.

#### Stratovolcanoes (Composite Volcanoes)
**Stratovolcanoes** are built from alternating layers of lava flows and pyroclastic deposits, producing steep-sided, symmetric cones. They form at subduction zones (Fuji, Rainier, Hood, St. Helens, Pinatubo, Vesuvius). More silica-rich, viscous magmas build these steep structures. They erupt less frequently than shield volcanoes but far more explosively and dangerously.

#### Calderas
A **caldera** is a large, roughly circular depression formed when a volcano's magma chamber empties (either by eruption or subsidence) and the overlying edifice collapses inward. Calderas range from a few km (Crater Lake, Oregon — formed ~7,700 years ago when Mt. Mazama collapsed after a VEI 7 eruption) to >100 km (Yellowstone caldera). They are distinct from craters (small summit depressions formed by eruption, not collapse).

#### Large Igneous Provinces (LIPs) and Flood Basalts
**Large igneous provinces** are massive accumulations of igneous rocks formed by extraordinary mantle melting events. The **Deccan Traps** (India, ~66 Ma) produced up to 2 million km³ of basalt over ~1 million years — likely contributing to the K-Pg extinction. The **Columbia River Basalts** (~17–15 Ma, Pacific Northwest) cover ~210,000 km² to depths of 1–4 km. These are not associated with normal plate boundaries or hotspots but with deep-seated mantle anomalies.

#### Review Questions
1. What determines whether a volcanic edifice becomes a shield volcano or a stratovolcano?
2. How does a caldera form, and how does it differ from a volcanic crater?
3. Why are Large Igneous Provinces (flood basalts) associated with mass extinctions?

### Volcanic Hazards and Monitoring
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** hs-ess-volcanic-hazards
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** volcanic monitoring · hazard zones · lahar · volcanic gas
**Summary:** Modern volcanic monitoring integrates seismology, GPS deformation, gas chemistry, and satellite imagery to forecast eruptions and mitigate hazards.

#### Primary Volcanic Hazards
1. **Lava flows:** slow-moving (usually hours to avoid), destroy property, very rarely kill people (2018 Kīlauea lower East Rift Zone eruption consumed ~700 homes in Leilani Estates).
2. **Pyroclastic flows/surges:** most deadly; no escape once one begins.
3. **Lahars (volcanic mudflows):** hot or cold debris flows mixing volcanic material with water (from crater lakes, ice melt, or rainfall) rushing down river valleys. The 1985 Nevado del Ruiz lahar (Colombia) buried the town of Armero, killing ~23,000 people.
4. **Volcanic gases:** SO₂ produces acid rain and sulfate aerosols; CO₂ accumulates in low-lying areas (1986 Lake Nyos CO₂ outburst killed ~1,700 in Cameroon); H₂S is toxic.
5. **Volcanic ash:** disrupts aviation (ash damages jet engines), collapses roofs, contaminates water.

#### Monitoring Tools
- **Seismology:** harmonic tremor (magma movement) and swarm earthquakes signal pre-eruptive activity
- **GPS/InSAR:** ground deformation (uplift/inflation) indicates magma intrusion
- **Gas monitoring:** SO₂ flux measured by DOAS; CO₂ ratio changes signal deeper magma
- **Thermal imaging:** satellite and aerial surveys detect new fumaroles and dome growth

#### Review Questions
1. What is a lahar, and why can it be deadly even at great distances from the eruption site?
2. What three monitoring signals typically precede a volcanic eruption?
3. Why does volcanic SO₂ injection into the stratosphere cause short-term global cooling?

## Subtopic: Mountain Building & Crustal Deformation

### How Mountains Form: Orogeny and Crustal Thickening
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hs-ess-orogeny
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** orogeny · isostasy · fold-and-thrust belt · crustal thickening · root
**Summary:** Mountain ranges form through compressional tectonics that thicken continental crust; isostasy then controls how high mountains rise and how quickly they erode.

#### Orogenic Belts and Their Origins
An **orogeny** is an episode of mountain building caused by horizontal compressive forces. Modern orogenic belts form at: (1) Continent-continent collision zones (Himalayas, Alps, Appalachians — each represents a different collision age); (2) Active continental margins with subduction (Andes — volcanic arc + fold-and-thrust belt). Ancient orogenic belts are preserved as **cratons** (stable continental cores) after erosion reduces the mountains to roots. The Appalachians were once as high as the Himalayas; 400 million years of erosion reduced them to modest heights.

#### Isostasy: Mountains Float on the Mantle
The crust floats on the denser mantle according to the principle of **isostasy** — just as an iceberg floats with most of its mass below water, mountain ranges have deep **crustal roots** extending into the mantle. The Himalayas have a root ~70 km deep (normal crust is 30–35 km). As erosion removes material from the surface, the mountain range isostatically rises (like a boat rising as cargo is removed). This is why even heavily eroded ancient mountain belts can have exposures of deep crustal rocks (granulites, high-pressure metamorphic minerals) that were once 30+ km below the surface.

#### Fold-and-Thrust Belts
In compressional settings, sedimentary strata are shortened and thickened by **thrust faults** (reverse faults with shallow dip) and **folds** (anticlines and synclines). The **Valley and Ridge Province** of the Appalachians exposes eroded fold-and-thrust belt structures: ridges of resistant sandstone and limestone formed the crests of anticlines or thrust sheets; valleys developed in weaker shales. The Canadian Rockies, Swiss Alps, and Zagros Mountains (Iran) all show classic fold-and-thrust geometry.

2. Why does erosion cause a mountain range to rise, rather than fall?

### Deformation Structures: Reading Strain in Rock
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** hs-ess-deformation-structures
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** folds · faults · stress vs. strain · brittle vs. ductile deformation
**Summary:** Rocks deform by folding or faulting depending on temperature, pressure, strain rate, and rock type; reading these structures allows reconstruction of past tectonic stresses.

#### Stress and Strain in Rocks
**Stress** is force per unit area applied to a rock; **strain** is the resulting deformation. Three types: compressional stress (squeezes rock, shortens and thickens), tensional stress (pulls rock apart, thins), and shear stress (slides one block past another). Rock response depends on conditions: cold, near-surface rock deforms **brittlely** (cracks, fault); hot, deep rock deforms **ductilely** (flows, folds without breaking). The same quartzite that faults at the surface will flow plastically at 20+ km depth and 400°C+.

#### Fault Classification
Faults are classified by the direction of relative movement: **Normal fault** — hanging wall drops relative to footwall (extensional setting, rift zones, Basin and Range Province); **Reverse fault** — hanging wall rises (compressional, thrust belts; when dip < 45°, called a thrust fault); **Strike-slip fault** — horizontal motion, no vertical component (transform boundaries). A **thrust fault** is a low-angle reverse fault; the Heart Mountain Thrust (Wyoming) moved ~800 km³ of rock 50 km horizontally at ~2° dip — an extraordinary feature.

#### Folds and Their Classification
Folds result from ductile deformation: **anticlines** arch upward (oldest rocks in core); **synclines** dip downward (youngest rocks in core). **Overturned folds** have one limb tilted past vertical. **Nappe structures** (recumbent folds) are characteristic of Alpine collision zones where entire fold sequences were overturned and transported tens of kilometers. **Monoclines** are step-like folds (one limb dips, others are flat) visible on the Colorado Plateau.

### Metamorphism: Deformation's Thermal Companion
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** hs-ess-metamorphism-mountain-building
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** metamorphism · P-T path · index minerals · contact vs. regional metamorphism
**Summary:** Mountain building drives regional metamorphism as rocks are buried, heated, and deformed; index minerals record the temperature-pressure conditions of metamorphism.

#### Regional vs. Contact Metamorphism
**Regional metamorphism** affects large volumes of rock during mountain building — burial under thick thrust sheets increases temperature and pressure, recrystallizing minerals without melting. **Contact metamorphism** occurs locally around igneous intrusions — heat from magma bakes surrounding rock into a **contact aureole**. Regional metamorphism is associated with orogenic belts; contact metamorphism with any magmatic body regardless of tectonic setting.

#### Metamorphic Grade and Index Minerals
As burial depth and temperature increase through a metamorphic terrane, Barrovian zones (defined by Scottish geologist George Barrow) appear in sequence: chlorite → biotite → garnet → staurolite → kyanite → sillimanite. Each **index mineral** first appears at a specific P-T threshold. The **garnet isograd** marks where garnet first forms; rock above (lower grade) contains biotite but no garnet; rock below (higher grade) contains garnet and higher-grade minerals. Kyanite forms under high pressure (20+ km depth); sillimanite at high temperature; andalusite at low pressure.
