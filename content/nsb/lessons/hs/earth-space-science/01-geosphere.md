## Subtopic: Earth's Interior Structure (crust, mantle, core)

### Layers of the Earth: A Journey to the Center
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** layers-of-the-earth-interior
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** crust · mantle · core · Mohorovičić discontinuity
**Summary:** Understand Earth's four main layers—crust, mantle, outer core, and inner core—their compositions, thicknesses, and the boundaries that define them.

#### What We Know and How We Know It
Earth's interior cannot be sampled directly beyond ~12 km (the Kola Superdeep Borehole, drilled in Russia, is the deepest human excavation). Almost everything we know about Earth's deep interior comes from seismic wave analysis—essentially using earthquakes as X-rays of the planet. Changes in wave speed and direction reveal boundaries between layers of different density and composition.

#### The Crust
Earth has two types of crust. Oceanic crust is ~7 km thick, composed mainly of basalt (mafic rock, rich in magnesium and iron), and has a density of ~3.0 g/cm³. Continental crust is ~35–70 km thick, composed mainly of granite (felsic rock, rich in silicon and aluminum—SIAL), and has a density of ~2.7 g/cm³. The boundary between crust and mantle is called the **Mohorovičić discontinuity** (Moho), named for Croatian seismologist Andrija Mohorovičić who discovered it in 1909 from seismic data.

#### The Mantle
The mantle extends from the Moho to ~2,900 km depth and makes up ~84% of Earth's volume. It is composed of peridotite (ultramafic rock, very high in iron and magnesium). The upper mantle includes a partially molten layer called the **asthenosphere** (~100–350 km depth), which flows plastically and enables plate motion. Above it, the rigid lithosphere (crust + uppermost mantle) rides on the asthenosphere like a sled on slush.

#### The Core
The outer core (2,900–5,150 km) is liquid iron-nickel alloy. Its convective motion generates Earth's magnetic field via the geodynamo. The inner core (5,150–6,371 km) is solid iron-nickel, kept solid by immense pressure despite temperatures of ~5,000–6,000°C. The boundary between them is called the **Lehmann discontinuity**, discovered by Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann in 1936.

#### Key Discontinuities and Densities
- Moho: crust–mantle boundary, P-wave speed jumps from ~6 to ~8 km/s
- Gutenberg discontinuity: mantle–outer core at 2,900 km; S-waves stop here (liquid)
- Lehmann discontinuity: outer–inner core at 5,150 km
- Average densities: crust 2.7–3.0, mantle 3.3–5.6, outer core ~10, inner core ~13 g/cm³

#### Review Questions
1. Why does the inner core remain solid despite being hotter than the outer core?
2. What is the Mohorovičić discontinuity and how was it discovered?
3. How does the asthenosphere differ from the lithosphere in terms of behavior, and why does this matter for plate tectonics?

### Pressure, Temperature, and Density with Depth
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** pressure-temperature-density-with-depth
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** geothermal gradient · pressure increase · density stratification
**Summary:** Trace how pressure, temperature, and density each change as you move from Earth's surface to its center, and understand why each profile has the shape it does.

#### The Geothermal Gradient
Temperature increases with depth at a rate called the **geothermal gradient**, averaging ~25–30°C per kilometer in the crust. In the mantle this rate decreases; at the core-mantle boundary temperatures reach ~3,700°C. The inner core is ~5,000–6,000°C—hotter than the Sun's surface—sustained by residual heat from Earth's formation and radioactive decay of uranium, thorium, and potassium-40.

#### Pressure with Depth
Pressure increases with depth because of the weight of overlying rock. At the Moho (~35 km continental), pressure is ~1 GPa (≈10,000 atmospheres). At the core-mantle boundary, pressure reaches ~136 GPa. At Earth's center: ~360 GPa. This extreme pressure is why the inner core is solid despite being extremely hot—high pressure raises the melting point of iron above the actual temperature there.

#### Density Stratification
Earth is density-stratified: heavier materials (iron, nickel) sank to the center during the **magma ocean** phase early in Earth history (the process is called **differentiation**). This is why the core is iron-nickel while the crust is silicon-aluminum-rich. The average density of Earth (~5.5 g/cm³) is much higher than surface rocks (~2.7 g/cm³), confirming a dense interior.

#### Evidence from Meteorites
We can study the compositions of iron meteorites (samples of differentiated asteroid cores) and stony-iron meteorites (mantle analogues) to infer what Earth's deep interior is like—since many meteorites are fragments of rocky bodies that formed from the same solar nebula as Earth.

#### Why This Matters for Science Bowl
Bowl questions often ask about specific values: inner core temperature (~5,000°C), mantle density range, Gutenberg discontinuity depth (2,900 km), and which discontinuity S-waves cannot cross (Gutenberg—because the outer core is liquid). Memorize these numbers.

#### Review Questions
1. Why is Earth's inner core solid while the outer core is liquid, even though the inner core is hotter?
2. What process caused Earth to become density-stratified early in its history?
3. What evidence do we use to infer the composition of Earth's core, given we cannot sample it?

### Reading Earth's Interior with Seismic Waves
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** seismic-waves-earth-interior
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** P-waves · S-waves · shadow zones · seismic tomography
**Summary:** Learn how P-waves and S-waves travel through Earth, what their behavior reveals about interior layers, and how shadow zones proved the existence of the liquid outer core.

#### P-Waves and S-Waves
**Primary waves (P-waves)** are compressional—particles move parallel to wave travel. They travel through solids, liquids, and gases, and are faster (~6 km/s in crust, ~8 km/s in upper mantle, up to ~13 km/s in inner core). **Secondary waves (S-waves)** are shear—particles move perpendicular to travel direction. They travel only through solids; they cannot move through liquids because liquids have no shear strength. S-waves travel at roughly 60% of P-wave speed.

#### Refraction and Reflection at Boundaries
When seismic waves cross a boundary between materials of different density (like mantle to core), they refract—bend—following Snell's Law. Waves also partially reflect at each boundary. By measuring arrival times at seismograph stations worldwide, geophysicists can calculate the depth and properties of each boundary.

#### Shadow Zones: Proving the Liquid Core
The **P-wave shadow zone** appears 103°–142° from an earthquake epicenter—a band where no direct P-waves arrive because they are refracted by the core. The **S-wave shadow zone** covers everything beyond ~103°, because S-waves cannot penetrate the liquid outer core at all. Inge Lehmann discovered the inner core in 1936 by noticing faint P-wave arrivals inside the P-wave shadow zone—explained by waves bouncing off the solid inner core.

#### Seismic Tomography
Modern seismic tomography uses thousands of seismometers and millions of earthquake records to create 3D images of Earth's interior—analogous to a medical CT scan. It reveals cold, dense subducting slabs sinking into the mantle and hot, buoyant plumes rising from near the core-mantle boundary (some reaching all the way to the surface as hot spots, like Hawaii).

#### Science Bowl Clue
Questions about shadow zones almost always expect you to name which wave type is absent and why. S-waves = absent beyond 103° because liquid outer core. P-waves = bent around core, creating shadow between 103° and 142°.

#### Review Questions
1. Why can S-waves not travel through the outer core?
2. What is the P-wave shadow zone, and what does its existence tell us about Earth's interior?
3. How does seismic tomography differ from traditional seismology, and what can it reveal?

### The Crust Beneath Our Feet: Continental vs. Oceanic
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** continental-vs-oceanic-crust-comparison
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** isostasy · felsic vs. mafic · crustal thickness variation
**Summary:** Apply your understanding of crustal composition and density to explain why mountains have deep roots, why oceanic crust is younger, and how isostasy governs crustal elevation.

#### Why Mountains Float
**Isostasy** is the principle that Earth's crust floats on the denser mantle like icebergs floating in water. Mountain ranges have deep crustal roots—the Himalayas have roots extending ~70 km into the mantle. Just as an iceberg has most of its mass below water, mountain ranges have most of their "extra" mass underground. When ice sheets melt (deglaciation), the crust rebounds upward as the load is removed—a process called **isostatic rebound** (still measurable in Scandinavia today at ~1 cm/year).

#### Why Oceanic Crust Is Always Young
The oldest oceanic crust is only ~200 million years old (found in the western Pacific), while continental crust can be up to 4 billion years old. This is because oceanic crust is constantly created at mid-ocean ridges and destroyed at subduction zones. The Atlantic Ocean's oldest crust is near the continental margins; the Mid-Atlantic Ridge is creating new crust today at ~2.5 cm/year per side.

#### The SIAL-SIMA Model
An older but still useful model divides Earth's outer layers into SIAL (Silicon + Aluminum = continental crust) and SIMA (Silicon + Magnesium = oceanic crust and upper mantle). Oceanic crust is denser (~3.0 g/cm³) than continental crust (~2.7 g/cm³), which is why it subducts beneath continental crust at convergent boundaries.

#### Geologic Implications
The density difference between continental and oceanic crust explains why: (1) the ocean floor is lower elevation than continents, (2) oceanic crust subducts rather than colliding when it meets continental crust, and (3) continental crust persists for billions of years while oceanic crust is recycled.

#### Review Questions
1. Why is oceanic crust always younger than continental crust?
2. Explain isostasy using the analogy of an iceberg, and give one real-world example of isostatic rebound.
3. Which type of crust would subduct if oceanic and continental crust collided? Why?

### Mapping Earth's Interior: From Seismographs to 3D Models
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** mapping-earths-interior-methods
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** travel-time curves · mantle plumes · core-mantle boundary
**Summary:** Work through how geophysicists use seismic travel-time data to pinpoint discontinuities, and explore what seismic tomography reveals about mantle convection and hot spots.

#### Travel-Time Curves
A travel-time curve plots wave arrival time vs. angular distance from the earthquake. P-waves arrive before S-waves; both arrive earlier at stations close to the earthquake. Kinks in the travel-time curve indicate refraction at a boundary. The slope of the curve gives wave speed at each depth. By inverting thousands of travel-time curves from global earthquakes, geophysicists have built detailed velocity models of Earth's interior (e.g., PREM: Preliminary Reference Earth Model, published 1981).

#### The Core-Mantle Boundary (CMB)
The CMB at 2,900 km is one of the most dramatic boundaries in Earth. The temperature jumps ~1,000°C across it. Chemical reactions here produce a region called D'' (D-double-prime) just above the CMB, which appears anomalously slow for seismic waves and may be piles of ancient, dense oceanic crust that sank to the bottom of the mantle over billions of years. The CMB is where mantle plumes originate—rising columns of unusually hot rock that punch through the mantle and reach the surface as hot spots (Hawaii, Yellowstone, Iceland).

#### Mantle Convection Revealed by Tomography
Seismic tomography shows cold, fast-velocity regions (subducted slabs sinking through the mantle) and hot, slow-velocity regions (rising plumes). Some slabs appear to pile up at the 660 km transition zone before eventually sinking into the lower mantle. This pattern reveals whole-mantle convection driving plate tectonics and refreshing Earth's surface over geologic time.

#### Practical Calculation: Depth from Travel Time
If a P-wave takes 13.5 minutes to travel from an earthquake to a station 100° away, and we know average P-wave speed is ~10 km/s through the mantle, we can estimate path length and infer depth. Real calculations use velocity models, but the principle is: Δt = Δd / v, where v varies with depth.

#### Review Questions
1. What is a travel-time curve, and what does a kink in it indicate?
2. Where do mantle plumes originate, and what is the evidence for their existence?
3. What does seismic tomography reveal about the fate of subducted oceanic plates?

### Cross-Concept: Interior Structure + Plate Tectonics + Magnetism
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** interior-structure-tectonics-magnetism-synthesis
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** geodynamo · convection cells · lithosphere-asthenosphere system
**Summary:** Connect Earth's interior structure to plate motion and the magnetic field, showing how the deep Earth drives surface processes.

#### The Geodynamo
Earth's magnetic field is generated by convective motion of liquid iron in the outer core—the **geodynamo**. As the liquid iron flows (driven by heat from the inner core and chemical buoyancy as the inner core slowly solidifies), it generates electric currents, which generate a magnetic field. Without the liquid outer core, Earth would have no magnetic field and would be exposed to lethal solar wind particles. Mars lost its magnetic field ~4 billion years ago when its smaller core solidified, and today its surface is bombarded by radiation.

#### Mantle Convection Drives Plates
Heat from the core warms the base of the mantle. Hot mantle rock rises (it's less dense when hot), spreads laterally, cools, and sinks at subduction zones—a convection cell. This is the engine of plate tectonics. The lithosphere (rigid plates) is carried along by mantle convection below. Mid-ocean ridges form where convection cells diverge; trenches form where they converge.

#### Magnetic Reversals and Ocean Floor Striping
Earth's magnetic field periodically reverses polarity (north becomes south) at irregular intervals averaging ~200,000–300,000 years. New oceanic crust records the field direction when it solidifies at mid-ocean ridges. The symmetric striped pattern of normal/reversed magnetism on either side of ridges (discovered in the 1960s by Vine and Matthews) was key evidence for seafloor spreading and plate tectonics.

#### The Lithosphere-Asthenosphere Boundary (LAB)
The LAB separates the rigid lithosphere from the flowing asthenosphere. Its depth varies: ~70 km under ocean basins (thin, young crust) to ~200 km under old cratons (ancient, stable continental interiors). The LAB is where seismic velocities drop sharply—the Low Velocity Zone—caused by partial melting.

#### Review Questions
1. How does the liquid outer core generate Earth's magnetic field?
2. How do magnetic anomaly stripes on the ocean floor support the theory of seafloor spreading?
3. Why is the lithosphere-asthenosphere boundary deeper under old cratons than under young oceanic crust?

### How Earthquakes Happen: Faults, Stress, and Seismic Energy
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** how-earthquakes-happen-faults-stress
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** elastic rebound theory · fault types · focus vs. epicenter
**Summary:** Understand the mechanism of earthquakes through elastic rebound theory, the geometry of different fault types, and how energy radiates from the focus.

#### Elastic Rebound Theory
**Elastic rebound theory**, developed by Harry Reid after the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, explains earthquakes as the sudden release of stored elastic strain energy. Tectonic plates move continuously, but friction locks faults together. Stress accumulates in rocks on either side of the fault (they deform elastically, like a stretched rubber band). When stress exceeds friction, the fault ruptures—rocks snap back to their unstrained positions, releasing energy as seismic waves. The point of initial rupture is the **focus (hypocenter)**; the point on the surface directly above is the **epicenter**.

#### Types of Faults
- **Normal fault**: hanging wall drops relative to footwall; caused by tensional stress (divergent boundaries). Example: Basin and Range Province, USA.
- **Reverse fault**: hanging wall rises relative to footwall; caused by compressional stress (convergent boundaries). A low-angle reverse fault is a **thrust fault**. Example: Himalayas.
- **Strike-slip fault**: blocks move horizontally past each other; caused by shear stress (transform boundaries). Example: San Andreas Fault. Left-lateral vs. right-lateral (determined by which direction the opposite block appears to move when you stand on one side).

#### Depth Categories
- Shallow focus: 0–70 km (most destructive; 75% of earthquake energy)
- Intermediate focus: 70–300 km
- Deep focus: 300–700 km (occur in subducting slabs; follow the Wadati-Benioff zone into the mantle)

#### The Wadati-Benioff Zone
Deep earthquakes cluster along the **Wadati-Benioff zone**—the inclined plane of a subducting plate descending into the mantle. They trace the slab's path to depths of ~700 km, below which the slab becomes too warm and plastic to generate brittle failure.

2. What type of fault is the San Andreas, and what stress regime creates it?

### Measuring Earthquakes: Scales, Waves, and Locating the Epicenter
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** measuring-earthquakes-scales-waves
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** Richter scale · moment magnitude · P-S time difference · triangulation
**Summary:** Learn how seismologists measure earthquake size using magnitude scales and locate the epicenter using the arrival time difference between P and S waves.

#### Magnitude Scales
The original **Richter scale** (1935, Charles Richter) measures local magnitude from maximum amplitude on a Wood-Anderson seismometer at 100 km distance. It is logarithmic: each step is 10× amplitude and ~31.6× energy. The **moment magnitude scale (Mw)** is now preferred for large earthquakes because Richter saturates above ~Mw 7. Mw is calculated from seismic moment = shear modulus × fault area × average displacement. The 2011 Tōhoku earthquake was Mw 9.0; the 1960 Valdivia earthquake (largest ever recorded) was Mw 9.5.

#### Intensity vs. Magnitude
**Magnitude** measures energy released at the source (one number per earthquake). **Intensity** measures shaking at a specific location (varies with distance, geology). The **Modified Mercalli Intensity (MMI) scale** runs I–XII and describes observed effects (I = not felt; XII = total destruction). Soft sediments amplify shaking relative to bedrock (amplification effect, as seen in Mexico City during the 1985 earthquake).

#### Locating the Epicenter: S-P Method
P-waves travel faster than S-waves. The difference in arrival times (t_S − t_P) increases with distance from the epicenter. Using the formula: Distance (km) ≈ 8 km/s × (t_S − t_P in seconds) [approximate], you can calculate distance to the epicenter from one station. Triangulation using three or more stations pinpoints the epicenter: draw circles of calculated radius from each station; their intersection is the epicenter.

#### Seismograph Anatomy
A seismograph records ground motion using a heavy mass (inertia keeps it still) and a reference frame that moves with the ground. Modern broadband seismometers can detect earthquakes globally and measure frequencies from 0.001 Hz to 50 Hz. The Global Seismographic Network (GSN) has ~150 stations worldwide.

#### Science Bowl Clue
"What scale is used to measure earthquake magnitude today?" → Moment magnitude scale (Mw), not Richter. "What is the relationship between magnitude steps and energy?" → 10× amplitude, ~31.6× energy per step.

#### Review Questions
1. Why has the moment magnitude scale largely replaced the Richter scale for large earthquakes?
2. Describe the S-P method for locating an earthquake epicenter. Why do you need at least three stations?
3. An earthquake registers MMI IX in one city but MMI VI 100 km away on bedrock, and MMI VIII 80 km away on soft sediment. Explain these observations.

### Seismic Wave Types: Surface Waves, Body Waves, and Destruction
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** seismic-wave-types-surface-body
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** Love waves · Rayleigh waves · body vs. surface waves · ground motion patterns
**Summary:** Distinguish the four types of seismic waves, understand their motion, speed, and why surface waves cause most earthquake damage.

#### Body Waves
Body waves travel through Earth's interior. **P-waves (primary/compressional)**: particles oscillate parallel to propagation; fastest (~6–13 km/s); travel through solids and liquids; felt as a sudden jolt. **S-waves (secondary/shear)**: particles oscillate perpendicular to propagation; slower (~60% of P-wave speed); travel only through solids; felt as a rolling motion.

#### Surface Waves
Surface waves travel along Earth's surface and are slower but more destructive than body waves. **Love waves**: horizontal shear motion perpendicular to propagation direction; fastest surface wave; causes side-to-side shaking (very destructive to buildings). **Rayleigh waves**: elliptical retrograde motion (like ocean waves but in the ground); causes rolling, up-and-down + back-and-forth motion; the most destructive of all seismic waves. Love waves were described by A.E.H. Love (1911); Rayleigh waves by Lord Rayleigh (1887).

#### Arrival Order at a Seismometer
P → S → Love → Rayleigh. Each wave type leaves a distinct signature on a seismogram. The S-P time gap (time between P and S arrivals) is used to calculate distance to the epicenter.

#### Why Surface Waves Are Most Destructive
Surface waves have larger amplitudes than body waves at the surface (energy is concentrated in the shallow rock, not spreading through the whole Earth volume). They also have longer periods (slower frequency), which resonates better with building natural frequencies. Most building collapse in earthquakes is due to Rayleigh and Love waves, not P or S waves.

#### Review Questions
1. Name all four seismic wave types in order of arrival at a distant seismometer.
2. Why can't S-waves travel through the outer core, but P-waves can (partially)?
3. Which seismic wave type causes the most structural damage, and why?

### Earthquake Hazards: Liquefaction, Tsunamis, and Secondary Effects
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** earthquake-hazards-liquefaction-tsunamis
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** liquefaction · tsunami generation · aftershocks · ground amplification
**Summary:** Apply earthquake science to understand the secondary hazards—liquefaction, tsunamis, fire, landslides—that often cause more casualties than the shaking itself.

#### Liquefaction
**Liquefaction** occurs when saturated, loosely-packed sediment (sand, silt) is shaken by seismic waves and temporarily behaves like a liquid. Water pressure between grains increases until grains lose contact and friction drops to near zero. Buildings sink, tilt, or topple; buried objects (pipes, tanks) float upward. Severely affected areas: Marina District, San Francisco (1989); Christchurch, New Zealand (2011). Liquefaction risk is highest in reclaimed land, river deltas, and coastal areas built on unconsolidated sediment.

#### Tsunamis
Tsunamis are generated when seafloor displacement (from a thrust earthquake, submarine landslide, or volcanic collapse) suddenly displaces the water column above. They travel at ~800 km/h in deep ocean (same as a jet airplane) with wavelengths of ~200 km and amplitudes of only ~1 m—barely noticeable at sea. As they enter shallow water, they slow down dramatically, but wave height increases (**shoaling**): the 2011 Tōhoku tsunami reached heights of ~40 m inland. Minimum earthquake size to generate a significant tsunami: ~Mw 7.5.

#### Aftershocks and the Omori Law
Aftershocks follow the mainshock in decreasing frequency over time, described by **Omori's Law**: rate of aftershocks ∝ 1/(time after mainshock). The largest aftershock is typically ~1.2 magnitude units smaller than the mainshock. Aftershocks can collapse structures weakened by the mainshock (e.g., 2010 Haiti earthquake aftershocks increased casualties significantly).

#### Secondary Hazards
Fire (1906 San Francisco: most damage from post-earthquake fires), landslides (2008 Wenchuan earthquake, China: triggered >15,000 landslides), disease from contaminated water (common after developing-world events). Ground deformation can rupture gas lines and water mains, enabling fires while preventing firefighting.

### Interpreting a Seismogram: From Trace to Earthquake Parameters
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** interpreting-seismograms
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** S-P interval · magnitude from amplitude · three-station triangulation
**Summary:** Work through the steps of reading a real seismogram to determine earthquake distance, and use triangulation from three stations to locate the epicenter.

#### Reading Wave Arrivals
A seismogram shows ground displacement vs. time. The first arrival is P-waves (sharp, high-frequency onset), followed by S-waves (larger amplitude, lower frequency), then surface waves (largest amplitude, lowest frequency, longest duration). Mark t_P (P-wave arrival time) and t_S (S-wave arrival time) from the seismogram.

#### Calculating Distance: The S-P Method
P-waves travel at ~6 km/s in continental crust; S-waves at ~3.5 km/s. If the S-P time difference = Δt seconds, distance D = Δt / (1/v_S − 1/v_P) = Δt / (1/3.5 − 1/6) ≈ Δt × 8 km/s. Example: Δt = 50 s → D ≈ 400 km. (This is approximate; real calculations use velocity models varying with depth.)

#### Triangulation
Using three seismograph stations, draw a circle of radius D around each station. Where all three circles intersect = epicenter. If circles don't intersect cleanly (due to measurement error), the intersection zone gives uncertainty in epicenter location. Four or more stations reduce uncertainty. Modern computers solve for optimal epicenter location simultaneously from all available stations globally.

#### Estimating Magnitude from Seismogram
Richter magnitude is calculated from: ML = log₁₀(A) − log₁₀(A₀(Δ)), where A = maximum wave amplitude (mm on seismogram), A₀(Δ) = correction for distance Δ. A nomogram (chart) with two scales (S-P time and maximum amplitude) lets you find magnitude graphically. A 10× increase in amplitude = increase of 1 magnitude unit.

1. On a seismogram, the S-P time interval is 30 seconds. Estimate the distance to the earthquake epicenter (use approximate formula D ≈ 8 × Δt).

## Subtopic: Plate Tectonics Overview

### The Theory of Plate Tectonics: History and Evidence
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** plate-tectonics-history-evidence
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** continental drift · seafloor spreading · Vine-Matthews hypothesis · plate boundary types
**Summary:** Trace the development of plate tectonics from Wegener's continental drift hypothesis through seafloor spreading to the modern theory, and master the key lines of evidence.

#### Alfred Wegener and Continental Drift (1912)
Alfred Wegener proposed **continental drift** in 1912, arguing that continents had once been joined in a supercontinent he called **Pangaea** (meaning "all lands"). His evidence included: (1) the fit of South America and Africa's coastlines; (2) identical fossil species on now-separated continents (e.g., *Glossopteris* fern fossils found in South America, Africa, India, Australia, and Antarctica—all fragments of the southern supercontinent **Gondwana**); (3) glacial striations in tropical Africa indicating past polar location; (4) matching rock sequences across the Atlantic. Wegener could not explain *how* continents moved, and his idea was largely rejected by the geological establishment until the 1960s.

#### Seafloor Spreading (Hess, 1962)
Harry Hess proposed **seafloor spreading** in 1962: new oceanic crust is created at mid-ocean ridges (divergent boundaries) and old crust is destroyed at subduction zones. This provided the mechanism Wegener lacked. Evidence: mid-ocean ridges are the most extensive mountain chain on Earth (~65,000 km), centered on the Atlantic and Pacific; the ocean floor is young (≤200 Ma) while continents are up to 4 Ga.

#### The Vine-Matthews Hypothesis (1963)
Vine, Matthews, and Morley explained the symmetric striped pattern of **magnetic anomalies** on the ocean floor: new crust records the current magnetic field direction as it solidifies at ridges; when the field reverses, new crust records the opposite polarity. This creates a mirror-image striped pattern of normal/reversed polarity on either side of ridges—a "tape recording" of Earth's magnetic reversals and direct proof of seafloor spreading.

#### The Seven (or Eight) Major Plates
Major plates: Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Antarctic, Indo-Australian (sometimes split into Indian + Australian). Plus many smaller plates (Caribbean, Philippine, Juan de Fuca, Cocos, Nazca, Scotia, Arabian, etc.).

### Plate Boundary Types: Divergent, Convergent, Transform
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** plate-boundary-types-divergent-convergent-transform
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** divergent ridges · subduction · collision zones · transform faults
**Summary:** Understand the three types of plate boundaries, the landforms they create, the type of crust involved, and specific real-world examples for each.

#### Divergent Boundaries
At divergent boundaries, plates pull apart. **Oceanic-oceanic divergence**: creates mid-ocean ridges (e.g., Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East Pacific Rise). New basaltic crust is created by decompression melting of the mantle. Spreading rates: slow (~2.5 cm/yr, Mid-Atlantic) to fast (~15 cm/yr, East Pacific Rise). Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and is actually being pulled apart. **Continental-continental divergence**: creates rift valleys (East African Rift System—may eventually open into a new ocean in ~10 million years), then transitions to oceanic spreading as rifting continues.

#### Convergent Boundaries
When plates collide: **Oceanic-continental**: denser oceanic plate subducts; creates a trench (e.g., Peru-Chile Trench), volcanic arc on the overriding continental plate (Andes), and intense seismicity. **Oceanic-oceanic**: one plate subducts; creates a deep trench (Mariana Trench, deepest at ~11 km) and volcanic island arc (Japan, Philippines, Aleutians). **Continental-continental**: neither plate subducts easily; creates fold-thrust mountain belts (Himalayas: India-Eurasia collision began ~50 Ma and continues; highest peaks still rising ~5 mm/yr).

#### Transform Boundaries
Plates slide horizontally past each other, creating **transform faults**. No crust created or destroyed. Produces shallow, moderate-to-large earthquakes. Examples: San Andreas Fault (Pacific-North American plates, right-lateral), North Anatolian Fault (Turkey, right-lateral), Alpine Fault (New Zealand, right-lateral with some compression). Oceanic transform faults connect offset segments of mid-ocean ridges.

#### Triple Junctions
Where three plates meet: most are unstable except RRR (triple ridge junction, like the Galapagos Triple Junction). Triple junctions migrate over time.

### Driving Forces of Plate Tectonics
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** driving-forces-plate-tectonics
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** slab pull · ridge push · mantle convection · hot spots
**Summary:** Understand what actually drives plate motion—the relative contributions of slab pull, ridge push, and mantle drag—and how hot spots provide a fixed reference frame.

#### What Drives the Plates?
For decades, mantle convection was thought to be the primary driver (hot mantle rises at ridges, cools, and sinks at trenches, "dragging" plates). However, modern analysis suggests that **slab pull** is the dominant driving force: cold, dense subducting slabs pull the rest of the plate behind them. Evidence: plates attached to subducting slabs (Pacific, Nazca, Indo-Australian) move faster than plates without attached slabs (North American, African, Eurasian). **Ridge push** contributes: the elevated mid-ocean ridge creates a gravitational potential that pushes plates apart. Mantle drag is now thought to provide some resistance, not drive.

#### Hot Spots: Fixed Reference Frame
**Hot spots** are areas of anomalously high heat flux from deep mantle plumes, independent of plate boundaries. Classic example: Hawaii-Emperor seamount chain. The Pacific Plate moves northwest over the Hawaiian hot spot at ~9 cm/yr, creating a chain of progressively older volcanoes toward the northwest (Kauai: ~5 Ma; Midway: ~28 Ma; Detroit Seamount: ~76 Ma). The ~43 Ma bend in the chain records a change in Pacific Plate motion direction. Hot spots provide a nearly fixed reference frame for measuring absolute plate motion.

#### Plate Velocities
Plates move at 1–15 cm/yr. India is the fastest major plate historically (~15 cm/yr before collision with Eurasia slowed it). The Pacific Plate moves at ~7–9 cm/yr. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads at ~2.5 cm/yr per side.

#### Supercontinent Cycle
Continents aggregate into supercontinents roughly every ~500 million years (Wilson Cycle). The last supercontinent was Pangaea (~300–180 Ma). Before Pangaea: Rodinia (~1.1 Ga). The next supercontinent (sometimes called "Amasia") may form in ~250 million years.

### Plate Tectonics in Action: Calculating Rates and Ages
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** plate-tectonics-rates-and-ages
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** spreading rate calculations · hot spot age-distance analysis · paleomagnetic dating
**Summary:** Apply plate tectonics concepts to quantitative problems: calculate spreading rates from magnetic anomaly ages, determine plate velocity from hot spot chains, and use paleomagnetism to date rock sequences.

#### Calculating Spreading Rate
If magnetic anomaly stripes show that rock of age 10 Ma (million years ago) is found 250 km from the ridge on one side, then spreading rate = 250 km / 10 Myr = 25 km/Myr = 2.5 cm/yr. This is a half-spreading rate (one side). Full spreading rate = 5 cm/yr total (both sides combined). The East Pacific Rise spreads at ~15 cm/yr (fast); the Mid-Atlantic Ridge at ~2.5 cm/yr (slow).

#### Hot Spot Distance-Age Analysis
Hawaiian chain: Big Island (active, 0 Ma) → Kauai (~5.1 Ma, 510 km from Big Island) → Midway (~28 Ma) → Detroit Seamount (~76 Ma). Plotting distance vs. age gives plate velocity: ~9 cm/yr for the Pacific Plate over the Hawaiian hot spot. The bend at 43 Ma in the Emperor-Hawaii chain marks a change in plate motion direction from more northward to the current northwest trend.

#### Paleomagnetism and Polar Wander Curves
As tectonic plates move, the ancient magnetic field direction "locked" in rocks changes relative to the current pole position. By measuring paleomagnetic directions in rocks of different ages from the same continent, you can reconstruct the continent's ancient latitude. Comparing apparent polar wander curves from different continents provided independent evidence that they had once been joined.

#### Ocean Drilling as Confirmation
The Deep Sea Drilling Project (DSDP) and Ocean Drilling Program (ODP) drilled into oceanic crust worldwide and confirmed that ages increased with distance from ridges, and that the oldest oceanic crust (~200 Ma) is at the oldest margins—exactly as seafloor spreading predicts.

1. If a magnetic anomaly stripe dated at 5 Ma is found 100 km from a mid-ocean ridge, what is the half-spreading rate? What is the full rate?
2. How does the age-distance relationship of volcanic islands in a hot spot chain allow scientists to calculate plate velocity?
3. What did the comparison of apparent polar wander curves from South America and Africa reveal about their past positions?

### Rocks: The Three Families and Their Formation
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** three-rock-types-formation
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** igneous · sedimentary · metamorphic · rock cycle
**Summary:** Understand how each major rock type forms, the conditions that create them, and key examples of each that appear frequently in Science Bowl.

#### Igneous Rocks
Igneous rocks form from the cooling and solidification of **magma** (underground) or **lava** (surface). **Intrusive (plutonic)** igneous rocks cool slowly underground, forming large crystals: granite (coarse-grained, felsic), gabbro (coarse-grained, mafic), diorite. **Extrusive (volcanic)** igneous rocks cool rapidly at the surface, forming small crystals or glass: basalt (fine-grained, mafic—most common rock on Earth's surface), obsidian (volcanic glass), pumice (vesicular glass, floats on water). Rock texture reflects cooling rate: slow cooling = large crystals (phaneritic); fast cooling = small crystals (aphanitic); very fast = glassy.

#### Sedimentary Rocks
Sedimentary rocks form from compaction and cementation (**lithification**) of sediment, or by chemical/biological precipitation. **Clastic sedimentary rocks** form from fragments of other rocks: conglomerate (large rounded clasts), sandstone (sand-sized), shale (clay-sized, most common sedimentary rock). **Chemical sedimentary rocks**: rock salt (halite, evaporite), rock gypsum, limestone (can be chemical or biological). **Biogenic/biochemical**: coal (compressed plant material), limestone from shells/coral. Sedimentary rocks make up ~75% of rocks exposed at Earth's surface (but only ~8% of crustal volume) and contain virtually all fossils.

#### Metamorphic Rocks
Metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are subjected to heat and/or pressure (without melting) that causes mineralogical changes. **Regional metamorphism**: large-scale heat+pressure at convergent boundaries. **Contact metamorphism**: heat only, around igneous intrusions. Examples: marble (metamorphosed limestone), quartzite (metamorphosed sandstone/quartz), slate (low-grade metamorphosed shale), schist (medium grade), gneiss (high grade, banded). Increasing metamorphic grade: shale → slate → phyllite → schist → gneiss.

#### The Rock Cycle
Rocks continuously transform: magma cools → igneous rock; erosion/transport/deposition → sedimentary rock; heat/pressure → metamorphic rock; melting → back to magma. The cycle has no preferred direction and can skip steps.

### Minerals: Identification, Properties, and the Most Important Ones
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** minerals-identification-properties
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** Mohs hardness scale · cleavage vs. fracture · silicate minerals
**Summary:** Learn how geologists identify minerals using physical properties, understand the Mohs hardness scale, and recognize the most common and bowl-relevant minerals.

#### What Is a Mineral?
A mineral is a naturally occurring, inorganic, crystalline solid with a definite chemical composition. There are ~5,500 known minerals; about 30 are common. Rocks are aggregates of minerals (or occasionally one mineral: quartzite = almost pure quartz).

#### Physical Properties for Identification
- **Hardness** (Mohs scale 1–10): Talc(1), Gypsum(2), Calcite(3), Fluorite(4), Apatite(5), Feldspar(6), Quartz(7), Topaz(8), Corundum(9), Diamond(10). Memory: "Tall Girls Can Flirt And Every Quite Tall Cute Dog." Fingernail ≈ 2.5; copper penny ≈ 3.5; steel file ≈ 6.5.
- **Cleavage**: tendency to break along flat planes (e.g., mica cleaves in one direction; halite in three directions at 90°; calcite in three directions not at 90°)
- **Fracture**: irregular break (e.g., quartz has conchoidal/shell-like fracture)
- **Luster**: metallic vs. nonmetallic (vitreous/glassy, resinous, pearly, silky)
- **Color**: often unreliable due to impurities; **streak** (color of powder) more reliable
- **Specific gravity**: density relative to water (gold = 19.3; galena = 7.6; quartz = 2.65)

#### Key Minerals to Know
- **Quartz** (SiO₂): hardness 7, conchoidal fracture, vitreous luster; most common mineral in continental crust
- **Feldspar group** (hardness 6): most abundant mineral family (~60% of crust); K-feldspar and plagioclase feldspar
- **Mica** (biotite, muscovite): perfect single-plane cleavage, flaky
- **Calcite** (CaCO₃): hardness 3, reacts with HCl (fizzes), forms limestone and marble
- **Olivine** ((Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄): common in mantle, mafic rocks; forms green gemstone peridot
- **Pyrite** (FeS₂): "fool's gold," metallic cubic crystals, streak = black

#### Silicate Minerals
Most common mineral group: silicates, based on the SiO₄ tetrahedron. Olivine → pyroxene → amphibole → mica → feldspar → quartz = Bowen's Reaction Series (order of crystallization from cooling magma, from high to low temperature).

### Geological Maps: Reading Stratigraphy and Geologic History
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** geological-maps-stratigraphy-history
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** law of superposition · cross-cutting relationships · unconformities · stratigraphic column
**Summary:** Apply the principles of relative dating to read geological maps and cross-sections, determining the sequence of events that produced a given rock record.

#### Principles of Relative Dating
1. **Law of Superposition** (Nicolas Steno, 1669): in undisturbed sequences, older layers are below younger layers.
2. **Principle of Original Horizontality**: sediment is deposited in horizontal layers; tilted strata have been deformed.
3. **Principle of Cross-Cutting Relationships**: a feature that cuts across another (fault, intrusion, erosion surface) is younger than what it cuts.
4. **Principle of Inclusions**: fragments included in a rock are older than the rock containing them.
5. **Law of Faunal Succession**: fossils succeed one another in a definite, recognizable order; specific fossil assemblages characterize specific time intervals.

#### Unconformities: Gaps in the Record
An **unconformity** is a surface representing missing time—a gap in the rock record. Types: **Angular unconformity**: tilted/folded rocks below, horizontal above (the Great Unconformity in the Grand Canyon represents ~1.2 billion years of missing rock). **Disconformity**: parallel layers above and below, but missing time (erosion surface). **Nonconformity**: sedimentary rocks above, igneous/metamorphic below.

#### Reading a Geological Cross-Section
Steps to determine the order of events:
1. Start with the oldest sedimentary layers at the bottom (superposition)
2. Identify any intrusions (younger than surrounding rock they cross-cut)
3. Identify faults (younger than rocks they cut)
4. Identify unconformities (erosion event, representing folding/tilting/uplift before next deposition)
5. Build a timeline from oldest to youngest

#### Example Event Sequence
Scenario: limestone → granite intrusion cuts limestone → erosion surface (unconformity) → shale deposited above → normal fault cuts both shale and granite. Order: (1) limestone deposited, (2) granite intruded, (3) erosion created unconformity, (4) shale deposited, (5) fault occurred.

#### How Ore Deposits Form
**Hydrothermal deposits**: hot water circulating through cracks in rock dissolves metals and deposits them as veins when it cools. Gold, silver, copper, lead, zinc deposits often form this way. Mid-ocean ridge hydrothermal vents (black smokers) deposit massive sulfide ores on the seafloor. **Magmatic segregation**: as magma cools, dense minerals settle out and concentrate (e.g., chromite, platinum group elements in mafic/ultramafic intrusions like South Africa's Bushveld Complex). **Placer deposits**: erosion concentrates dense minerals in river beds (gold panning) or beaches.

#### Key Economic Minerals
- Hematite/magnetite (Fe₂O₃ / Fe₃O₄): iron ore (75% of world's iron from banded iron formations, BIFs)
- Bauxite: aluminum ore (hydrated aluminum oxide, formed by tropical weathering)
- Chalcopyrite (CuFeS₂): primary copper ore
- Galena (PbS): lead ore
- Sphalerite (ZnS): zinc ore
- Halite (NaCl): rock salt, evaporite; potassium evaporites = fertilizer

#### Rock Uses
- Limestone → cement/concrete (calcined to form lime, CaO); also crushed aggregate
- Granite → building stone, monuments (very durable, hard)
- Marble → sculpture, building (Michelangelo's David; Parthenon)
- Shale → brick, pottery (fired clay)
- Sandstone → building material (Empire State Building originally planned with brownstone)
- Obsidian → prehistoric cutting tools (sharper than surgical steel when knapped)

#### Rock Types Summary
| Type | Formed From | Examples | Key Property |
|---|---|---|---|
| Igneous (intrusive) | Slow-cooling magma | Granite, gabbro | Coarse crystals |
| Igneous (extrusive) | Fast-cooling lava | Basalt, obsidian | Fine crystals/glass |
| Sedimentary (clastic) | Fragments + cementation | Shale, sandstone | Contains fossils |
| Sedimentary (chemical) | Precipitation | Limestone, halite | Chemically deposited |
| Metamorphic | Heat + pressure | Marble, gneiss, schist | Foliated/non-foliated |

#### Mineral Identification Quick Reference
- Hardness 1 = Talc; 7 = Quartz; 10 = Diamond
- Fizzes in HCl = Calcite (CaCO₃)
- Perfect single-plane cleavage, flaky = Mica
- Cubic crystals, heavy, gray luster, black streak = Galena
- Gold color but black streak, cubic = Pyrite (fool's gold)
- Green, found in mantle rocks = Olivine

#### Relative Dating Principles
1. Superposition (older = lower)
2. Original horizontality (deformed = moved)
3. Cross-cutting relationships (cutter = younger)
4. Inclusions (included fragment = older)
5. Faunal succession (fossil assemblages = time markers)

#### Top 18 High-Yield Facts

1. Mohs hardness scale: Talc(1), Gypsum(2), Calcite(3), Fluorite(4), Apatite(5), Feldspar(6), Quartz(7), Topaz(8), Corundum(9), Diamond(10)
2. Most common mineral in continental crust: **feldspar** (>60%)
3. Basalt = fine-grained mafic igneous; most common rock on Earth's surface
4. Granite = coarse-grained felsic igneous; most common continental intrusive rock
5. Shale = most common sedimentary rock; fine-grained clastic (clay particles)
6. Marble = metamorphosed limestone; calcite composition, nonfoliated
7. Quartzite = metamorphosed sandstone; very hard, nonfoliated
8. Gneiss = high-grade metamorphic; strongly foliated (banded), from granite or shale
9. Calcite (CaCO₃): hardness 3, fizzes with HCl, forms limestone and marble
10. Pyrite (FeS₂): "fool's gold"; cubic crystals; streak is **black** (not gold!)
11. Law of Superposition: Nicolas Steno, 1669; older rocks below younger
12. Cross-cutting relationships: fault/intrusion younger than what it cuts
13. Angular unconformity: tilted rocks below + erosion surface + horizontal above = Great Unconformity, Grand Canyon (~1.2 Ga of missing time)
14. Banded iron formations (BIFs): primary iron ore source; formed ~2.5–1.8 Ga when cyanobacteria oxygenated oceans
15. Bowen's Reaction Series: crystallization order from magma; olivine first, quartz last
16. Hydrothermal deposits: hot water deposits metals (gold, copper, lead, zinc) in veins
17. Bauxite = aluminum ore; formed by deep tropical weathering of aluminosilicate rocks
18. Placer deposits: river current concentrates dense minerals; gold rush rivers
