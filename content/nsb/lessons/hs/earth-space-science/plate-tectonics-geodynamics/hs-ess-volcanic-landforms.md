#### Shield Volcanoes
**Shield volcanoes** are built from successive basaltic lava flows that spread widely, producing broad, gently sloping edifices (resembling a warrior's shield lying flat). They form over hotspots (Hawaii's Mauna Loa, tallest volcano on Earth from base — 9 km from seafloor to summit) and at some MOR settings. Eruptions are frequent, effusive, and non-explosive. Mauna Loa has erupted 33 times since 1843 and is expected to erupt again within years to decades.

#### Stratovolcanoes (Composite Volcanoes)
**Stratovolcanoes** are built from alternating layers of lava flows and pyroclastic deposits, producing steep-sided, symmetric cones. They form at subduction zones (Fuji, Rainier, Hood, St. Helens, Pinatubo, Vesuvius). More silica-rich, viscous magmas build these steep structures. They erupt less frequently than shield volcanoes but far more explosively and dangerously.

#### Calderas
A **caldera** is a large, roughly circular depression formed when a volcano's magma chamber empties (either by eruption or subsidence) and the overlying edifice collapses inward. Calderas range from a few km (Crater Lake, Oregon — formed ~7,700 years ago when Mt. Mazama collapsed after a VEI 7 eruption) to >100 km (Yellowstone caldera). They are distinct from craters (small summit depressions formed by eruption, not collapse).

#### Large Igneous Provinces (LIPs) and Flood Basalts
**Large igneous provinces** are massive accumulations of igneous rocks formed by extraordinary mantle melting events. The **Deccan Traps** (India, ~66 Ma) produced up to 2 million km³ of basalt over ~1 million years — likely contributing to the K-Pg extinction. The **Columbia River Basalts** (~17–15 Ma, Pacific Northwest) cover ~210,000 km² to depths of 1–4 km. These are not associated with normal plate boundaries or hotspots but with deep-seated mantle anomalies.
