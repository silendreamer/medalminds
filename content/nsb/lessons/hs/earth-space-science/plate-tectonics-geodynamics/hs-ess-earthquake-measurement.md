#### The Richter Scale (Local Magnitude, Mₗ)
Charles Richter (1935) developed a magnitude scale based on the maximum amplitude of seismic waves recorded on a specific seismograph (Wood-Anderson torsion seismograph) at a standard distance of 100 km. The scale is **logarithmic**: each integer increase represents a 10× increase in ground motion amplitude and roughly a 31.6× increase in energy released. A M6 earthquake releases ~31.6× more energy than a M5. The Richter scale works well for local California earthquakes but saturates for large earthquakes (M > 7) because it doesn't account for fault area and slip.

#### Moment Magnitude (Mw)
The **moment magnitude scale (Mw)** is now the standard for earthquake science. It is based on the **seismic moment**: M₀ = μ × A × D, where μ is rock rigidity, A is the fault rupture area, and D is average slip. This scale doesn't saturate and works for all earthquake sizes. The 2011 Tōhoku earthquake (Japan) was Mw 9.0; the 1960 Valdivia earthquake (Chile) was Mw 9.5 — the largest ever recorded. Each 1-unit increase in Mw = 10× ground amplitude = ~31.6× energy; 2 units = 1,000× energy.

#### Modified Mercalli Intensity Scale (MMI)
**Intensity** measures the effects of shaking at a specific location and varies with distance from the epicenter, local geology, and building type. The **Modified Mercalli Intensity (MMI) scale** runs from I (not felt) to XII (total destruction). Soft sediments (bay mud, river deposits) amplify shaking dramatically — a phenomenon called **site amplification**. The 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake preferentially damaged buildings on bay mud in San Francisco's Marina District, 100 km from the epicenter.
