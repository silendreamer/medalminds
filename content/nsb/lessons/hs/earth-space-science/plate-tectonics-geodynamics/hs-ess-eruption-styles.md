#### Viscosity Controls Eruption Style
**Viscosity** (resistance to flow) in magma depends on: SiO₂ content (higher Si → more polymerization → higher viscosity); temperature (higher temp → lower viscosity); dissolved volatiles (water, CO₂). Basaltic magma (low Si, high temp): low viscosity → **effusive eruptions** — lava fountains and fast-moving lava flows (Hawaiian shield volcanoes). Rhyolitic magma (high Si, lower temp, volatile-rich): high viscosity → **explosive eruptions** — gases can't escape gradually; pressure builds until catastrophic fragmentation (Plinian eruptions, supervolcanoes).

#### Pyroclastic Material
Explosive eruptions fragment magma into **pyroclastic material**: ash (< 2 mm), lapilli (2–64 mm), and volcanic bombs (> 64 mm). **Pyroclastic flows** (also called pyroclastic density currents) are fast-moving (100–700 km/hr), hot (300–800°C), turbulent avalanches of gas and pyroclasts — the most dangerous volcanic phenomenon. The 79 CE eruption of Vesuvius killed Pompeii's residents by pyroclastic surges reaching ~300°C. The 1980 Mount St. Helens eruption lateral blast was a pyroclastic density current.

#### Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI)
The **VEI** scale (0–8) rates eruption size by volume of ejecta and eruption column height. Each unit represents a 10× increase in ejecta volume. VEI 8 = "super-eruption" (≥ 1,000 km³ tephra); examples: Toba (~74,000 years ago, VEI 8, nearly drove humans to extinction with a volcanic winter), Yellowstone Huckleberry Ridge (~2.1 Ma, VEI 8). For comparison, the 1980 Mount St. Helens eruption was VEI 5; the 1991 Pinatubo (Philippines) was VEI 6 and cooled global temperatures by ~0.5°C for 1–2 years.
