## Subtopic: Natural Resources (Fossil Fuels, Minerals, Water)

### Energy Resources and Fossil Fuels
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** energy-resources-fossil-fuels
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** coal · petroleum · natural gas · resource depletion · energy content
**Summary:** Fossil fuels—coal, oil, and natural gas—are the primary global energy source; understanding their formation, extraction, and depletion timescales is essential for energy policy.

#### Formation and Types of Fossil Fuels
Coal forms from accumulated plant debris (peat) compressed and heated over millions of years. The rank progression: peat (50% C) → lignite (70% C) → subbituminous (77% C) → bituminous (80% C) → anthracite (93% C) reflects increasing carbon content and energy density. Anthracite, formed at highest pressure/temperature, has the highest energy content (~30 MJ/kg) and lowest sulfur (burns cleanest). Petroleum and natural gas form from marine plankton and algae buried in sediments. Source rocks must be: (1) organic-rich (marine shales), (2) buried deeply (thermal maturity window 50–150°C), (3) isolated from oxygen (anoxic). Oil generation occurs at temperatures 60–120°C; temperatures >120°C produce mainly gas. The maturation timescale is geological—millions of years. Generated hydrocarbons migrate upward into reservoir rocks (porous sandstones, carbonates) and are trapped by impermeable seals (shale, evaporites). Without a trap, hydrocarbons continue migrating to surface, lost. This trap-seal-source-migration system is the petroleum system—all must be present for economic accumulation.

#### Global Reserves and Production
Proven coal reserves are ~1 trillion tonnes, lasting ~130 years at current consumption (25 billion tonnes/year). Proven petroleum reserves are ~1.7 trillion barrels (~250 billion tonnes), lasting ~50 years at current production (90 million barrels/day). Proven natural gas reserves are ~180 trillion m³, lasting ~52 years. These "reserve lifetimes" assume static production—increasing production shortens them, while price increases might expand reserves (costlier extraction becomes economic at higher prices). Actual depletion timescales are hard to predict because: (1) new discoveries extend reserves, (2) technology (enhanced recovery, unconventional resources like shale) accesses previously uneconomic resources, (3) price-driven economics determine whether resources are "proven." The concept of "peak oil" (maximum production rate reached, then declining) is still debated—some predict peak oil has occurred, others forecast it in the 2030s, while others argue unconventional oil extends production.

#### Energy Density and Comparison
Energy content of fuels varies: coal ~24 MJ/kg, crude oil ~46 MJ/kg, natural gas ~55 MJ/kg (by mass). Natural gas has higher energy density than coal but lower than oil. However, practical energy yields depend on thermodynamic efficiency (power plant efficiency ~35% for coal, ~45% for natural gas, ~60% for combined cycle gas plants). CO2 emissions per unit energy vary: coal ~95 g CO2/MJ, oil ~75 g CO2/MJ, natural gas ~50 g CO2/MJ. So natural gas is the cleanest fossil fuel, coal the dirtiest. Switching from coal to natural gas reduces CO2 emissions by ~50%, a significant mitigation step (though not sufficient to meet climate targets without renewable transition).

#### Unconventional Fossil Fuels
Shale oil and shale gas (hydrocarbons in low-permeability source rocks) were historically uneconomic but hydraulic fracturing (fracking) made them viable. Fracking injects pressurized fluid to crack rock and prop open fractures, allowing hydrocarbon flow. Shale gas production in the US surged from <1% of gas supply (2000) to ~50% (2020), dramatically extending US gas resources. Similarly, shale oil contributes to US oil production. Environmental concerns include: (1) water contamination (fracturing fluids, produced water), (2) methane leakage (greenhouse gas), (3) induced seismicity (earthquakes from wastewater injection). Oil sands (viscous bitumen mixed with sand in Alberta, Canada; Venezuela; Russia) require thermal processing (heating) to extract, producing 1 tonne of CO2 per barrel (vs. ~0.5 tonne for conventional oil). Heavy oil, tar sands, and methane hydrates are difficult/costly to extract and have high environmental impacts, making them poor choices for climate-constrained future.

#### Review Questions
1. Describe the petroleum system including source rock, maturation, migration, and trap.
2. Calculate the reserve lifetime for oil assuming reserves of 1.7 trillion barrels and production of 90 million barrels/day.
3. Compare energy density and CO2 intensity of coal, oil, and natural gas.

### Mineral Resources and Mining
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** mineral-resources-mining
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** ore deposits · concentrations · mining impacts · sustainability
**Summary:** Mineral resources (metals, construction materials, industrial minerals) are essential for civilization; extraction has environmental impacts and sustainability concerns.

#### Ore Deposits and Mineral Concentrations
Ore is rock with economically recoverable mineral(s). A "ore deposit" requires: (1) elevated concentration of desired metal, (2) adequate volume, (3) economic viability. Background crustal abundance vs. ore concentration:
- Copper: 50 ppm crustal, 5,000 ppm ore (~100× enrichment)
- Gold: 0.004 ppm crustal, 10 ppm ore (~2,500× enrichment)
- Iron: 5% crustal, 50–70% ore (~10× enrichment)

Achieving this enrichment requires geological processes: hydrothermal alteration (hot water dissolving and reprecipitating metals), weathering (surface concentration via leaching), evaporation (salt, potash deposits), biomineralization. Ore deposit types:
- **Porphyry Cu**: magma crystallizes, cooling fluids deposit Cu—typical grade 0.5–2% Cu
- **Placer deposits**: mechanical weathering concentrates dense minerals (gold, diamonds) in stream gravels
- **Laterite**: tropical weathering concentrates Fe, Al, Ni in residual soils
- **Evaporites**: seawater evaporation concentrates salt, gypsum, potash

#### Mining Methods and Impacts
**Surface mining** (open pit) removes overburden to access ore; efficient for low-grade deposits but creates massive disturbance. Pits 1+ km deep have been excavated (Bingham Canyon copper mine, Utah). Environmental impacts: landscape destruction, acid mine drainage (exposed sulfides oxidize, creating acidic water that mobilizes metals), habitat loss, dust. **Underground mining** for deep deposits uses shafts and tunnels; less surface disturbance but higher cost and safety risks. Methane/gas hazards and collapse risks are common. **Placer mining** (panning, sluicing) disturbs stream sediments; cyanide leaching for gold dissolves ore but is toxic to aquatic life if not contained.

#### Water Resources from Mining
Mining is water-intensive: copper extraction ~100,000 L/tonne ore, and concentrating ore requires flotation (water-intensive separation). In water-scarce regions (Chile, Peru for copper; South Africa for gold), mining competes with agriculture and municipal use. The Atacama Desert (driest non-polar place) has massive copper mines that deplete freshwater aquifers, threatening indigenous communities. Climate change exacerbates water stress—seasonal snowmelt (which mines traditionally rely on) declines, forcing deeper groundwater extraction. Peru's water conflicts between mining and agriculture are escalating.

#### Sustainability and Circular Economy
Ore depletion is inevitable at current consumption. High-grade deposits are preferentially mined first (lower-cost extraction). As grades decline, extraction costs rise and environmental impacts worsen (more waste rock per tonne metal). Some metals (like phosphorus, a critical agricultural nutrient) have no substitutes; once depleted, civilization is constrained. Recycling reduces primary mining demand: aluminum recycling is ~50% global supply (reduces energy use by 95%), copper recycling ~30%, gold recycling ~30%. A circular economy (products designed for recycling, high recovery rates) could extend resource lifetimes. However, current recycling is limited by economics and technology—rare earth elements (essential for renewable energy, electronics) are < 2% recycled globally. Future supply security is a major concern for energy transition (wind turbines need rare earths, neodymium for generators).

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate the ore concentrate ratio needed to produce 1 tonne of copper from 0.5% grade ore.
2. Explain why ore grades decline over time and what this implies for environmental impacts.
3. How does recycling reduce environmental impacts of mining?

### Water Resources and Freshwater Availability
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** water-resources-freshwater-availability
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** water cycle · aquifers · groundwater depletion · water stress · sea-level rise impacts
**Summary:** Freshwater is essential for agriculture, industry, and human consumption; water availability is unevenly distributed geographically and is changing with climate.

#### Global Water Distribution and Availability
Earth has ~1.386 billion km³ of water; only ~2.5% is freshwater, and of that, ~70% is frozen in ice sheets and glaciers. Accessible freshwater (liquid, in lakes, rivers, aquifers) is ~0.3% of total water. Per capita freshwater availability varies enormously: Canada >100,000 m³/capita/year, Egypt <1,000 m³/capita/year. The UN defines "water stress" as <1,700 m³/capita/year, and "severe water scarcity" as <500 m³/capita/year. ~4 billion people (>50% global population) experience severe water stress during part of the year. By 2050, projected population growth and climate change could increase this to ~6 billion people.

#### Aquifers and Groundwater
Groundwater (water in porous rock/sediment) is the largest source of freshwater for human use (~70% of freshwater withdrawals). Aquifers recharge from precipitation/infiltration over years to millennia, depending on depth and recharge area. Renewable aquifers have recharge rates sufficient to sustain extraction; non-renewable (fossil) aquifers accumulate over millions of years without active recharge. The Ogallala Aquifer (Great Plains, USA) has been depleted from ~300 m average thickness to <60 m in 60 years of irrigation-driven withdrawal. Its remaining lifetime is ~20–30 years if current depletion continues. The North China Plain, Indian subcontinent, and Middle East have similar aquifer depletion. Groundwater depletion has three impacts: (1) well deepening costs, (2) land subsidence (as aquifer pore space collapses), (3) freshwater-saltwater interface intrusion (in coastal regions).

#### Climate Change and Water Availability
Warming alters water cycles: (1) increased evaporation (warmer air holds more moisture), (2) changed precipitation (some regions wetter, others drier), (3) snowpack decline (shifts runoff timing, reduces storage), (4) glacier loss (eliminates buffering of seasonal flow). Snow-dependent water supplies (Alps, Himalayas, Andes, Sierra Nevada) are vulnerable. Spring snowmelt currently provides summer baseflow; with reduced snowpack, summer water availability declines. California's water crisis is partly driven by reduced snowpack (~50% decline relative to 1960s), forcing groundwater overdraft. Tropical and subtropical regions face intensified droughts (projected 10–50% decline in water availability in some regions). Conversely, high-latitude regions may see increased precipitation and runoff (some benefit for hydropower). The net global effect is increased water stress in already water-stressed regions.

#### Dams, Hydropower, and Ecosystem Impacts
Dams provide hydropower (15% global electricity) and water storage (irrigation, municipal). However, dams have massive ecosystem impacts: (1) river fragmentation (blocks fish migration, disrupts sediment transport), (2) delta destruction (dammed rivers don't reach deltas, starving them of sediment—Nile Delta and Mekong Delta are collapsing), (3) reservoir inundation (submerges ecosystems, displaces people). The Three Gorges Dam (China) submerged 1,300 km² and displaced 1.3 million people. Hydropower is renewable but ecosystem-damaging. Future dams are increasingly opposed by environmental and indigenous groups. Some regions are removing dams (e.g., Elwha River, USA) to restore rivers despite losing hydropower capacity—a statement that ecosystem services outweigh energy benefits in some cases.

#### Review Questions
1. What fraction of Earth's water is accessible freshwater and why is freshwater so limited?
2. Explain why the Ogallala Aquifer is considered "non-renewable" despite its massive size.
3. How does climate change intensify water stress in already water-stressed regions?

## Subtopic: Resource Extraction & Sustainability

### Extractive Industries and Environmental Impacts
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** extractive-industries-environmental-impacts
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** acid mine drainage · tailings · habitat loss · land reclamation
**Summary:** Extracting fossil fuels and minerals creates environmental damage through acid mine drainage, tailings disposal, habitat loss, and land subsidence.

#### Acid Mine Drainage
When sulfide minerals (pyrite, chalcopyrite) in ore deposits are exposed to oxygen and water (during mining), they oxidize, producing sulfuric acid:
2 FeS₂ + 7 O₂ + 2 H₂O → 2 Fe²⁺ + 4 SO₄²⁻ + 4 H⁺

This acidic drainage (pH 2–3) mobilizes metals dissolved in the acidic solution. Iron oxidizes to Fe³⁺, which hydrolyzes to form iron oxyhydroxide precipitate (red/orange discoloration). Copper, zinc, lead also dissolve. AMD can persist for decades or centuries; streams downgradient of mines are acidified and biologically sterile for tens of kilometers. Remediation (treating AMD to neutralize acidity and precipitate metals) is expensive—costs $1,000s per acre for active treatment. Some sites (Summitville, Colorado) required $150+ million cleanup of abandoned mining. Natural AMD occurs too (from weathering of sulfide deposits), but mining accelerates the process and concentrates drainage at specific discharge points, intensifying local impacts.

#### Tailings and Storage
Extraction produces vast quantities of tailings (ground rock after ore separation). A single copper mine might process 100,000 tonnes/day, producing 99,500 tonnes of tailings. Tailings must be stored—historically in tailings ponds behind dams. If the dam fails (rare but catastrophic), tailings spill downstream. The 2015 Samarco (Vale/BHP) dam failure in Brazil released 62 million cubic meters of tailings, destroying the Rio Doce river ecosystem and affecting 250,000 people. The 2014 Mount Polley mine tailings dam failure (Canada) released 25 million cubic meters into Polley Lake. These failures motivate better tailings management—dry stacking (mechanically compacted tailings, stack above ground) is becoming standard but more expensive. Tailings ponds remain a significant risk.

#### Habitat Loss and Biodiversity
Surface mining directly destroys habitat. Mountaintop removal mining (blasting off mountaintops to access coal in Appalachia) has flattened over 500 mountaintops, eliminating irreplaceable forest ecosystems and stream habitat. The cumulative area equals ~3,000 km². Oil and gas extraction fragmentizes habitats—drilling pads, pipelines, roads create a networked landscape hostile to wildlife. Rainforest habitat in Ecuador, Peru, and Indonesia is fragmented by oil exploration and extraction. A single well pad might seem small, but hundreds to thousands of pads create pervasive disturbance. Biodiversity loss from habitat fragmentation is difficult to quantify but known to be severe—edge effects (sun exposure, temperature increase) extend beyond the direct impact zone.

#### Land Subsidence
Extracting fluids (groundwater, petroleum, natural gas) from depth removes support for overlying rock. The sediment compacts, causing surface subsidence. Long Beach, California, subsided >9 meters over the 20th century due to oil extraction. Subsidence costs: (1) damage to built infrastructure (cracked foundations, buckled roads), (2) increased flood risk (subsided areas lie below normal drainage), (3) saltwater intrusion (subsided coastal areas allow seawater encroachment). The Netherlands has extensive subsidence from past peat extraction and groundwater withdrawal; extensive dike systems manage the resulting low-lying areas. Future subsidence is expected from CO₂ storage (storing CO₂ underground in depleted oil fields or aquifers requires accounting for pressure changes and potential subsidence).

#### Review Questions
1. Write the oxidation reaction for pyrite exposed to oxygen and water, explaining why it produces acidity.
2. Why do tailings dam failures have catastrophic impacts and how can they be prevented?
3. Explain the causes and consequences of land subsidence from resource extraction.

### Energy Transition and Renewable Energy Viability
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** energy-transition-renewable-viability
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** levelized cost of energy · capacity factor · grid integration · energy storage
**Summary:** Transitioning to renewable energy requires understanding costs, reliability, grid integration, and storage challenges; pathways to decarbonization depend on technology maturation and policy.

#### Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE)
LCOE is the average cost per unit energy ($/MWh or $/kWh) over a project lifetime, accounting for capital costs, operation, maintenance, and fuel costs. In 2024: Coal LCOE ~$60–100/MWh, Gas ~$50–80/MWh, Nuclear ~$130–190/MWh, Solar ~$30–50/MWh, Wind ~$40–70/MWh. By pure cost, solar and wind are cheapest. However, LCOE alone doesn't determine total energy system cost because: (1) renewables have capacity factor <100% (solar ~20%, wind ~35%, vs. coal/gas/nuclear >80%), (2) renewables are intermittent, requiring storage/backup. Total system cost includes transmission, storage, backup generation, and grid modernization. Adding these, system cost for high-renewable penetration (~80%) is estimated $100–200/MWh in developed economies, comparable to fossil fuel systems.

#### Capacity Factor and Grid Contribution
Capacity factor (actual output / maximum theoretical output) determines energy contribution. Solar at 20% capacity means a 1 GW solar plant produces 0.2 GW average. To replace a retiring 1 GW coal plant (operating at 85% capacity, providing 0.85 GW average), you need ~4–5 GW of solar + storage/backup. Geographic variation is huge: solar in Arizona gets 25% capacity factor, solar in Germany gets 15%. Wind in the Great Plains gets 40–50% capacity factor, offshore wind 50%+. Choosing locations with high capacity factors reduces the overbuild needed. Seasonal variation is also critical: solar productivity peaks in summer (excess generation), wind peaks in winter (supply-demand mismatch in heating-dominant regions).

#### Energy Storage and Grid Integration
Intermittency is addressed through: (1) storage (batteries, pumped hydro, thermal storage), (2) demand response (shifting consumption to match supply), (3) geographic distribution (balancing wind from multiple regions), (4) backup generation (natural gas plants). Batteries are expensive (~$100–200/kWh) but costs are declining (~10% annually). A single day's storage requires ~1–5 kWh storage per kW of peak generation. Seasonal storage (months of storage for winter solar deficit) is impractical with batteries—requires alternative technologies (hydrogen, synthetic fuels, thermal storage). Pumped hydro (existing technology) provides ~90% of global energy storage but is geographically limited (requires topographic relief and water). Addressing seasonal mismatch in renewable scenarios requires long-duration storage technologies still under development.

#### Decarbonization Pathways
Achieving net-zero emissions by 2050 requires: (1) electricity decarbonization (80% renewable + nuclear by 2050), (2) electrification of transport (EVs replace ICE), (3) building efficiency (heat pumps, insulation), (4) industrial transitions (steel/cement/chemicals use hydrogen, electric furnaces, or carbon capture). The International Energy Agency projects ~90% electricity decarbonization is feasible with current technology deployed at scale. However, industrial process heat, aviation, shipping, and agriculture remain challenging. Hydrogen (produced from electrolysis using renewable electricity) can decarbonize these sectors but is expensive (~$5–10/kg presently, targets <$2/kg by 2050). Carbon capture (removing CO₂ from flue gas or directly from air) is expensive (~$100–300/tonne) and energy-intensive, limiting feasibility. Most scenarios require all three transitions (renewables + efficiency + carbon removal) for net-zero.

#### Review Questions
1. Compare LCOE for coal, nuclear, solar, and wind; explain why lowest-cost energy doesn't guarantee lowest-cost system.
2. Calculate the solar panel area needed to replace a 1 GW coal plant assuming 20% capacity factor and 20% panel efficiency.
3. What storage duration is needed to address seasonal (month-to-month) renewable variability?

## Subtopic: Natural Hazards (Earthquakes, Volcanoes, Floods, Landslides)

### Earthquakes and Seismic Hazards
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** earthquakes-seismic-hazards
**Estimated time:** 15 min
**Key concepts:** plate boundaries · stress accumulation · P and S waves · magnitude scales · seismic hazards
**Summary:** Earthquakes occur at plate boundaries where accumulated stress is suddenly released; magnitude and location determine hazard to people and infrastructure.

#### Earthquake Mechanics
Earthquakes result from sudden slip on faults, releasing accumulated elastic strain. Stress accumulates as plates move and friction locks the fault. When stress exceeds rock strength, rupture initiates and propagates, releasing seismic waves. Moment magnitude (Mw) quantifies earthquake size: Mw = (2/3) × log(M₀) - 10.7, where M₀ is seismic moment (related to rigidity, area, slip distance). Unlike the older Richter scale (limited at high magnitudes, saturating at ~Ml 7.5), moment magnitude is valid across all earthquake sizes. Moment magnitude preserves information about actual rupture: Mw 7 earthquake represents ~10⁶ times more energy release than Mw 5. The energy release is: log(E) ≈ 4.8 + 1.5 × Mw (joules). A Mw 8 earthquake releases ~6 × 10¹⁶ joules—equivalent to ~1,000 Hiroshima bombs.

#### Seismic Wave Propagation
Two types of body waves propagate through the Earth: P-waves (primary, compressional, faster ~6 km/s) and S-waves (secondary, shear, slower ~3.5 km/s). P-waves cause ground motion parallel to wave propagation (push-pull); S-waves cause motion perpendicular (shaking). Surface waves (Rayleigh and Love waves) are slower but larger amplitude, causing most damage. Seismometers measure ground motion displacement, velocity, or acceleration. Strong-motion accelerometers (recording high-frequency, high-amplitude shaking) are used for engineering hazard assessment. Peak ground acceleration (PGA), spectral acceleration (Sa), and peak ground velocity (PGV) are key parameters affecting structural damage. Buildings resonate at specific frequencies (natural period); if seismic waves contain energy at the building's natural frequency, resonance amplifies shaking and can cause collapse. Soft-soil sites amplify shaking (unconsolidated sediment resonates at lower frequencies, trapping seismic energy).

#### Seismic Hazards and Ground Effects
Shaking damage varies with: (1) earthquake magnitude and proximity (larger, closer = stronger shaking), (2) site conditions (soft soil >> bedrock), (3) building design (unreinforced masonry < wood < reinforced concrete). Liquefaction occurs when strong shaking causes saturated sandy sediment to lose strength (pore pressure rises, overburden pressure drops), turning soil into liquid-like behavior. Buildings founded on liquefied soil may tilt or sink. Lateral spreads (spreading of ground toward open faces like river banks) occur on gentle slopes due to liquefaction. The 1964 Niigata earthquake (Japan) caused extensive liquefaction, tilting apartment buildings intact but unusable. Landslides are triggered by strong shaking—steep slopes become unstable. The 1999 Chi-Chi earthquake (Taiwan) triggered 20,000+ landslides, blocking valleys and creating dams. Tsunami (generated by submarine earthquakes displacing ocean floor) caused ~100x more deaths in the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake (230,000+) than the earthquake shaking itself.

#### Seismic Hazard Assessment
Seismic hazard maps show PGA or spectral acceleration exceeded with given probability in given time interval (e.g., "10% chance of exceedance in 50 years" or "475-year return period"). These maps drive building code seismic design requirements. US Seismic Hazard Maps show California, Pacific Northwest, and Intermountain West at highest risk; most of the interior US is moderate to low risk. However, surprise earthquakes occur—the 2011 Christchurch earthquake (New Zealand) caused ~$40 billion damage in a region with moderate hazard rating; poor building stock (old, unreinforced) amplified impacts. Early warning systems (detecting P-waves, broadcasting warning before S-waves arrive, typically 1–10 seconds) can trigger automated actions (trains braking, surgeries pausing), saving some lives in seconds-to-seconds decisions. However, earthquakes very close to epicenter won't have warning time.

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate seismic moment and energy release for a Mw 7.5 earthquake vs. Mw 6.5 (compare ratios).
2. Explain why soft soil sites experience stronger shaking than bedrock sites.
3. What is liquefaction and under what conditions does it occur?

### Volcanic Hazards and Eruption Styles
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** volcanic-hazards-eruption-styles
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** magma composition · eruption types · pyroclastic flows · lahars · volcanic emissions
**Summary:** Volcanic hazards vary with magma composition and eruption style; understanding these is critical for hazard mitigation in volcanic regions.

#### Magma Composition and Eruption Style
Magma composition (silica content, water content) determines eruption type. Low-silica basaltic magma (~50% SiO₂, low viscosity) erupts as effusive flows (Hawaii eruptions). High-silica rhyolitic magma (~70% SiO₂, high viscosity) erupts explosively, forming pyroclastic flows (Philippines 1991 Pinatubo). The relationship: higher silica = higher viscosity = higher gas pressure required to exsolve bubbles = more explosive. Dissolved water content also matters: water lowers magma melting point and, when exsolved, creates gas bubbles driving eruptions. A magma at depth might be quiescent (no bubbles because pressure keeps water dissolved), but rapid ascent causes decompression, gas exsolution, and explosive behavior. This explains how eruptions can begin suddenly.

#### Eruption Classification
The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) classifies eruptions 0–8: VEI 0–1 (Hawaiian, gentle effusion), VEI 2 (Strombolian, small explosions), VEI 3–4 (Vulcanian/Plinian, major explosive), VEI 5+ (rare, catastrophic). Mount Pinatubo (1991) was VEI 6, one of the largest of the 20th century. VEI 7–8 (Yellowstone supereruptions) haven't occurred in recorded history but are known from geological record. VEI correlates with magma composition: basaltic shield volcanoes are VEI 1–2, andesitic stratovolcanoes are VEI 3–5, rhyolitic calderas are VEI 6–8. This composition variation reflects tectonic setting: subduction zones (where water-enriched oceanic crust descends) produce arc magmas (intermediate composition, VEI 3–5), while hot spots (Hawaiian islands) produce basalts (VEI 1–2).

#### Pyroclastic Flows and Lahars
Pyroclastic flows are mixtures of hot gas and fragmented rock traveling >100 km/h down slopes. They cannot be outrun and have temperatures >500°C. The 1902 Mount Pelée (Martinique) pyroclastic flow killed 29,000 people in seconds. Pyroclastic flows deposit pyroclastic density currents (PDCs) that can travel far from volcano (100+ km for large eruptions) and destroy everything in their path. Lahars are mudflows of volcanic sediment, triggered by heavy rainfall on fresh ash deposits or melting of summit ice/snow. The 1985 Nevado Ruiz (Colombia) lahars killed 23,000 when pyroclastic flows melted glaciers, creating lahars that filled valleys downslope. Lahars move faster than lava (60+ km/h), travel farther, and are one of the most dangerous volcanic hazards. Lahars can remobilize years after eruption if heavy rainfall occurs on steep ash deposits.

#### Volcanic Ash and Gas
Volcanic ash (fine particles <63 micrometers) is abrasive and causes respiratory irritation. Ash falls from eruption columns can darken the sky at midday, closing schools/businesses. The 1991 Pinatubo ash fell across the Indian Ocean, turning day to darkness in Tagaytay (80 km away). More seriously, ash clogs machinery, contaminates water supplies, and causes respiratory problems (silicosis from chronic exposure). Volcanic gases (SO₂, CO₂, HCl) can be lethal in high concentrations. The 1986 Lake Nyos (Cameroon) CO₂ release (from a volcanic lake) asphyxiated 1,700 people in surrounding villages—the heavier CO₂ hugged the ground, displacing breathable air. Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) forms sulfate aerosols that cool the climate (as discussed in the climate section above) but cause acid rain and respiratory problems. SO₂ from volcanoes contributes to air pollution in downwind regions.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain why rhyolitic magma erupts explosively while basaltic magma erupts effusively.
2. What is a pyroclastic flow and why is it so dangerous?
3. Describe lahars and explain how a 1985 Nevado Ruiz eruption triggered catastrophic lahars.

### Mass Wasting and Slope Stability
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** mass-wasting-slope-stability
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** factor of safety · slope angle · pore pressure · landslides · debris flows
**Summary:** Gravity continuously pulls material downslope; when slope stability factors become unfavorable, mass wasting (landslides, debris flows) occurs, threatening people and infrastructure.

#### Factor of Safety and Slope Stability
Slope stability is governed by: resisting force (friction, cohesion) vs. driving force (gravity component down slope). Factor of Safety (FoS) = resisting force / driving force. FoS > 1 = stable, FoS < 1 = unstable, FoS ≈ 1 = critical. For a simple slope: FoS = (c + γ_n × tan φ) / (γ_sat × sin θ cos θ), where c is cohesion, γ_n is normal stress, φ is friction angle, γ_sat is saturated weight, θ is slope angle. Increasing slope angle decreases FoS (steeper = less stable). Increasing pore pressure (water saturation) decreases normal stress and FoS (wetness = less stable). Cohesion (binding strength) decreases with weathering (fresh rock > weathered rock). The Atterberg limits describe clay behavior: liquid limit (LL) is water content at which clay flows, plastic limit (PL) is where it becomes moldable. Between PL and LL is the plastic range. Exceeding LL causes flows; clay soils near LL are at risk.

#### Types of Failures
Landslides are classified by movement type: (1) **Falls** (free fall from cliff), (2) **Slides** (shear failure on discrete surface—rotational slumps or translational blocks), (3) **Flows** (plastic deformation without discrete surface—mudflows, debris flows, earth flows), (4) **Complex** (combination of types). Rotational failures (slumps) are common in clay slopes; the failure surface is curved, soil rotates into failure. Translational failures occur on pre-existing surfaces (faults, bedding) or at weak layer boundaries. Debris flows (rapid flows of soil/rock/vegetation) are triggered by heavy rainfall in steep terrain. The 2017 Montecito debris flows (California) killed 23 people; a wildfire had burned vegetation stabilizing slopes, and heavy rain triggered flows. The Philippine typhoon season triggers thousands of landslides annually; terrain is steep and prone to saturation.

#### Triggering Mechanisms
Landslides are triggered by: (1) **rainfall** (increasing pore pressure, decreasing FoS), (2) **earthquakes** (vibration reducing friction, stress redistribution), (3) **anthropogenic factors** (excavation at toe reducing resistance, loading on top, deforestation removing root reinforcement). Heavy rainfall for hours to days causes failures; antecedent moisture (soil already saturated) increases susceptibility. Seasonal patterns: Hawaii gets landslides in winter (wet season); Asian monsoons trigger failures during rainy seasons. Earthquake-triggered landslides occur within minutes; proximity to epicenter matters—strongest shaking triggers most landslides. A Mw 7.5 earthquake in steep terrain can trigger 10,000+ landslides.

#### Mitigation Strategies
Landslide hazard maps identify high-risk areas using: (1) historical failures, (2) terrain analysis (slope angle, aspect, curvature), (3) geology (weak layers, fractures), (4) hydrology (drainage, saturation). Zoning restricts development in high-hazard areas. Active mitigation: (1) **drainage** (reduce pore pressure via ditches, drilling), (2) **retaining walls/anchors** (stabilize slopes mechanically), (3) **revegetation** (increase root reinforcement), (4) **slope reduction** (excavate top, reduce angle). Early warning systems based on rainfall thresholds (if rainfall exceeds historical max-safe, issue warning) can evacuate people before failure. Japan has extensive warning systems due to frequent typhoon-triggered landslides. However, landslide prediction is imprecise—many potential slopes don't fail even when thresholds are exceeded.

### Floods and Flood Hazards
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** floods-flood-hazards
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** discharge · flood recurrence intervals · floodplain development · flood mitigation
**Summary:** Floods are the most frequent natural disaster, causing more deaths and economic damage than other hazards; understanding discharge and recurrence intervals aids prediction and mitigation.

#### Discharge and Flow Dynamics
River discharge (Q) is flow rate (m³/s) = velocity × cross-sectional area. During floods, discharge increases 10–100 fold above normal (base flow). A ~50-year flood for a large river might have discharge 2–3× the annual peak; a 100-year flood 3–4×. At peak discharge, water overflows banks and inundates floodplain. Flow energy (power) = ρ × g × Q × slope, so high discharge and steep slope produce high energy, causing erosion and damage. Manning's equation relates discharge, channel geometry, and slope; from this, hydraulic models predict flood extent and depth for given discharge. Large floods create waves (flood surge) traveling downstream; the 2010 Pakistan floods created a 6-meter surge that destroyed villages 100 km downstream. Flood velocity depends on channel slope and roughness: steep channels with low roughness (bedrock) transmit floods quickly; flat, vegetated floodplains slow flows and reduce peak discharge downstream.

#### Flood Recurrence Intervals and Probability
The 100-year flood is the discharge with 1% annual probability of exceedance (1/100 chance yearly). In a 50-year period, the probability of experiencing a 100-year flood is ~39% (not guaranteed to occur). Conversely, a 10-year flood (10% annual probability) is likely to occur multiple times in 50 years (~99% probability). These statistics are based on historical discharge records, assuming stationarity (past = future). However, climate change and land-use change alter discharge distributions—future 100-year floods might be more frequent (non-stationary). Building codes often require structures to withstand 100-year flood depth; in flood-prone regions, buildings are elevated on stilts or designed to "wet floodproofs" (allow water entry with minimal damage).

#### Floodplain Development and Disaster Risk
Historically, floodplains were natural flood storage areas—excess discharge spreads laterally, reducing peak and slowing propagation downstream. As floodplains are developed (levees, dikes, buildings), this storage is lost. Levees confine water to a narrower channel, increasing velocity and peak discharge downstream (pushing the problem downstream). The Mississippi River is highly leveed; a 100-year flood reaches higher stages than in pre-development times despite similar discharge—levees have increased flood hazard downstream. The 2011 Thailand floods inundated Bangkok despite dikes, because dikes upstream forced water downstream. Sustainable approaches: (1) restore floodplain wetlands (absorb flood water), (2) remove dikes (allow lateral spreading), (3) implement retention ponds/wetlands upstream (buffer peak discharge). These "nature-based solutions" are gaining favor but compete with development interests.

#### Compound Events and Climate Impacts
Recent flood disasters often involve compound events: rainfall + high tide + storm surge + snowmelt. The 2016 Louisiana floods combined heavy rain + high tide + surging, causing historic inundation. Climate change increases: (1) extreme rainfall intensity (Clausius-Clapeyron: warmer air holds 7% more moisture per °C, intensifying storms), (2) snowpack loss (shifts runoff to winter, increasing winter flood risk), (3) sea level rise (amplifying coastal/tidal river flooding). Projections: flood frequency increases 10–100% by 2100 in many regions. However, streamflow (total water from snow + rain) changes regionally—some areas get wetter (more runoff), others drier. Adaptation requires updated flood hazard maps reflecting future discharge, not historical statistics.
