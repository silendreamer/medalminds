## Subtopic: Ocean Basins & Seafloor Spreading

### Plate Tectonics and Seafloor Spreading
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** plate-tectonics-seafloor-spreading
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** mid-ocean ridges · seafloor spreading rates · magnetic reversals · plate boundaries
**Summary:** Oceanic crust forms at mid-ocean ridges through seafloor spreading, creating new oceanic lithosphere and driving plate motion.

#### Mid-Ocean Ridge Geometry
Mid-ocean ridges are underwater mountain ranges where new oceanic crust forms through the upwelling of hot mantle material. The ridge system extends ~80,000 km globally, connecting all ocean basins. Ridges are characterized by a central rift valley (where the plate splits) flanked by steep walls. The East Pacific Rise (Pacific Ocean) spreads at ~15 cm/year (fast), the Mid-Atlantic Ridge at ~2.5 cm/year (slow), and the Indian Ocean Ridge at ~5 cm/year (intermediate). Slow-spreading ridges are rougher with pronounced rift valleys; fast-spreading ridges are smoother with less prominent rifts. The ridge bathymetry (depth) reflects thermal structure—young crust near the ridge is hot and buoyant (shallow, ~2.5 km depth), while older crust cools and contracts with distance from ridge (deeper, ~6 km).

#### Seafloor Spreading Mechanism
New oceanic crust forms as magma rises at the ridge axis, crystallizes, and is pushed aside by continued magma input. Basaltic magma (iron and magnesium-rich) cools from the sides and top, forming the oceanic crust. The process is continuous: at fast-spreading ridges, crustal generation rates support seafloor movement. Spreading rates are determined by radiometric dating of the oceanic crust and paleomagnetic reversals. The average crustal thickness is ~6–7 km (compared to continental ~35 km), and composition is primarily basalt and gabbro. Hydrothermal circulation in ridge-proximal crust cools the crust and creates submarine hot springs ("black smokers") rich in dissolved metals and chemicals. These hydrothermal vents support unique chemosynthetic ecosystems independent of sunlight.

#### Magnetic Anomalies and Reversal Chronology
As new oceanic crust forms, it cools through the Curie temperature (~580°C), at which point magnetic minerals freeze in the ambient magnetic field. Earth's magnetic field reverses polarity irregularly—on average every 200,000–300,000 years, though intervals vary from tens of thousands to millions of years. When reversals occur, new crust records the opposite magnetic polarity. This creates striped magnetic anomalies parallel to ridges: normal-polarity stripes alternate with reversed-polarity stripes. These "barcode" patterns can be matched to the magnetic reversal timescale (determined from magnetostratigraphy of dated rock sequences and radiometric ages). Seafloor spreading rates are calculated by measuring stripe widths and dividing by the time interval of the polarity zone. The latest major reversal occurred ~780,000 years ago (Brunhes-Matuyama reversal).

#### Plate Tectonic Implications
Seafloor spreading rate determines plate motion rates. Plates move 1–18 cm/year globally. The Pacific Plate moves ~11 cm/year westward; the North American Plate moves ~2–3 cm/year westward. Where plates diverge (spread), new lithosphere forms (constructive margin). Where plates converge, one plate subducts beneath another (destructive margin). Where plates slide past each other, transform faults form (conservative margin). The ridge axis marks the plate boundary; crust progressively ages away from the ridge. The oldest oceanic crust is ~200 million years (central Pacific), limiting how far plates can be pushed before subducting. This age-area relationship is a key constraint on plate motion.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain how magnetic reversals recorded in oceanic crust allow dating and measurement of seafloor spreading rates.
2. Why does fast-spreading ridge topography differ from slow-spreading ridge topography?
3. If the Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads at 2.5 cm/year, how far apart will two points on opposite sides of the ridge be after 10 million years?

### Ocean Basin Evolution and Passive Margins
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ocean-basin-evolution-passive-margins
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** continental rifting · passive margins · subsidence · sediment transport · isostasy
**Summary:** Passive margins form at continental edges where plates diverge; they cool and subside over millions of years, creating sites of thick sediment accumulation.

#### Continental Rifting and Breakup
Continents rift when tensional stresses overcome lithospheric strength. Crustal extension thins the continental crust, causing isostatic subsidence—cooler, denser mantle material replaces buoyant crustal material. As the crust thins, rift basins form (e.g., East African Rift). Continued extension can rupture continents completely, creating a new ocean basin (spreading center). The Atlantic Ocean formed from Pangaea breakup ~200 million years ago, with rift zones in eastern North America and western Africa. Evidence for breakup includes: matching fossils on opposite continents (Pangaea evidence), paleomagnetic poles that coincide for different continents at the same age, complementary coastlines (Africa's west coast fits North America's east coast—Wegener's observation).

#### Passive Margin Structure
After continental breakup, a new spreading center forms offshore. Passive margins are non-plate-boundary margins where oceanic crust meets continental crust without active tectonics (earthquakes are rare). The continental margin consists of: (1) continental shelf (shallow water, ~200 m deep, gentle slope extending 10–100+ km offshore), (2) continental slope (steeper descent from shelf break to abyssal plain, ~200–3,000 m depth), (3) continental rise (subtle slope from base of slope to abyssal plain). Sediment from continents is transported by rivers and gravity flows, accumulating on margins. The shelf is covered by sediment to ~100 m thickness. The slope and rise have much thicker sequences (kilometers of sediment). These sediment wedges are sites of petroleum and natural gas accumulation (exploration targets).

#### Subsidence and Thermal Structure
Oceanic crust formed at the ridge is ~1,250°C; as it moves away from the ridge, it cools by conduction, contract, and is denser than ridge-proximal crust. This temperature-driven subsidence follows a diffusion model: depth increases with square root of age (d ≈ 350√t_Ma meters, where t_Ma is age in millions of years). Young ocean crust (5 Ma) is ~1.5 km deep; 100 Ma crust is ~6 km deep. This subsidence loads the underlying mantle, causing further subsidence (lithospheric flexure). The continental margin—a transition from continental to oceanic crust—develops a complex thermal structure. Geothermal gradients are high along young ocean crust (>25°C/km), promoting organic matter maturation. This is why petroleum is abundant in young passive margins (e.g., Gulf of Mexico, where crust is ~150 Ma, producing vast hydrocarbon reservoirs).

#### Sediment Architecture
Passive margins develop a characteristic stratigraphy: a rift phase (syn-rift sediments in half-grabens), a transition phase (early post-rift), and a mature phase (younger passive margin with thick sediment wedges). Seismic reflection data shows layered sediments with angular unconformities recording subsidence events. Deltaic sediments from river input build seaward-stepping clinoforms (sediment dips progressively seaward with age). Turbidite flows deliver sediment far basinward into the abyssal plain. The balance between sediment supply and accommodation (subsidence) determines margin geometry: high sediment input fills accommodation quickly (progradational geometry), while low sediment input with continued subsidence creates retrogradational (backstepping) patterns. Paleocene-Eocene margin evolution in the Atlantic shows prograding deltas; Miocene patterns show backstepping as subsidence slowed.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain why passive margins subside over time despite being far from plate boundaries.
2. Why are passive margins sites of petroleum accumulation?
3. Draw a cross-section of a passive margin showing continental shelf, slope, and rise; label sediment thickness changes and explain the cause.

### Submarine Canyons and Turbidite Systems
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** submarine-canyons-turbidites
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** submarine canyons · turbidity currents · turbidites · abyssal plains · sediment transport
**Summary:** Submarine canyons deliver sediment to abyssal plains via turbidity currents, creating distinctive turbidite deposits and shaping deep-sea bathymetry.

#### Formation and Morphology
Submarine canyons are steep-sided valleys on continental margins, often aligned with major river mouths (sourcing sediment). The Grand Canyon analogue, Monterey Canyon (off central California), extends ~150 km from shelf to abyssal plain with relief >2 km. Canyons form through: (1) headward erosion from turbidity currents, (2) direct river drainage extending to shelf edge (some canyons incised during lower sea level stands), (3) mass wasting. Canyons serve as sediment conduits—they concentrate sediment flows and deliver material to the deep sea that would otherwise settle on shelves. Sediment accumulation in canyons creates unstable gradients that trigger mass failures, releasing sediment and generating turbidity currents.

#### Turbidity Currents and Flow Dynamics
Turbidity currents are gravity-driven flows of sediment-laden water denser than surrounding seawater. They initiate when sediment concentration exceeds a threshold (~0.5% by weight), creating density excess sufficient to overcome friction and flow downslope. Initial flows are often triggered by slope failure: a submarine landslide mobilizes sediment and initializes a turbidity current. The current accelerates downslope, entrains additional water and suspended sediment, and can reach speeds of 5–15 m/s (18–54 km/h). The 1929 Grand Banks earthquake (Newfoundland) triggered a major turbidity current that severed multiple submarine cables; current arrival times at cables measured speeds of 5.6 m/s. The current eventually decelerates as it reaches gentler slopes or spreads laterally, depositing sediment. Deposition occurs as flow velocity decreases—coarser sediment (sand) deposits first, finer sediment (mud) last. This produces the classic Bouma sequence (Ta through Te): massive or graded sand (Ta), lower parallel laminations (Tb), ripple cross-laminations (Tc), upper parallel laminations (Td), and pelitic mud (Te).

#### Turbidite Deposits and Recognition
Turbidites are thin deposits of sediment from single turbidity current events, typically laminated and graded. They are abundant in deep-ocean sequences and provide a record of slope instability and sediment transport history. Paleocene-Eocene turbidites in the Atlantic show increased frequency and coarser grain sizes, indicating enhanced erosion from continental uplift. Turbidite sequences can be correlated among cores—a widespread, synchronous turbidite layer (such as from a major earthquake-triggered submarine landslide) can act as a chronostratigraphic marker. The 1755 Lisbon earthquake triggered an Atlantic turbidite that can be identified across multiple deep-sea sites, allowing dating of the event.

#### Submarine Fans and Abyssal Plain Filling
At the base of continental margins, sediment-laden turbidity currents spread laterally and deposit sediment, building submarine fans—analogues to alluvial fans on land. Large rivers with high sediment loads build massive fans (e.g., Mississippi Fan, Amazon Fan, Ganges-Brahmaputra Fan). These fans have channels, levees (natural confining ridges built by repeated overspill), and lobes (distal fan areas where sediment spreads and deposits). Abyssal plains (deep ocean floor areas) accumulate thin turbidites, with individual event thicknesses of 1–10 cm. Over millions of years, these accumulate to kilometers of sediment. The abyssal plain is not homogeneous—pelagic mud (deep-sea mud from hemipelagic sedimentation and airborne particles) dominates between turbidite events, but turbidites provide coarser sediment and episodic high energy. Analyzing turbidite frequency and grain size provides a record of continental erosion history and paleoclimate (high discharge during wet periods produces larger turbidites).

#### Review Questions
1. What triggers a turbidity current and how does flow speed influence depositional grain size?
2. Describe the Bouma sequence and explain why it records decreasing flow intensity.
3. Why can widespread, synchronous turbidite layers serve as chronostratigraphic markers?

## Subtopic: Ocean Circulation (Surface & Thermohaline)

### Surface Currents and Wind-Driven Circulation
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** surface-currents-wind-driven-circulation
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** Ekman spiral · geostrophic balance · subtropical gyres · Western Boundary Currents
**Summary:** Ocean surface currents are driven by wind stress, modified by Earth's rotation (Coriolis effect) and pressure gradients, forming organized circulation patterns.

#### Ekman Spiral and Wind-Driven Flow
When wind blows over the ocean, it exerts a stress (force per unit area) on the surface water, initiating flow. The surface water accelerates in the direction of wind stress, but as it moves, the Coriolis effect deflects it to the right (NH) / left (SH). A velocity gradient develops in the upper ocean. Each deeper layer is deflected more than the layer above (by ~45°, cumulative) and moves slightly slower (due to friction). This produces the Ekman spiral—if you plot velocity vectors at different depths, they form a spiral converging to zero at depth (~100 m). The net (depth-integrated) transport is at 90° to the wind—this is Ekman transport. On a western coast with northward wind, Ekman transport is offshore, inducing upwelling. On an eastern coast, Ekman transport is onshore, suppressing upwelling.

#### Geostrophic Balance and Gyres
Far from boundaries and at depth (below ~50 m where wind stress is negligible), ocean flow is in geostrophic balance: pressure gradient force equals Coriolis force, and flow is parallel to isobars. Subtropical high-pressure atmospheric systems create an ocean surface elevation dome (higher sea level due to wind convergence and reduced evaporation). This elevated sea level slopes downward away from the dome center. The pressure gradient drives geostrophic flow around the high (clockwise in NH, counterclockwise in SH)—creating subtropical gyres. The gyres are the largest oceanic circulation features, spanning ocean basins. The North Atlantic Gyre, North Pacific Gyre, South Atlantic Gyre, South Pacific Gyre, and Indian Ocean Gyre are the main five. Gyre flow is slow (1–5 cm/s in the interior) but transport is large due to enormous area.

#### Western Boundary Currents
A striking feature of subtropical gyres is the intensification of currents on the western boundary—the Gulf Stream (Atlantic), Kuroshio Current (Pacific), Agulhas Current (Indian Ocean). These western boundary currents are narrow (50–100 km), fast (100–150 cm/s), and deep (reaching 1,000+ m). They transport enormous water volumes (Gulf Stream: 30 million m³/s, comparable to ~1,000 Amazon Rivers). This intensification (narrowness and speed) is a consequence of beta-plane dynamics and friction: on a rotating Earth, the Coriolis parameter varies with latitude (f = 2Ω sinφ), affecting flow structure. Mathematical analysis shows that western boundary currents develop to balance interior Ekman transport divergence with western boundary mass convergence. Observationally, western boundary currents are conspicuous: satellites detect their warm water signatures, their fronts are sites of high eddy activity, and they strongly influence climate (Gulf Stream warmth sustains Northern Europe).

#### Eastern Boundary Currents and Upwelling
Eastern boundaries feature cold-water currents (California Current, Peru Current, Benguela Current, Canary Current) flowing poleward toward the equator (with Ekman transport offshore, inducing upwelling). These currents are broad and slow—essentially the return flow of subtropical gyre circulation. The California Current brings cold water from north to south; in the northern part (off Oregon/Washington), this cold water is nutrient-rich, supporting high productivity. The Peru Current (Humboldt Current) is one of the world's most productive ecosystems—cold, upwelled water supports enormous plankton blooms and fisheries. El Niño weakens this upwelling when trade winds weaken, causing catastrophic fishery collapses and redistributing global wealth from fishermen to El Niño–driven climate anomalies.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain the Ekman spiral and why net wind-driven transport is perpendicular to wind direction.
2. Why do western boundary currents intensify relative to interior gyre flow?
3. How do eastern boundary current upwelling dynamics differ from the interior gyre?

### Eddy Formation and Mesoscale Circulation
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** eddies-mesoscale-circulation
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** eddy formation · geostrophic eddies · eddy kinetic energy · mesoscale stirring
**Summary:** Mesoscale eddies (10–100 km diameter) form from instability of currents and contribute significantly to heat and mass transport.

#### Eddy Genesis
Eddies form through barotropic and baroclinic instability. Barotropic instability occurs when a strong flow develops velocity shear (velocity varies perpendicular to flow). This shear converts mean kinetic energy into eddy kinetic energy. The Gulf Stream's strong velocity gradient creates shear instability, producing Gulf Stream rings and eddies visible in satellite imagery. Baroclinic instability occurs when temperature and density gradients exist (stratification is unstable). Sloping isopycnals (surfaces of equal density) can become overturned by perturbations, converting available potential energy into kinetic energy. Baroclinic eddies are among the most energetic features in the ocean. The instability grows exponentially over days to weeks, reaching saturation when eddy scale equals the deformation radius (~50 km). Both processes produce cyclonic (rotating counterclockwise in NH) and anticyclonic (clockwise) eddies with opposing rotational senses but similar size.

#### Structure and Dynamics
Mature eddies have circular or elliptical shapes with diameter 10–100 km. Cyclonic eddies have cold water in the center (upwelled along the eddy boundary), while anticyclonic eddies have warm centers (downwelled). Eddy rotation is geostrophic (pressure gradient balanced by Coriolis). The maximum velocity is at the eddy boundary (~10–100 cm/s), with weaker circulation inside. Eddies persist weeks to years—anticyclonic eddies in the Gulf of Mexico have lifespans >1 year and translate westward (toward shallower water). Satellite altimetry maps sea surface height, detecting eddies as bumps (anticyclonic) or dips (cyclonic) of 5–50 cm—resolving thousands of eddies globally at any time.

#### Heat and Mass Transport
Eddies transport heat by moving warm or cold water masses. Anticyclonic eddies shedding from the Gulf Stream carry warm, salty water southwestward into the Sargasso Sea. This heat transport is comparable in magnitude to mean current transport: eddies alone account for ~20–50% of total heat transport in regions of high eddy activity. Eddies also mix properties—as eddies interact and decay, they redistribute tracer concentrations. An anticyclonic eddy in the Gulf Stream's thermocline (density-stratified region) carries a water mass (specific temperature, salinity) into surrounding water of different properties, spreading that signature. Eddy-induced stirring is the primary mechanism for mesoscale mixing in the ocean.

#### Practical Applications
Understanding eddies is crucial for: (1) ocean forecasting (where will an eddy translate?), (2) transport and pollutant tracking (an eddy carries a pollutant parcel hundreds of km), (3) biological productivity (eddy upwelling brings nutrients, supporting plankton blooms), (4) climate modeling (models must parameterize eddy effects if resolution doesn't resolve them). Satellites (altimetry) routinely track eddies, providing real-time forecasts useful for fisheries (where to find fish schools associated with productivity fronts) and shipping (avoiding eddies in strong current regions).

#### Review Questions
1. What mechanisms generate ocean eddies and how do they grow?
2. Explain why anticyclonic eddies have warm centers and cyclonic eddies have cold centers.
3. How do eddies contribute to heat transport and mesoscale mixing?

## Subtopic: Ocean Chemistry & Physical Properties

### Salinity, Temperature, and Density Stratification
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** salinity-temperature-density-stratification
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** salinity · temperature structure · density · thermocline · pycnocline
**Summary:** Ocean water properties (salinity, temperature, density) vary with depth, creating density stratification that affects mixing, circulation, and sound propagation.

#### Salinity Distribution
Ocean salinity is ~35 practical salinity units (psu), but varies from ~0 psu in river mouths to ~40+ psu in evaporative basins (Red Sea, Persian Gulf, Mediterranean). The convention: salinity = mass of dissolved salt (grams) per kilogram of seawater. Historically measured by chlorinity (chloride ion concentration), salinity is now measured by conductivity. Salinity varies with evaporation/precipitation and freshwater input. The tropics have high evaporation and high salinity (>35 psu). Polar regions have low salinity from ice melt and high precipitation. River discharge creates fresh plumes (salinity <30 psu) extending tens to hundreds of kilometers offshore. The Amazon River discharge freshens the North Atlantic surface ~1,000 km offshore. Mixing with open ocean gradually increases salinity of river plumes. Salinity affects density—at constant temperature, higher salinity creates denser water. This density difference drives estuarine circulation: fresh river water (light) floats over denser ocean water, creating a horizontal density gradient that drives flow.

#### Temperature Structure
Sea surface temperature varies 0°C (polar) to 30°C (tropics), with largest gradients in subtropical transitions. Temperature typically decreases rapidly in the thermocline (roughly 200–1,000 m depth), where temperature change per depth interval is steepest (>1°C per 100 m). Below the thermocline, temperature decreases slowly to bottom temperatures of ~0–4°C globally (cold abyssal water). The thermocline's depth and sharpness vary: tropical thermoclines are ~50 m thick and deep (~500 m); polar regions have weak/absent thermoclines because water temperature is uniformly cold. The thermocline depth is dynamically important: its shoaling/deepening changes mixed layer depth, affecting upwelling potential and ocean-atmosphere heat exchange. El Niño deepens the thermocline in the eastern Pacific, suppressing upwelling; La Niña shoals the thermocline, enhancing upwelling.

#### Density and Stratification
Water density depends on both temperature and salinity (via the equation of state). Cold, salty water is denser than warm, fresh water. Density increases with depth; this is stable stratification (denser water below, lighter above). Stable stratification resists mixing—a parcel displaced downward encounters denser ambient water, providing restoring buoyancy that pushes it back up. This gravity-based resistance to overturning is the Brunt-Väisälä frequency (N), a measure of stratification strength. Strong stratification (N high) means strong resistance to vertical mixing. Weak stratification (N low) allows easier mixing. Polar regions with cold surface water have weak stratification; tropics with sharp thermoclines have strong stratification. The pycnocline is the depth interval of steepest density change, typically coincident with thermocline but offset if salinity changes are large.

#### Implications for Mixing and Biological Productivity
Strong density stratification (thick thermocline) isolates surface warm water from deep cold water, reducing nutrient flux from deep ocean. Nutrients are depleted in warm surface layers by phytoplankton uptake, and can't be easily replenished from below. This creates a "nutrient trap" limiting productivity. Weak stratification (shallow thermocline) allows easier mixing and upwelling, bringing nutrients to surface. This is why polar regions and upwelling zones have high productivity despite cold temperatures (which slow biological rates); nutrient supply compensates. When spring heating establishes a thermocline (seasonal stratification), phytoplankton growth is initially limited (spring bloom requires both stratification for light exposure and residual nutrients). As the growing season progresses, nutrients deplete and productivity declines unless upwelling resupplies them. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for fisheries forecasting.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain why salinity varies with latitude and how freshwater input affects estuarine salinity structure.
2. Define thermocline and explain why it resists vertical mixing.
3. Why do upwelling zones have high biological productivity despite cold temperatures?

### Ocean Acidification and Chemical Changes
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ocean-acidification-chemical-changes
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** CO2 solubility · carbonate chemistry · pH · saturation state · shell dissolution
**Summary:** Rising atmospheric CO2 dissolves in seawater, lowering pH and reducing the saturation state of carbonate minerals, threatening calcifying organisms.

#### CO2 Solubility and the Carbonate System
When CO2 dissolves in seawater, it forms carbonic acid (H2CO3), which partially dissociates into bicarbonate (HCO3⁻) and carbonate (CO3²⁻) ions. The equilibrium: CO2(g) + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H⁺ + HCO3⁻ ⇌ 2H⁺ + CO3²⁻. The distribution among species depends on pH: at pH ~8.2 (typical seawater), bicarbonate dominates (~90%), with smaller fractions of carbonate (~10%) and dissolved CO2 (~1%). As pH decreases (acidification), bicarbonate increases and carbonate decreases. Pre-industrial pH was ~8.21; today it's ~8.10—a 0.11 unit decrease representing ~30% increase in H⁺ concentration (pH is log scale: ΔpH = -Δlog[H⁺]). The rate of acidification (~0.002 pH units per decade) is ~100× faster than natural rates, stressing organisms.

#### Saturation State and Shell Dissolution
Calcifying organisms (corals, mollusks, pteropods, planktic foraminifera) precipitate CaCO3 (calcium carbonate) to build shells/skeletons. The saturation state (Ω) = [Ca²⁺][CO3²⁻]/Ksp, where Ksp is the solubility product. When Ω > 1, the water is supersaturated and CaCO3 precipitates (shell growth); when Ω < 1, water is undersaturated and CaCO3 dissolves (shells dissolve). As pH decreases (acidification), [CO3²⁻] decreases, and Ω decreases. The surface ocean has been supersaturated (Ω ≈ 4–5) for millennia. But if CO2 continues to rise, the surface could become undersaturated (Ω < 1) within 50–100 years. This is the "carbonate saturation horizon"—the depth where water transitions from supersaturated to undersaturated. Currently this horizon is ~1,000 m globally, but in the Southern Ocean and Arctic, it's shallow (~500 m) due to high CO2 solubility in cold water and upwelled acidic water. In shallow undersaturated waters, existing shells can dissolve.

#### Case Studies of Acidification Impacts
Pteropods (sea butterflies) are small mollusks that form shells. Lab experiments show pteropod shells become pitted and corroded in low-Ω conditions. Field observations in the Southern Ocean (where Ω is already low due to upwelling and temperature) find pteropods with dissolving shells. Coral bleaching (triggered by heat stress) is compounded by acidification—corals have reduced calcification rates in low-pH conditions, hampering recovery after bleaching. Larval oysters and other shellfish have reduced settlement success in acidified conditions (lower pH prevents larvae from developing the sensory cues to settle). Planktic foraminifera—crucial food for fish larvae—have reduced shell mass and calcification in acidification experiments. The Arctic is particularly vulnerable: melting sea ice freshens surface water (lower salinity, lower Ω), cold temperatures increase CO2 solubility (lower Ω), and upwelling brings deep, acidic water (low Ω). Some Arctic regions already have Ω < 1 surface conditions seasonally.

#### Adaptation and Evolutionary Questions
Some organisms show adaptive responses to acidification (e.g., sea urchin larvae produced by acidification-acclimated adults show greater tolerance to low pH). Others show limited adaptive capacity. The question of whether adaptation can keep pace with acidification rate remains open—the current rate (~0.002 pH units/decade) is extremely rapid on evolutionary timescales. Organism with short generation times (bacteria, some plankton) can evolve faster than long-lived species (corals, fish). Most research suggests organism-level adaptation is insufficient; ecosystem-level shifts (species replacement) are more likely.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain how CO2 dissolution and the carbonate system lead to ocean acidification.
2. Define saturation state and explain why undersaturated conditions cause shell dissolution.
3. Why is the Southern Ocean particularly vulnerable to acidification impacts?

### Ocean Oxygen and Dead Zones
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ocean-oxygen-dead-zones
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** oxygen minimum zones · denitrification · eutrophication · hypoxia · dead zones
**Summary:** Oxygen minimum zones form in stratified waters where oxygen depletion occurs; nutrient enrichment (eutrophication) expands these zones, creating "dead zones" hostile to most marine life.

#### Oxygen Distribution and Minimum Zones
Oxygen enters the ocean through diffusion at the sea surface and is consumed by respiration throughout the water column. Deep water (>2,000 m) has very low oxygen (~2–5 mL/L) because it forms far from the surface and oxygen-poor waters remain isolated by stratification. Intermediate depths (~200–1,000 m) can develop oxygen minimum zones (OMZ) where oxygen falls below detection limits. OMZs occur in: (1) poorly ventilated basins (Mediterranean, Black Sea, some fjords), (2) open ocean regions with strong oxygen utilization (Arabian Sea, eastern Pacific). These regions have thick stratification (trapping oxygen-poor water) and/or reduced inflow of fresh oxygen-rich water. The Black Sea is permanently anoxic (zero oxygen) below ~200 m due to strong salinity stratification and high respiration.

#### Denitrification and Fixed Nitrogen Loss
In anoxic zones, heterotrophic bacteria use nitrate (NO3⁻) as an electron acceptor instead of oxygen, reducing it to nitrogen gas (N2). This process (denitrification) releases fixed nitrogen (nutrient form) as inert N2 gas, removing it from the marine biologically available pool. In regions of strong OMZs, denitrification is rapid and can remove 10–50% of the nitrate produced by nitrification (oxidation of ammonia to nitrate). The Arabian Sea OMZ removes ~100 Tg N/year through denitrification—a massive loss of nutrient nitrogen. This affects downstream productivity: water sinking into the OMZ loses nitrate, and upwelling in the eastern Pacific or Arabian Sea brings nitrate-depleted water, limiting productivity despite other favorable upwelling conditions.

#### Eutrophication and Coastal Dead Zones
Excess nutrient input from rivers (nitrogen from fertilizer, sewage) stimulates algal growth. The algae eventually die, sink, and decompose at depth. Decomposition consumes oxygen. In stratified coastal systems, oxygen can't be replenished from above (stratification blocks mixing), and oxygen depletion creates hypoxic zones (oxygen <2 mg/L, unsuitable for most fish). Severe hypoxia (anoxia, <0.5 mg/L) creates "dead zones" where only specialized anaerobic microbes survive. The Gulf of Mexico dead zone (fed by Mississippi River nutrients) grows each summer when discharge is highest, shrinking each winter when stratification weakens. The zone has reached 20,000+ km² in area—roughly the size of New Jersey. Impacts: fish cannot survive, benthic invertebrates die, and only resistant organisms (jellyfish, bacteria) remain. These zones threaten fisheries and ecosystem services.

#### Global Patterns and Climate Connections
Globally, ~400+ coastal dead zones exist, with growing frequency. Climate change exacerbates eutrophication: (1) warming enhances stratification, isolating surface water from oxygen replenishment, (2) altered river discharge patterns change nutrient loading, (3) warmer water holds less oxygen. Model projections suggest a ~4% decrease in global ocean oxygen and expansion of OMZs by 10–50% by 2100. Some regions (North Atlantic, Arctic) could see less expansion due to changes in circulation. Other regions (tropical Pacific, Indian Ocean) could see severe expansion. This poses risks to marine biodiversity and fisheries.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain how strong density stratification promotes oxygen depletion in intermediate depths.
2. Describe the process of denitrification and its impact on nutrient cycling.
3. Why does eutrophication from river nutrient input create coastal dead zones in stratified systems?

## Subtopic: Waves, Tides & Coastal Processes

### Wave Mechanics and Propagation
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** wave-mechanics-propagation
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** wave characteristics · dispersion · shallow water waves · wave energy
**Summary:** Ocean waves are generated by wind and propagate according to wave mechanics; their properties depend on wavelength, depth, and propagation distance.

#### Wave Parameters and Classification
Surface waves are characterized by wavelength (λ, distance between crests), period (T, time between crest passages), amplitude (A, half-height from trough to crest), and water depth (h). The wave number (k = 2π/λ) and angular frequency (ω = 2π/T) define wave properties. The dispersion relationship (relation between ω and k) depends on depth:
- **Deep water** (h > λ/2): ω² = gk, so v_phase = ω/k = √(gλ/2π) — phase speed increases with wavelength. Large waves move faster than small waves.
- **Shallow water** (h < λ/20): ω² = gk²h, so v_phase = √(gh) — phase speed is independent of wavelength, depending only on depth.
- **Intermediate water** (λ/20 < h < λ/2): dispersion relation is intermediate, requiring numerical solution.

#### Wind-Generated Waves
Waves form when wind transfers energy to the ocean surface. The wave age (ratio of wave speed to wind speed) determines if waves are "young" (still accelerating from wind) or "fully developed" (maximum height for given wind speed). Young, growing waves have steeper slopes and higher energy transfer from wind. Once developed, waves cease growing even with continued wind. Fetch (distance over which wind blows) and duration (how long wind blows) determine final wave height. A storm with 40-knot winds over 500 km fetch for 12 hours produces significant wave height ~5–6 meters. Longer fetch or longer duration increases wave height. After generation, waves propagate far from the source, appearing as "swell" (organized waves with long periods, ~10–20 seconds) on distant beaches. Swell can travel thousands of kilometers; a storm-generated swell can cross the Pacific in ~1 week.

#### Group Velocity and Wave Packets
In deep water, individual wave crests move at the phase velocity (v_p = √(gλ/2π)) but the energy (contained in the wave packet) moves at group velocity (v_g = v_p/2 in deep water). This means the envelope of waves (the group) moves at half the crest speed. This surprising result occurs because deeper water waves disperse (slow waves cluster behind fast waves, causing slow crests to appear to emerge from and disappear into the tail of the group). In shallow water (v_p = √(gh)), group velocity equals phase velocity (v_g = v_p). This dispersion has implications: storm-generated swell separates into frequency components during propagation, with high-frequency (short-period) waves arriving first (thinning of wave spectrum with distance), and low-frequency waves arriving last but with higher amplitude.

#### Wave Energy and Breaking
Wave energy per unit area is E = (ρg/8)H², where H is significant wave height. Energy is proportional to amplitude squared. A 1-meter wave has 8× energy of a 0.5-meter wave. Wave energy is transported by group velocity. On reaching shallow water, waves "feel the bottom" (at h ≈ λ/2) and their propagation changes: phase velocity decreases (√gh decreases as h decreases), wavelength shortens, and amplitude increases (energy conservation—same energy in shorter wavelength means higher amplitude). Wave steepness (H/λ) increases; when H/λ > 1/7 (in deep water) or H/h > 0.78 (in shallow water), wave becomes unstable and breaks. Breaking waves dissipate energy as heat and turbulence, driving sediment transport and coastal erosion.

### Tides and Tidal Forces
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** tides-tidal-forces
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** tidal potential · lunar and solar tides · spring and neap tides · tidal constituents
**Summary:** Tides result from the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun on the oceans, creating periodic sea level changes with predictable cycles.

#### Tidal Forces and Potential
Tides arise from the differential gravitational force of the Moon (and Sun) across Earth's diameter. The Moon pulls on the near side of Earth with greater force than on the far side (inverse square law: F ∝ 1/r²). This differential creates a tidal force. The classical explanation: two bulges form (one on the near side toward the Moon, one on the far side away), creating two high tides and two low tides per lunar day (~24.84 hours). However, self-gravitation of water and Earth's response complicate this picture. Modern tidal theory uses the tidal potential—the gravitational potential due to lunar/solar position. The potential's spatial variation drives tidal currents and sea level changes.

#### Lunar and Solar Tides
The Moon dominates tidal forcing (the lunar tidal amplitude is ~2.4× the solar tidal amplitude) because despite the Sun being much more massive, the Moon is much closer. Lunar tides cycle every 12 hours 25 minutes (M2 constituent, the dominant lunar semidiurnal tide). Solar tides cycle every 12 hours exactly (S2 constituent). When the Moon and Sun align (new and full moon), their tidal forces add, producing large tides—spring tides (tidal range is maximum). When the Moon and Sun are perpendicular (first and third quarters), their forces partially cancel, producing small tides—neap tides (tidal range is minimum). The spring/neap cycle has a ~14-day period (half the lunar month).

#### Tidal Constituents and Prediction
Tidal oscillations are sums of multiple sinusoidal constituents at different frequencies. The principal constituents: M2 (lunar semidiurnal, 12.42 h), S2 (solar semidiurnal, 12.00 h), N2 (lunar ellipticity, 12.66 h), K2 (luni-solar, 11.97 h), K1 (luni-solar diurnal, 23.93 h), O1 (lunar diurnal, 25.82 h), P1 (solar diurnal, 24.07 h). Harmonic analysis of tide gauge records extracts the amplitude and phase of each constituent. Tidal prediction then sums all constituents at any future time. This method is extremely accurate—tide predictions are accurate to ±0.1 m for months ahead. Tidal atlases provide maps of tidal amplitude and phase at grid points, allowing interpolation for any location.

#### Tidal Currents and Resonance
Tides drive horizontal currents (tidal currents). In narrow bays or straits, tidal currents can exceed 1 m/s (Saltstraumen in Norway exceeds 2 m/s). Tidal current direction rotates with the tidal cycle—it's not just back and forth (reciprocating flow) but rotates elliptically in the Northern Hemisphere (clockwise). Some regions have resonant amplification of tidal amplitude. The Bay of Fundy (Nova Scotia, Canada) has a resonant period matching the M2 tidal period, creating a standing wave. This resonance amplifies the tidal amplitude to ~15 meters—the highest tides on Earth. This dramatic amplification also produces strong tidal currents (up to 2.5 m/s), enabling tidal power extraction.

### Coastal Erosion and Sediment Transport
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** coastal-erosion-sediment-transport
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** wave-driven sediment transport · littoral drift · erosion rates · human impacts on coasts
**Summary:** Waves and currents transport sediment along coasts; imbalances in transport cause erosion or accretion, with implications for coastal hazards and infrastructure.

#### Sediment Transport Mechanisms
Breaking waves suspend sediment and generate currents that transport it. In the surf zone (where waves break), wave orbital velocities and breaking-wave-induced currents combine to transport sediment. The sediment transport is proportional to wave power (energy flux) and sediment availability. Transport direction depends on wave approach angle (Snell's law refraction causes wave rays to bend as they enter shallow water). If waves approach the shore at an oblique angle, the surf zone current has a component parallel to shore—littoral current. This current transports sediment down-drift—longshore sediment transport. The rate is: Q ≈ k × E_b × sin(2α), where E_b is breaking wave energy, α is wave approach angle, and k is a transport coefficient (~0.1–1 m³/m³ in lab experiments). Typical transport rates are 10,000–1,000,000 m³/year depending on wave climate and sediment supply.

#### Littoral Drift and Beach Evolution
Littoral drift creates downdrift erosion (sediment removed) and updrift accretion (sediment added). Jetties and breakwaters disrupt natural drift: updrift beaches accrete, downdrift beaches erode. Ocean City, Maryland has a jetty at the inlet, causing updrift accretion and downdrift erosion—creating a shoreline discontinuity. If a sediment source (river) is blocked by a dam, downdrift erosion accelerates (reduced sediment supply, continued transport). The Nile River's sediment supply to the Nile Delta dropped 99% after the Aswan High Dam was built; downdrift erosion now threatens Alexandria. Coastal engineering structures (seawalls, bulkheads) can reflect energy, increasing scour and erosion of adjacent beach. Hard defenses often cause problems elsewhere—not a solution to erosion, merely transferring erosion. Soft engineering (beach nourishment, living shorelines with native vegetation) is increasingly favored for maintaining beaches while reducing downdrift effects.

#### Erosion Rates and Coastal Hazards
Coastal erosion rates vary from <1 cm/year to >1 m/year depending on geology, wave climate, and sea level rise. Soft sediment coasts (sand and mud) erode faster (~10–100 cm/year) than rocky coasts. Sea level rise amplifies erosion by allowing storm surge to reach higher elevations—a 0.5 m rise can increase erosion rates by 50% or more. Storm surge from tropical cyclones or nor'easters exceeds predicted tide levels, delivering more energy to the coast and removing more sediment. Compound events (storm surge + high tide + river discharge + large waves) cause extreme erosion. The Norfolk, Virginia area has high erosion rates (>4 cm/year) from relative sea level rise (land subsidence + absolute sea level rise), threatening military installations. Some islands (Tuvalu, Kiribati) face existential threats from sea level rise and increased storm surge; "climate migration" will likely force relocation of populations.

#### Sediment Budgets and Coastal Management
A sediment budget tracks input and output: sediment input (river, upwind erosion, cliff collapse) minus sediment output (transport into deep ocean, human removal) equals change in beach volume. If input exceeds output, beach accretes; if output exceeds input, beach erodes. Many developed coasts have strongly negative budgets because: (1) rivers are dammed (reducing input), (2) littoral drift removes sediment (output), (3) human extraction (mining for construction) reduces inventory. Beach nourishment (adding sediment to beaches) temporarily offsets erosion but requires repeated feeding as sediment is transported away. Netherlands has maintained much of its coast through continuous nourishment and dike management, but costs are rising with sea level rise. Integrated coastal zone management (ICZM) aims to balance development with natural processes—moving development inland, allowing wetland restoration, maintaining natural sediment pathways.

## Subtopic: Marine Ecosystems

### Phytoplankton, Nutrient Cycling, and Primary Productivity
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** phytoplankton-nutrients-productivity
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** primary production · limiting nutrients · nutrient cycles · phytoplankton growth
**Summary:** Phytoplankton form the base of marine food webs, converting solar energy and inorganic nutrients into organic matter; nutrient availability limits productivity.

#### Primary Productivity and Nutrient Requirements
Phytoplankton (photosynthetic microorganisms) fix CO2 into organic carbon via photosynthesis. Primary productivity is the rate of organic matter production per area per time (g C / m² / year). Global ocean productivity varies: oligotrophic open ocean (0.1–1 g C/m²/year), mesotrophic waters (1–10 g C/m²/year), eutrophic coastal and upwelling zones (10–1,000 g C/m²/year). Phytoplankton require: light (for photosynthesis), CO2 (abundant, not limiting), macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, silica), and micronutrients (iron, manganese, zinc, etc.). Most of the ocean is limited by nitrogen (a key component of proteins and nucleic acids) or phosphorus (critical for ATP and nucleic acids). Iron is also limiting in some regions (Southern Ocean, tropical Pacific) despite low demand (used as enzyme cofactor) because dust delivery is minimal.

#### Redfield Ratio and Nutrient Stoichiometry
Phytoplankton require nutrients in specific ratios. The Redfield ratio (derived from analysis of plankton composition) is C:N:P ≈ 106:16:1 (by atoms). When phytoplankton die and decompose, organic matter is remineralized to inorganic nutrients in roughly this ratio. If deep water upwelling supplies nutrients in this ratio, no one nutrient limits growth. But if the supply ratio deviates (e.g., high nitrogen, low phosphorus), phosphorus becomes limiting. Much of the open ocean is nitrogen-limited because denitrification in OMZs removes fixed nitrogen, creating N/P ratios below the Redfield ratio. Conversely, freshwater systems are typically phosphorus-limited due to high N:P from terrestrial sources.

#### Seasonal Productivity Cycles
Temperate ocean productivity has strong seasonality. Winter: strong mixing replenishes nutrients but low light (high latitude, cloud cover) limits growth. Spring: warming establishes stratification, light increases, and residual nutrients drive the spring phytoplankton bloom. Early bloom species are typically diatoms (require silica for shells, abundant in winter mixing). Summer: continued warming strengthens stratification, nutrient depletion occurs, and productivity declines. Some upwelling at the thermocline edge provides small nutrient input, sustaining reduced summer productivity. Fall: cooling weakens stratification, wind-driven mixing begins to erode thermocline, and a smaller fall bloom occurs. These seasonal patterns drive zooplankton and fish recruitment dynamics—larval fish must arrive when food (plankton) is abundant.

#### Nutrient Trapping and Coastal Eutrophication
Strong density stratification can "trap" nutrients: regenerated nutrients from deep remineralization can't reach surface because stratification blocks upward mixing. This nutrient trap reduces productivity. Conversely, in stratified systems with external nutrient input (eutrophication from sewage or agricultural runoff), nutrient levels can become very high. Excessive productivity causes algal blooms, which die and deplete oxygen (hypoxia). Harmful algal blooms (HABs) can produce toxins (domoic acid from Pseudo-nitzschia, saxitoxin from Alexandrium dinoflagellates), poisoning shellfish and humans. Understanding nutrient dynamics is crucial for predicting productivity, HAB risk, and hypoxia occurrence.

### Zooplankton, Fish, and Marine Food Webs
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** zooplankton-fish-food-webs
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** trophic levels · food webs · predator-prey cycles · trophic efficiency
**Summary:** Marine food webs transfer energy from phytoplankton through zooplankton and fish to apex predators; energy is lost at each trophic transfer.

#### Zooplankton and the Microbial Loop
Zooplankton (small animals) graze on phytoplankton and bacteria. Copepods are the dominant zooplankton in most oceans, comprising ~50% of marine zooplankton biomass. Other zooplankton include larval fish, jellyfish, pteropods, and radiolarians. Grazing removes ~40–70% of daily primary production. Zooplankton excretion releases dissolved organic matter (DOM) and regenerates nutrients (completing nutrient cycles). Zooplankton fecal pellets (compact, sinking feces) are efficient vehicles for exporting carbon to depth—sinking particles carry carbon away from surface. In the microbial loop, bacteria consume DOM released by zooplankton, and protists graze bacteria. This cycles nutrients back to phytoplankton without exporting carbon. The efficiency of the microbial loop vs. direct grazing affects carbon export efficiency and deep-sea productivity.

#### Trophic Levels and Energy Transfer
Energy flows from primary producers (phytoplankton) through herbivorous zooplankton (trophic level 2) to planktivorous fish (trophic level 3) to piscivorous fish (trophic level 4). At each transfer, ~90% of energy is lost to respiration, growth inefficiency, and heat. Thus, trophic efficiency is ~10%. This means to support 1 kg of apex predator (e.g., tuna, shark), ~10 kg of planktivorous fish are needed, ~100 kg of zooplankton, and ~1,000 kg of phytoplankton. This trophic structure limits the abundance of higher trophic levels. The 10% rule implies that pyramid-shaped food webs are more stable than inverted pyramids—large biomass at the base can support small biomass at the apex.

#### Predator-Prey Dynamics
Predator-prey populations oscillate: when prey is abundant, predators increase, which depletes prey, causing predators to starve and decline, allowing prey to recover. These Lotka-Volterra oscillations are observed in many systems. In the North Pacific, zooplankton populations fluctuate with copepod-dominated and jellyfish-dominated regimes separated by decades. Regime shifts appear linked to climate (PDO phase), but mechanisms are complex. Overfishing (removing predators) can trigger regime shifts—when Atlantic cod were overfished, jellyfish and shrimp proliferated, changing the food web structure. Recovery from overfishing is slow and uncertain; some ecosystems remain locked in alternative stable states.

#### Pelagic vs. Benthic Food Webs
Pelagic food webs (in the water column) include phytoplankton → zooplankton → fish → apex predators. Benthic food webs (on the seafloor) rely on organic matter sinking from the pelagic zone or chemosynthesis (vent/seep ecosystems). In most open ocean, benthic food webs are starved (little organic matter sinks to abyssal depths), supporting low densities of benthic life. In coastal zones and upwelling regions, high productivity supplies abundant food, supporting diverse benthic communities. Hydrothermal vent communities derive energy from chemosynthetic bacteria oxidizing H2S, creating unique food webs independent of sunlight. These vent ecosystems were discovered in 1977 and revolutionized understanding of life's limits.
