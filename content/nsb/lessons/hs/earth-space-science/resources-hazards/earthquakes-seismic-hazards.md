#### Earthquake Mechanics
Earthquakes result from sudden slip on faults, releasing accumulated elastic strain. Stress accumulates as plates move and friction locks the fault. When stress exceeds rock strength, rupture initiates and propagates, releasing seismic waves. Moment magnitude (Mw) quantifies earthquake size: Mw = (2/3) × log(M₀) - 10.7, where M₀ is seismic moment (related to rigidity, area, slip distance). Unlike the older Richter scale (limited at high magnitudes, saturating at ~Ml 7.5), moment magnitude is valid across all earthquake sizes. Moment magnitude preserves information about actual rupture: Mw 7 earthquake represents ~10⁶ times more energy release than Mw 5. The energy release is: log(E) ≈ 4.8 + 1.5 × Mw (joules). A Mw 8 earthquake releases ~6 × 10¹⁶ joules—equivalent to ~1,000 Hiroshima bombs.

#### Seismic Wave Propagation
Two types of body waves propagate through the Earth: P-waves (primary, compressional, faster ~6 km/s) and S-waves (secondary, shear, slower ~3.5 km/s). P-waves cause ground motion parallel to wave propagation (push-pull); S-waves cause motion perpendicular (shaking). Surface waves (Rayleigh and Love waves) are slower but larger amplitude, causing most damage. Seismometers measure ground motion displacement, velocity, or acceleration. Strong-motion accelerometers (recording high-frequency, high-amplitude shaking) are used for engineering hazard assessment. Peak ground acceleration (PGA), spectral acceleration (Sa), and peak ground velocity (PGV) are key parameters affecting structural damage. Buildings resonate at specific frequencies (natural period); if seismic waves contain energy at the building's natural frequency, resonance amplifies shaking and can cause collapse. Soft-soil sites amplify shaking (unconsolidated sediment resonates at lower frequencies, trapping seismic energy).

#### Seismic Hazards and Ground Effects
Shaking damage varies with: (1) earthquake magnitude and proximity (larger, closer = stronger shaking), (2) site conditions (soft soil >> bedrock), (3) building design (unreinforced masonry < wood < reinforced concrete). Liquefaction occurs when strong shaking causes saturated sandy sediment to lose strength (pore pressure rises, overburden pressure drops), turning soil into liquid-like behavior. Buildings founded on liquefied soil may tilt or sink. Lateral spreads (spreading of ground toward open faces like river banks) occur on gentle slopes due to liquefaction. The 1964 Niigata earthquake (Japan) caused extensive liquefaction, tilting apartment buildings intact but unusable. Landslides are triggered by strong shaking—steep slopes become unstable. The 1999 Chi-Chi earthquake (Taiwan) triggered 20,000+ landslides, blocking valleys and creating dams. Tsunami (generated by submarine earthquakes displacing ocean floor) caused ~100x more deaths in the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake (230,000+) than the earthquake shaking itself.

#### Seismic Hazard Assessment
Seismic hazard maps show PGA or spectral acceleration exceeded with given probability in given time interval (e.g., "10% chance of exceedance in 50 years" or "475-year return period"). These maps drive building code seismic design requirements. US Seismic Hazard Maps show California, Pacific Northwest, and Intermountain West at highest risk; most of the interior US is moderate to low risk. However, surprise earthquakes occur—the 2011 Christchurch earthquake (New Zealand) caused ~$40 billion damage in a region with moderate hazard rating; poor building stock (old, unreinforced) amplified impacts. Early warning systems (detecting P-waves, broadcasting warning before S-waves arrive, typically 1–10 seconds) can trigger automated actions (trains braking, surgeries pausing), saving some lives in seconds-to-seconds decisions. However, earthquakes very close to epicenter won't have warning time.
