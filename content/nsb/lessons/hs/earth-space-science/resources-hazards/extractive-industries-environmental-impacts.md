#### Acid Mine Drainage
When sulfide minerals (pyrite, chalcopyrite) in ore deposits are exposed to oxygen and water (during mining), they oxidize, producing sulfuric acid:
2 FeS₂ + 7 O₂ + 2 H₂O → 2 Fe²⁺ + 4 SO₄²⁻ + 4 H⁺

This acidic drainage (pH 2–3) mobilizes metals dissolved in the acidic solution. Iron oxidizes to Fe³⁺, which hydrolyzes to form iron oxyhydroxide precipitate (red/orange discoloration). Copper, zinc, lead also dissolve. AMD can persist for decades or centuries; streams downgradient of mines are acidified and biologically sterile for tens of kilometers. Remediation (treating AMD to neutralize acidity and precipitate metals) is expensive—costs $1,000s per acre for active treatment. Some sites (Summitville, Colorado) required $150+ million cleanup of abandoned mining. Natural AMD occurs too (from weathering of sulfide deposits), but mining accelerates the process and concentrates drainage at specific discharge points, intensifying local impacts.

#### Tailings and Storage
Extraction produces vast quantities of tailings (ground rock after ore separation). A single copper mine might process 100,000 tonnes/day, producing 99,500 tonnes of tailings. Tailings must be stored—historically in tailings ponds behind dams. If the dam fails (rare but catastrophic), tailings spill downstream. The 2015 Samarco (Vale/BHP) dam failure in Brazil released 62 million cubic meters of tailings, destroying the Rio Doce river ecosystem and affecting 250,000 people. The 2014 Mount Polley mine tailings dam failure (Canada) released 25 million cubic meters into Polley Lake. These failures motivate better tailings management—dry stacking (mechanically compacted tailings, stack above ground) is becoming standard but more expensive. Tailings ponds remain a significant risk.

#### Habitat Loss and Biodiversity
Surface mining directly destroys habitat. Mountaintop removal mining (blasting off mountaintops to access coal in Appalachia) has flattened over 500 mountaintops, eliminating irreplaceable forest ecosystems and stream habitat. The cumulative area equals ~3,000 km². Oil and gas extraction fragmentizes habitats—drilling pads, pipelines, roads create a networked landscape hostile to wildlife. Rainforest habitat in Ecuador, Peru, and Indonesia is fragmented by oil exploration and extraction. A single well pad might seem small, but hundreds to thousands of pads create pervasive disturbance. Biodiversity loss from habitat fragmentation is difficult to quantify but known to be severe—edge effects (sun exposure, temperature increase) extend beyond the direct impact zone.

#### Land Subsidence
Extracting fluids (groundwater, petroleum, natural gas) from depth removes support for overlying rock. The sediment compacts, causing surface subsidence. Long Beach, California, subsided >9 meters over the 20th century due to oil extraction. Subsidence costs: (1) damage to built infrastructure (cracked foundations, buckled roads), (2) increased flood risk (subsided areas lie below normal drainage), (3) saltwater intrusion (subsided coastal areas allow seawater encroachment). The Netherlands has extensive subsidence from past peat extraction and groundwater withdrawal; extensive dike systems manage the resulting low-lying areas. Future subsidence is expected from CO₂ storage (storing CO₂ underground in depleted oil fields or aquifers requires accounting for pressure changes and potential subsidence).

#### Review Questions
1. Write the oxidation reaction for pyrite exposed to oxygen and water, explaining why it produces acidity.
2. Why do tailings dam failures have catastrophic impacts and how can they be prevented?
3. Explain the causes and consequences of land subsidence from resource extraction.
