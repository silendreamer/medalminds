#### Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE)
LCOE is the average cost per unit energy ($/MWh or $/kWh) over a project lifetime, accounting for capital costs, operation, maintenance, and fuel costs. In 2024: Coal LCOE ~$60–100/MWh, Gas ~$50–80/MWh, Nuclear ~$130–190/MWh, Solar ~$30–50/MWh, Wind ~$40–70/MWh. By pure cost, solar and wind are cheapest. However, LCOE alone doesn't determine total energy system cost because: (1) renewables have capacity factor <100% (solar ~20%, wind ~35%, vs. coal/gas/nuclear >80%), (2) renewables are intermittent, requiring storage/backup. Total system cost includes transmission, storage, backup generation, and grid modernization. Adding these, system cost for high-renewable penetration (~80%) is estimated $100–200/MWh in developed economies, comparable to fossil fuel systems.

#### Capacity Factor and Grid Contribution
Capacity factor (actual output / maximum theoretical output) determines energy contribution. Solar at 20% capacity means a 1 GW solar plant produces 0.2 GW average. To replace a retiring 1 GW coal plant (operating at 85% capacity, providing 0.85 GW average), you need ~4–5 GW of solar + storage/backup. Geographic variation is huge: solar in Arizona gets 25% capacity factor, solar in Germany gets 15%. Wind in the Great Plains gets 40–50% capacity factor, offshore wind 50%+. Choosing locations with high capacity factors reduces the overbuild needed. Seasonal variation is also critical: solar productivity peaks in summer (excess generation), wind peaks in winter (supply-demand mismatch in heating-dominant regions).

#### Energy Storage and Grid Integration
Intermittency is addressed through: (1) storage (batteries, pumped hydro, thermal storage), (2) demand response (shifting consumption to match supply), (3) geographic distribution (balancing wind from multiple regions), (4) backup generation (natural gas plants). Batteries are expensive (~$100–200/kWh) but costs are declining (~10% annually). A single day's storage requires ~1–5 kWh storage per kW of peak generation. Seasonal storage (months of storage for winter solar deficit) is impractical with batteries—requires alternative technologies (hydrogen, synthetic fuels, thermal storage). Pumped hydro (existing technology) provides ~90% of global energy storage but is geographically limited (requires topographic relief and water). Addressing seasonal mismatch in renewable scenarios requires long-duration storage technologies still under development.

#### Decarbonization Pathways
Achieving net-zero emissions by 2050 requires: (1) electricity decarbonization (80% renewable + nuclear by 2050), (2) electrification of transport (EVs replace ICE), (3) building efficiency (heat pumps, insulation), (4) industrial transitions (steel/cement/chemicals use hydrogen, electric furnaces, or carbon capture). The International Energy Agency projects ~90% electricity decarbonization is feasible with current technology deployed at scale. However, industrial process heat, aviation, shipping, and agriculture remain challenging. Hydrogen (produced from electrolysis using renewable electricity) can decarbonize these sectors but is expensive (~$5–10/kg presently, targets <$2/kg by 2050). Carbon capture (removing CO₂ from flue gas or directly from air) is expensive (~$100–300/tonne) and energy-intensive, limiting feasibility. Most scenarios require all three transitions (renewables + efficiency + carbon removal) for net-zero.

#### Review Questions
1. Compare LCOE for coal, nuclear, solar, and wind; explain why lowest-cost energy doesn't guarantee lowest-cost system.
2. Calculate the solar panel area needed to replace a 1 GW coal plant assuming 20% capacity factor and 20% panel efficiency.
3. What storage duration is needed to address seasonal (month-to-month) renewable variability?
