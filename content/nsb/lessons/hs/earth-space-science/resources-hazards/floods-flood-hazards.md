#### Discharge and Flow Dynamics
River discharge (Q) is flow rate (m³/s) = velocity × cross-sectional area. During floods, discharge increases 10–100 fold above normal (base flow). A ~50-year flood for a large river might have discharge 2–3× the annual peak; a 100-year flood 3–4×. At peak discharge, water overflows banks and inundates floodplain. Flow energy (power) = ρ × g × Q × slope, so high discharge and steep slope produce high energy, causing erosion and damage. Manning's equation relates discharge, channel geometry, and slope; from this, hydraulic models predict flood extent and depth for given discharge. Large floods create waves (flood surge) traveling downstream; the 2010 Pakistan floods created a 6-meter surge that destroyed villages 100 km downstream. Flood velocity depends on channel slope and roughness: steep channels with low roughness (bedrock) transmit floods quickly; flat, vegetated floodplains slow flows and reduce peak discharge downstream.

#### Flood Recurrence Intervals and Probability
The 100-year flood is the discharge with 1% annual probability of exceedance (1/100 chance yearly). In a 50-year period, the probability of experiencing a 100-year flood is ~39% (not guaranteed to occur). Conversely, a 10-year flood (10% annual probability) is likely to occur multiple times in 50 years (~99% probability). These statistics are based on historical discharge records, assuming stationarity (past = future). However, climate change and land-use change alter discharge distributions—future 100-year floods might be more frequent (non-stationary). Building codes often require structures to withstand 100-year flood depth; in flood-prone regions, buildings are elevated on stilts or designed to "wet floodproofs" (allow water entry with minimal damage).

#### Floodplain Development and Disaster Risk
Historically, floodplains were natural flood storage areas—excess discharge spreads laterally, reducing peak and slowing propagation downstream. As floodplains are developed (levees, dikes, buildings), this storage is lost. Levees confine water to a narrower channel, increasing velocity and peak discharge downstream (pushing the problem downstream). The Mississippi River is highly leveed; a 100-year flood reaches higher stages than in pre-development times despite similar discharge—levees have increased flood hazard downstream. The 2011 Thailand floods inundated Bangkok despite dikes, because dikes upstream forced water downstream. Sustainable approaches: (1) restore floodplain wetlands (absorb flood water), (2) remove dikes (allow lateral spreading), (3) implement retention ponds/wetlands upstream (buffer peak discharge). These "nature-based solutions" are gaining favor but compete with development interests.

#### Compound Events and Climate Impacts
Recent flood disasters often involve compound events: rainfall + high tide + storm surge + snowmelt. The 2016 Louisiana floods combined heavy rain + high tide + surging, causing historic inundation. Climate change increases: (1) extreme rainfall intensity (Clausius-Clapeyron: warmer air holds 7% more moisture per °C, intensifying storms), (2) snowpack loss (shifts runoff to winter, increasing winter flood risk), (3) sea level rise (amplifying coastal/tidal river flooding). Projections: flood frequency increases 10–100% by 2100 in many regions. However, streamflow (total water from snow + rain) changes regionally—some areas get wetter (more runoff), others drier. Adaptation requires updated flood hazard maps reflecting future discharge, not historical statistics.

#### Review Questions
1. Define the 100-year flood and calculate the probability of experiencing it in a 50-year lifespan.
2. Explain how levees paradoxically increase downstream flood hazard.
3. How does climate change alter future flood frequency and why historical recurrence intervals become unreliable?
