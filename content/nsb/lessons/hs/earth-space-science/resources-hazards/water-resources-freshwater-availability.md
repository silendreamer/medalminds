#### Global Water Distribution and Availability
Earth has ~1.386 billion km³ of water; only ~2.5% is freshwater, and of that, ~70% is frozen in ice sheets and glaciers. Accessible freshwater (liquid, in lakes, rivers, aquifers) is ~0.3% of total water. Per capita freshwater availability varies enormously: Canada >100,000 m³/capita/year, Egypt <1,000 m³/capita/year. The UN defines "water stress" as <1,700 m³/capita/year, and "severe water scarcity" as <500 m³/capita/year. ~4 billion people (>50% global population) experience severe water stress during part of the year. By 2050, projected population growth and climate change could increase this to ~6 billion people.

#### Aquifers and Groundwater
Groundwater (water in porous rock/sediment) is the largest source of freshwater for human use (~70% of freshwater withdrawals). Aquifers recharge from precipitation/infiltration over years to millennia, depending on depth and recharge area. Renewable aquifers have recharge rates sufficient to sustain extraction; non-renewable (fossil) aquifers accumulate over millions of years without active recharge. The Ogallala Aquifer (Great Plains, USA) has been depleted from ~300 m average thickness to <60 m in 60 years of irrigation-driven withdrawal. Its remaining lifetime is ~20–30 years if current depletion continues. The North China Plain, Indian subcontinent, and Middle East have similar aquifer depletion. Groundwater depletion has three impacts: (1) well deepening costs, (2) land subsidence (as aquifer pore space collapses), (3) freshwater-saltwater interface intrusion (in coastal regions).

#### Climate Change and Water Availability
Warming alters water cycles: (1) increased evaporation (warmer air holds more moisture), (2) changed precipitation (some regions wetter, others drier), (3) snowpack decline (shifts runoff timing, reduces storage), (4) glacier loss (eliminates buffering of seasonal flow). Snow-dependent water supplies (Alps, Himalayas, Andes, Sierra Nevada) are vulnerable. Spring snowmelt currently provides summer baseflow; with reduced snowpack, summer water availability declines. California's water crisis is partly driven by reduced snowpack (~50% decline relative to 1960s), forcing groundwater overdraft. Tropical and subtropical regions face intensified droughts (projected 10–50% decline in water availability in some regions). Conversely, high-latitude regions may see increased precipitation and runoff (some benefit for hydropower). The net global effect is increased water stress in already water-stressed regions.

#### Dams, Hydropower, and Ecosystem Impacts
Dams provide hydropower (15% global electricity) and water storage (irrigation, municipal). However, dams have massive ecosystem impacts: (1) river fragmentation (blocks fish migration, disrupts sediment transport), (2) delta destruction (dammed rivers don't reach deltas, starving them of sediment—Nile Delta and Mekong Delta are collapsing), (3) reservoir inundation (submerges ecosystems, displaces people). The Three Gorges Dam (China) submerged 1,300 km² and displaced 1.3 million people. Hydropower is renewable but ecosystem-damaging. Future dams are increasingly opposed by environmental and indigenous groups. Some regions are removing dams (e.g., Elwha River, USA) to restore rivers despite losing hydropower capacity—a statement that ecosystem services outweigh energy benefits in some cases.

#### Review Questions

2. Explain why the Ogallala Aquifer is considered "non-renewable" despite its massive size.
3. How does climate change intensify water stress in already water-stressed regions?
