#### Factor of Safety and Slope Stability
Slope stability is governed by: resisting force (friction, cohesion) vs. driving force (gravity component down slope). Factor of Safety (FoS) = resisting force / driving force. FoS > 1 = stable, FoS < 1 = unstable, FoS ≈ 1 = critical. For a simple slope: FoS = (c + γ_n × tan φ) / (γ_sat × sin θ cos θ), where c is cohesion, γ_n is normal stress, φ is friction angle, γ_sat is saturated weight, θ is slope angle. Increasing slope angle decreases FoS (steeper = less stable). Increasing pore pressure (water saturation) decreases normal stress and FoS (wetness = less stable). Cohesion (binding strength) decreases with weathering (fresh rock > weathered rock). The Atterberg limits describe clay behavior: liquid limit (LL) is water content at which clay flows, plastic limit (PL) is where it becomes moldable. Between PL and LL is the plastic range. Exceeding LL causes flows; clay soils near LL are at risk.

#### Types of Failures
Landslides are classified by movement type: (1) **Falls** (free fall from cliff), (2) **Slides** (shear failure on discrete surface—rotational slumps or translational blocks), (3) **Flows** (plastic deformation without discrete surface—mudflows, debris flows, earth flows), (4) **Complex** (combination of types). Rotational failures (slumps) are common in clay slopes; the failure surface is curved, soil rotates into failure. Translational failures occur on pre-existing surfaces (faults, bedding) or at weak layer boundaries. Debris flows (rapid flows of soil/rock/vegetation) are triggered by heavy rainfall in steep terrain. The 2017 Montecito debris flows (California) killed 23 people; a wildfire had burned vegetation stabilizing slopes, and heavy rain triggered flows. The Philippine typhoon season triggers thousands of landslides annually; terrain is steep and prone to saturation.

#### Triggering Mechanisms
Landslides are triggered by: (1) **rainfall** (increasing pore pressure, decreasing FoS), (2) **earthquakes** (vibration reducing friction, stress redistribution), (3) **anthropogenic factors** (excavation at toe reducing resistance, loading on top, deforestation removing root reinforcement). Heavy rainfall for hours to days causes failures; antecedent moisture (soil already saturated) increases susceptibility. Seasonal patterns: Hawaii gets landslides in winter (wet season); Asian monsoons trigger failures during rainy seasons. Earthquake-triggered landslides occur within minutes; proximity to epicenter matters—strongest shaking triggers most landslides. A Mw 7.5 earthquake in steep terrain can trigger 10,000+ landslides.

#### Mitigation Strategies
Landslide hazard maps identify high-risk areas using: (1) historical failures, (2) terrain analysis (slope angle, aspect, curvature), (3) geology (weak layers, fractures), (4) hydrology (drainage, saturation). Zoning restricts development in high-hazard areas. Active mitigation: (1) **drainage** (reduce pore pressure via ditches, drilling), (2) **retaining walls/anchors** (stabilize slopes mechanically), (3) **revegetation** (increase root reinforcement), (4) **slope reduction** (excavate top, reduce angle). Early warning systems based on rainfall thresholds (if rainfall exceeds historical max-safe, issue warning) can evacuate people before failure. Japan has extensive warning systems due to frequent typhoon-triggered landslides. However, landslide prediction is imprecise—many potential slopes don't fail even when thresholds are exceeded.
