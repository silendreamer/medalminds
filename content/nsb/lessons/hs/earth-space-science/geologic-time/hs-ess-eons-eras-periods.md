#### The Paleozoic Era (541–252 Ma)
The Paleozoic ("ancient life") spans from the Cambrian explosion to the Permian-Triassic extinction. Its six periods — Cambrian, Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, Carboniferous, Permian — record the diversification of all major animal body plans, the colonization of land by plants and tetrapods, and the assembly of the supercontinent Pangaea. The Carboniferous Period produced the coal deposits now fueling industrial civilization; dense forests of lycopods and tree ferns sequestered carbon and elevated atmospheric oxygen to ~35% (vs. 21% today), enabling giant insects like Meganeura (dragonfly-like, ~75 cm wingspan).

#### The Mesozoic Era (252–66 Ma)
The Mesozoic ("middle life") is the age of reptiles — dinosaurs, pterosaurs, and marine reptiles dominated terrestrial and marine ecosystems. Its three periods are the Triassic (recovery from P-Tr extinction; first dinosaurs and mammals), Jurassic (dinosaur diversification; first birds from theropod lineage), and Cretaceous (flowering plants radiate; largest dinosaurs; warm, ice-free poles). The Mesozoic ended with the Chicxulub impactor striking the Yucatán Peninsula, triggering wildfires, a global "impact winter," and the K-Pg mass extinction.

#### The Cenozoic Era (66 Ma–present)
The Cenozoic ("recent life") is the age of mammals and, ultimately, humans. It comprises two periods: the Paleogene (66–23 Ma) and Neogene (23 Ma–present), or alternatively, the older Tertiary (informal) plus Quaternary. After the K-Pg extinction, mammals rapidly diversified into ecological niches vacated by non-avian dinosaurs. Grasslands expanded in the Miocene (~23–5 Ma), driving ungulate and primate evolution. The Pleistocene (~2.6 Ma–11.7 ka) was marked by glacial-interglacial cycles.

#### Biological Index Fossils as Time Markers
Certain organisms lived for short, well-defined time intervals and had wide geographic distributions — these are **index fossils** used to correlate rock layers across continents. Classic examples: trilobites (Cambrian–Permian), ammonites (Devonian–K-Pg boundary), foraminifera species (widely used in oil exploration to date subsurface rock), and conodonts (Cambrian–Triassic). If a rock layer contains a known index fossil, geologists can assign it to a specific time interval without radiometric dating.
