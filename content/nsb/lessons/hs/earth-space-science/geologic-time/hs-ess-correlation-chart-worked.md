#### The Scenario
Three drill cores (A, B, C) are taken 100 km apart across a sedimentary basin. Core A (from east): shale with trilobites → limestone with coral → sandstone with fish fossils → coal with fern spores. Core B (center): limestone with coral → sandstone with fish fossils → shale with ammonites. Core C (west): sandstone with fish fossils → shale with ammonites → limestone with rudists.

#### Correlation Steps
Step 1: Identify index fossils. Trilobites → Cambrian–Permian; corals (rugose/tabulate) → Ordovician–Permian; fish fossils → Devonian; coal + fern spores → Carboniferous; ammonites → Devonian–K-Pg; rudists → Cretaceous. Step 2: Order the layers in each core by superposition. Step 3: Match the sandstone with fish fossils across all three cores — this is likely the same Devonian unit (a correlation marker). Step 4: The shale with ammonites in B and C correlates but is absent in A — A was either above sea level during ammonite time, or the ammonite shale was eroded.

#### Marker Beds and Time-Transgressive Deposits
A **marker bed** (key bed) is a distinctive, geologically instantaneous deposit — a volcanic ash layer (tephra), an impact ejecta layer, or a distinctive chemical horizon — that can be used to correlate globally. Unlike fossils, marker beds are **not time-transgressive**: a specific ash fall happened in a single moment and was deposited simultaneously across its dispersal area. In contrast, a sandstone beach deposit is time-transgressive: it migrates laterally as sea level changes, so the same rock type represents different ages in different locations.

#### Review Questions
1. Why are volcanic ash layers (tephras) more precise correlation tools than sandstone beds?
2. If the ammonite shale in Core A is missing, what are two possible explanations?
3. What is a time-transgressive deposit, and why does it complicate correlation?
