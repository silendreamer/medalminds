#### Defining a Mass Extinction
A mass extinction is an event in which more than ~75% of species disappear within a geologically brief interval (less than a few million years). Background extinction — the normal, ongoing loss of species — is estimated at 0.1–1 species per million species per year. Mass extinctions represent a rate 100–1,000× higher. They reset ecosystems, opening ecological space for previously minor groups to diversify rapidly (evolutionary radiations).

#### The Five Events and Their Causes
1. **End-Ordovician (~443 Ma):** ~85% species loss; caused by glaciation and sea-level drop when Gondwana drifted over the South Pole, withdrawing shallow marine habitat.
2. **Late Devonian (~375 Ma):** ~75% species loss; primarily affected marine invertebrates; likely caused by anoxic ocean events linked to land-plant colonization releasing nutrients.
3. **End-Permian (~252 Ma):** ~96% marine species, ~70% terrestrial species lost — the "Great Dying"; caused by Siberian Traps flood volcanism releasing CO₂ and SO₂, triggering ocean acidification, warming, and anoxia.
4. **End-Triassic (~201 Ma):** ~80% species loss; linked to Central Atlantic Magmatic Province (CAMP) volcanism; cleared the way for dinosaur dominance.
5. **K-Pg (~66 Ma):** ~76% species loss; Chicxulub asteroid impact (10 km diameter) produced ejecta, global fires, and impact winter blocking photosynthesis for months to years.

#### Recovery Timescales
Recovery from a mass extinction takes 5–10 million years — far longer than the extinction event itself. The fossil record shows "disaster fauna" (opportunistic, low-diversity communities) immediately post-extinction, followed by gradual ecological rebuilding. The P-Tr event took ~10 million years for full ecosystem recovery.
