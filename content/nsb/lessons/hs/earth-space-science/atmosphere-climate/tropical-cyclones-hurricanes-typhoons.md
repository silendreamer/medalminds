#### Formation Requirements
Tropical cyclones require: (1) sea surface temperature ≥26.5°C, (2) low wind shear (<10 m/s), (3) sufficient Coriolis force (5°–30° from equator; >5° needed for the Coriolis effect to initiate rotation), (4) high atmospheric moisture, and (5) atmospheric instability. A weak low-pressure system over warm water can develop a circulation if these conditions align. The system organizes as convection becomes concentrated and latent heat release from condensation warms the air column, lowering pressure. Rapid intensification occurs when all conditions are met and the system can convert warm ocean water's energy into organized rotating motion. Hurricane Wilma (2005) intensified from tropical depression to Category 5 in 24 hours—an extreme example.

#### Structure and the Eye
A mature hurricane has a clear eye (radius ~10–30 km) surrounded by the eyewall—a ring of the most intense convection with the strongest winds. Outside the eyewall, rainbands spiral outward, weaker but covering larger areas (300 km+). The eye is an region of subsiding air, clear skies, and light winds—a striking contrast to the eyewall's violence. This subsidence is dynamically required for the circulation to balance the intense pressure gradient. The Central Pressure (CP) is often used as a hurricane intensity proxy: Cat 1: 980–965 mb, Cat 2: 965–945 mb, ..., Cat 5: <920 mb. Hurricane Camille (1969) reached 900 mb, one of the lowest pressures ever recorded in an Atlantic hurricane. Maximum Sustained Winds (MSW) correlate with CP but are not perfectly correlated—some weak storms have surprisingly high winds, others vice versa.

#### Intensification and Decay
Hurricanes intensify while over warm water with low shear. The Saffir-Simpson Wind Scale ranks them by intensity: Cat 1 (74–95 mph), Cat 2 (96–110), Cat 3 (111–129), Cat 4 (130–156), Cat 5 (>157). Most Atlantic hurricanes are Cat 1–2; Cat 4–5 occur when conditions are optimal. Sea surface temperature provides the "fuel"—warmer water means more latent heat release from evaporation, stronger updrafts, and faster intensification. Climate change has warmed sea surface temperatures, potentially increasing the proportion of intense hurricanes (though total frequency may decrease slightly). Decay occurs when hurricanes move over cooler water or land, cutting off evaporative fuel supply. Vertical wind shear tears the system apart by separating warm core from updrafts.

#### Storm Surge and Coastal Impact
The most dangerous hurricane impacts are often storm surge—the rise in sea level due to wind pushing water toward the coast and low pressure allowing the ocean surface to bulge upward. Hurricane surge of 5–10 meters can submerge coastal areas, destroying infrastructure and drowning people. Katrina (2005) produced 6-meter surge, devastating New Orleans. Compound events (surge + high tide + heavy rain) increase flooding. Precipitation is also heavy—Hurricane Harvey (2017) stalled over Texas and produced 1.5 meters of rain, causing $125 billion damage—mostly from freshwater flooding, not wind. Future storms over warmer oceans may be more intense, while sea level rise amplifies surge impacts.
