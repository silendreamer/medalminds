#### What Are Aerosols?
Aerosols are microscopic solid particles or liquid droplets suspended in the atmosphere, ranging from 0.001 to 100 micrometers in diameter. Natural sources include sea salt, dust, volcanic ash, pollen, and biogenic particles. Human sources include industrial emissions, vehicle exhaust, fossil fuel burning, and biomass burning. Aerosols have both direct effects (scattering and absorbing radiation) and indirect effects (serving as cloud condensation nuclei, changing cloud properties). A single volcanic eruption can inject millions of tons of sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere, cooling global temperatures by 0.5–1°C for 1–3 years.

#### Aerosol Optical Depth and Radiative Forcing
Aerosol optical depth (AOD) measures how much aerosols scatter and absorb light; higher AOD means more aerosol scattering. AOD varies from ~0.05 over clean oceans to >1.0 over polluted regions. Aerosols cause negative radiative forcing (cooling), with an estimated forcing of -0.9 W/m² globally, partially offsetting greenhouse gas warming of +1.8 W/m². This masking effect is important: as air pollution regulations reduce aerosol emissions, this "aerosol masking" decreases, potentially accelerating warming. China's reduction of sulfate aerosol emissions from coal burning is expected to contribute ~0.1°C of additional warming independent of CO2 increases.

#### Case Study: Mount Pinatubo (1991)
When Mount Pinatubo erupted in the Philippines, it injected ~20 million tons of SO2 into the stratosphere. This formed sulfate aerosols that spread globally within weeks. AOD increased to unprecedented levels in the observational record. Global temperatures dropped by ~0.5°C within a year. This natural experiment demonstrated the power of stratospheric aerosols. Some scientists propose "solar geoengineering" by intentionally injecting aerosols to cool the planet, mimicking volcanic effects—a controversial idea with potential unintended consequences for precipitation patterns and the ozone layer.

#### Air Quality and Health
Tropospheric aerosols (PM2.5 and PM10—particles smaller than 2.5 and 10 micrometers) cause respiratory and cardiovascular disease. The WHO estimates ~7 million premature deaths annually from air pollution. India and China experience seasonal aerosol increases from agricultural burning (stubble burning in India, especially November–January) and coal burning. Satellite data from instruments like MODIS tracks aerosol plumes crossing continents, showing transboundary air pollution. Reducing anthropogenic aerosols improves air quality but allows more greenhouse warming to manifest.
