#### Pressure Gradient and Coriolis Effect
Wind is driven by pressure gradients—air flows from high to low pressure. The pressure gradient force (PGF) accelerates air toward lower pressure. However, as air moves, the Coriolis effect deflects it to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and left in the Southern Hemisphere (proportional to wind speed). Eventually, wind and pressure gradient force balance, with wind flowing parallel to isobars at the geostrophic balance. The geostrophic wind speed is: V_g = (1/fρ) × (dP/dn), where f is Coriolis parameter (2Ω sinφ), ρ is air density, and dP/dn is pressure gradient. At 45° latitude in mid-latitudes, a 5 mb pressure difference per 500 km produces ~30 knot winds. Near the equator, Coriolis force is weak, so winds don't balance geostrophically—this is why the doldrums (low wind near the equator) exist and why trade winds are weaker than mid-latitude westerlies.

#### Trade Winds: Structure and Variability
Trade winds are steady winds from the northeast (NH) and southeast (SH) in the tropics (5–30° latitude), with typical speeds of 10–15 knots. They result from the Hadley cell's descending branch creating subtropical high pressure; air flows equatorward and is deflected westward by Coriolis. Trade winds are most consistent over oceans (Atlantic, Pacific, Indian Oceans) where there's less friction. Wind speed varies seasonally—stronger in winter when the subtropical high is more intense. Historically, trade winds were critical for sailing; the Columbus expedition relied on them, as did spice trade routes. Modern sailing still uses trade winds for efficiency. Interannual variability is largest during El Niño, when Pacific trade winds weaken (or reverse) due to suppressed Walker circulation.

#### Westerlies and Mid-Latitude Wind Patterns
The mid-latitudes (30–60°) are dominated by westerly winds (from the west) in the upper troposphere and surface. These are stronger and more variable than trade winds, featuring traveling cyclones and anticyclones. Surface westerlies are around 10–20 knots on average but can exceed 50 knots in storms. Upper-level westerlies (jet streams) exceed 100 knots routinely. Jet stream position determines mid-latitude weather—a northward-displaced jet brings warm conditions, while a southward dip brings cold air and storms. The North Atlantic and North Pacific see the most intense cyclones where ocean temperature gradients are steep and jet streams are strongest.

#### Case Study: Trade Wind Weakening and Sea Level Rise
During the 2015–2016 El Niño, Pacific trade winds weakened, allowing the warm water pool to spread eastward across the central Pacific. This atmospheric change had oceanic consequences: reduced trade winds meant less upwelling of cold water, warmer SST, and higher sea level in the Western Pacific (water expands when heated). Sea level in the Western Pacific (near the Philippines) rose ~20 cm above normal, while Eastern Pacific sea level fell. This pattern reversed sharply in 2016 as La Niña (opposite of El Niño) strengthened trade winds. Understanding wind-ocean coupling is crucial for seasonal sea level and rainfall prediction.
