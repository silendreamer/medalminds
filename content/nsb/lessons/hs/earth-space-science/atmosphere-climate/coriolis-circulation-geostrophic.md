#### The Coriolis Effect

The **Coriolis effect** is an apparent deflection of moving objects caused by Earth's rotation. In the **Northern Hemisphere**, freely moving air (and water) is deflected to the **right** of its direction of motion; in the **Southern Hemisphere** it is deflected to the **left**. The effect is zero at the equator and maximized at the poles, and its magnitude depends on the speed of the moving parcel and the sine of its latitude. It does not create wind — it only redirects air already in motion.

#### The Three-Cell Circulation Model

Earth's atmosphere organizes into three latitudinal circulation cells per hemisphere. The **Hadley cell** spans 0°–30°: solar heating at the equator drives air upward, it flows poleward aloft, descends near 30°, and returns equatorward at the surface. The **Ferrel cell** (30°–60°) is thermally indirect, driven by the neighboring cells, with surface air flowing poleward. The **Polar cell** (60°–90°) mirrors the Hadley cell: cold, dense air sinks at the poles, flows equatorward at the surface, and rises near 60°. Sinking air at 30° and 90° creates high-pressure belts; rising air at 0° and 60° creates low-pressure belts.

#### Global Wind Belts

The surface branches of each cell, deflected by the Coriolis effect, produce the major **global wind belts**. The **trade winds** blow from the subtropical highs (~30°) toward the equator — northeasterly in the Northern Hemisphere, southeasterly in the Southern Hemisphere. The **westerlies** occupy the mid-latitudes (30°–60°) and blow from southwest to northeast in the Northern Hemisphere. The **polar easterlies** flow from the polar highs toward ~60° latitude. Between major cells lie the **doldrums** (equatorial calm belt) and the **horse latitudes** (subtropical calm belt near 30°).

#### Geostrophic Wind

At altitudes above the friction layer (~1 km), the **pressure-gradient force** (PGF) — directed from high to low pressure — is balanced by the Coriolis effect, producing **geostrophic wind** that flows *parallel* to isobars rather than across them. In the Northern Hemisphere, geostrophic flow is **clockwise around highs** (anticyclones) and **counterclockwise around lows** (cyclones); these directions reverse in the Southern Hemisphere. Geostrophic balance is a good approximation for large-scale, quasi-horizontal flow away from the equator.

#### Review Questions

1. A parcel of air moves northward from the equator in the Northern Hemisphere. In which direction is it deflected by the Coriolis effect, and how does that deflection change as the parcel moves toward higher latitudes?
2. Describe the vertical motion and surface wind direction associated with the Hadley cell, and explain what pressure belt forms near 30° latitude as a result.
3. Why does geostrophic wind flow parallel to isobars rather than from high to low pressure, and in which direction does it circulate around a low-pressure system in the Southern Hemisphere?
