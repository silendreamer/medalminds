#### Major and Trace Gases
Nitrogen (78%) and oxygen (21%) dominate the atmosphere by volume, but their concentrations are stable on human timescales. The critical gases for climate are the trace gases: carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), nitrous oxide (N2O), and water vapor (H2O). CO2 has increased from 280 ppm in pre-industrial times to 420 ppm today—a 50% increase in 150 years. Methane is 28–34 times more potent than CO2 per molecule over a 100-year period but breaks down in ~12 years. Water vapor is the most abundant greenhouse gas but acts more as a feedback (warming air holds more water, amplifying warming) than a forcing agent.

#### Sources and Sinks
Carbon dioxide enters the atmosphere through respiration, fossil fuel burning, cement production, and deforestation. It is removed by photosynthesis and oceanic absorption—the ocean has absorbed ~30% of emitted CO2. Methane comes from wetlands, livestock (cow digestion produces ~14.5% of global emissions), rice paddies, natural gas leaks, and permafrost thaw. Nitrous oxide comes primarily from agricultural soil microbes and industrial processes. Understanding these cycles is crucial for climate projections.

#### Feedback Mechanisms
The atmosphere exhibits both positive and negative feedbacks. A positive feedback amplifies change: CO2 increases → more warming → more water vapor → even more warming (water vapor feedback). Another: warming melts ice → less sunlight reflected → more warming (albedo feedback). A negative feedback resists change: more CO2 → more plant growth → more CO2 uptake (though this is small). The net effect of feedbacks is still positive—warming amplifies warming—which is why climate sensitivity (how much warming per doubling of CO2) is ~3°C, not the direct calculation of ~1°C.

#### Measurement and Monitoring
Scientists measure atmospheric composition using surface stations (like Mauna Loa in Hawaii, which has recorded CO2 since 1958), satellite instruments (like SCIAMACHY), and ice cores. Ice cores trap atmospheric bubbles, allowing reconstruction of atmospheric composition back 800,000 years. These records show that current CO2 levels are the highest in at least 800,000 years. Real-time monitoring networks track greenhouse gas concentrations globally, providing early warning of changing atmospheric composition.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the current atmospheric CO2 concentration and how much has it increased since pre-industrial times?
2. Why does methane have a greater per-molecule warming effect than CO2?
3. Explain the water vapor feedback loop and why it amplifies warming.
