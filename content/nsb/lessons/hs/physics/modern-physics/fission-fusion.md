#### Fission Basics
When a heavy nucleus (e.g., uranium-235) absorbs a neutron, it can fission into two smaller nuclei plus 2–3 free neutrons and energy (~200 MeV per fission). The products have higher binding energy per nucleon than the original nucleus, releasing energy. Fission is used in nuclear reactors and bombs. Unlike radioactive decay (spontaneous), fission requires an incoming neutron trigger.

#### Chain Reaction & Critical Mass
The free neutrons from one fission can trigger additional fissions, creating an exponential chain reaction if the reaction conditions are right. In a reactor, control rods absorb neutrons, maintaining a steady reaction rate. In a bomb, the reaction rate is uncontrolled, releasing enormous energy suddenly. The critical mass is the minimum amount of fissile material needed to sustain a chain reaction; it depends on the material's properties and geometry.

#### Fusion Basics
Fusion occurs when two light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy. The Sun's energy comes from fusion of hydrogen into helium via the proton-proton chain (pp-chain). A deuterium-tritium fusion (d + t → ⁴He + n) releases 17.6 MeV. Fusion requires extremely high temperatures (millions of kelvins) to overcome the Coulomb barrier (electrostatic repulsion between nuclei). Fusion is the basis for hydrogen bombs and is being pursued for controlled, clean energy generation (ITER tokamak project).

#### Stellar Nucleosynthesis
Fusion in stellar cores creates heavier nuclei: hydrogen fuses to helium, helium to carbon, and so on up to iron. Iron fusion is endothermic (requires energy), so massive stars end their lives in supernovae when iron accumulates. Supernovae create even heavier elements and spread them through space, enriching the interstellar medium. All nuclei heavier than lithium were created in stars or supernovae.

#### Worked Example: Energy from Fission
U-235 + n → Ba-141 + Kr-92 + 3n (rough fission). Using E = Δm · c² with typical mass defect Δm ≈ 0.1 u ≈ 1.66 × 10⁻²⁸ kg: E ≈ (1.66 × 10⁻²⁸) × (3 × 10⁸)² ≈ 1.5 × 10⁻¹¹ J ≈ 93 MeV (in the right ballpark for 200 MeV per fission after accounting for multiple fragments).
