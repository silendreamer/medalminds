#### The Mass-Energy Relation
Einstein's famous equation E = mc² states that a mass m at rest has energy equivalent of mc². The speed of light c ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s, so c² ≈ 9 × 10¹⁶ m²/s². A mere 1 kg of matter has rest energy E = 1 × (9 × 10¹⁶) ≈ 9 × 10¹⁶ joules—equivalent to the energy released by burning about 3 billion tons of coal. This enormous energy underlies nuclear reactions.

#### Nuclear Reactions & Binding Energy
In nuclear fusion (combining light nuclei) and fission (splitting heavy nuclei), a small fraction of mass converts to energy. For example, in deuteron-tritium fusion (d + t → ⁴He + n), about 0.3% of the reactant mass converts to kinetic energy of products and neutrons. Over timescales of billions of years, this mass-to-energy conversion powers the Sun. In a nuclear bomb, this energy release is catastrophically fast.

#### Antimatter-Matter Annihilation
When a particle meets its antiparticle (e.g., electron and positron), they annihilate, converting their entire rest masses to energy: e⁻ + e⁺ → 2γ (gamma rays). The released energy is E = (m_e + m_e)c² ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻¹³ joules per pair—a large amount for such tiny masses. Antimatter would be an ideal fuel if it could be contained and controlled.

#### Worked Example: Nuclear Binding Energy
The helium-4 nucleus (⁴He) has a mass slightly less than the sum of its components (2 protons + 2 neutrons). The mass defect is Δm ≈ 0.0305 u (atomic mass units; 1 u ≈ 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg). The binding energy is BE = Δm c² ≈ (0.0305 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷) × (9 × 10¹⁶) ≈ 4.6 × 10⁻¹² joules, or about 28.3 MeV (million electron volts).
