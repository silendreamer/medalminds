#### Nuclear Composition & Stability
A nucleus contains Z protons and N neutrons (total mass number A = Z + N). The nuclear diameter is roughly 10⁻¹⁵ m (1 femtometer). Despite the electrostatic repulsion between protons, nuclei are stable due to the strong nuclear force, which is stronger than electromagnetism at nuclear distances but falls off rapidly. Nuclei with the "right" neutron-to-proton ratio are stable; those with too many or too few neutrons are unstable and decay.

#### Binding Energy & Mass Defect
The mass of a nucleus is less than the sum of its nucleon masses—the mass defect Δm = Z·m_p + N·m_n − M_nucleus. This difference represents the binding energy: BE = Δm · c². Nucleons gain binding energy when assembled; conversely, breaking apart nuclei requires energy. The binding energy per nucleon (BE/A) is largest for iron-56 (~8.8 MeV/nucleon), making it the most stable nucleus. Fusion of light nuclei or fission of heavy nuclei release energy because the products have higher BE/A.

#### Radioactive Decay Modes
(1) Alpha decay: nucleus emits helium-4 (α particle), decreasing A by 4, Z by 2. (2) Beta-minus (β⁻) decay: neutron converts to proton, emitting electron (e⁻) and antineutrino (ν̄_e), increasing Z by 1. (3) Beta-plus (β⁺) decay: proton converts to neutron, emitting positron (e⁺) and neutrino (ν_e), decreasing Z by 1. (4) Gamma decay: nucleus emits high-energy photon (γ ray), decreasing energy but not changing A or Z. Different nuclei decay preferentially by different modes depending on their structure.

#### Activity & Half-Life
The activity (A) is the decay rate: A = λN, where λ is the decay constant and N is the number of nuclei. Activity has units of becquerels (Bq = decays/second) or curies (Ci ≈ 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq). The number of nuclei decays exponentially: N(t) = N₀ e^(−λt). The half-life (t₁/₂) is the time for half the nuclei to decay: t₁/₂ = ln(2)/λ ≈ 0.693/λ.
