#### Compton Scattering
When a high-energy photon (typically X-ray) scatters off an electron at rest, the photon transfers momentum to the electron, recoils to lower energy (longer wavelength), and the electron recoils (gains kinetic energy). Energy and momentum are both conserved. The Compton scattering formula relates the initial wavelength λ, final wavelength λ', and scattering angle θ: λ' − λ = (h/(m_e c))(1 − cos θ). The quantity h/(m_e c) ≈ 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m is the Compton wavelength of the electron.

#### Physical Interpretation
Compton scattering provides direct evidence that photons carry momentum (p = h/λ). Classical wave theory predicts that scattered light should have the same wavelength as incident light (resonant scattering by oscillating charges). The observed wavelength shift uniquely confirms photon momentum. The shift is maximum at 180° (backscattering), where λ' − λ = 2h/(m_e c) ≈ 4.86 × 10⁻¹² m.

#### X-Ray Production
X-rays are produced by accelerating electrons and letting them decelerate rapidly (bremsstrahlung, or "braking radiation"). When a high-speed electron decelerates, it radiates electromagnetic energy as X-rays. A more efficient method is to accelerate electrons into a target metal; inner-shell electrons are knocked out, and higher-shell electrons fall down, emitting characteristic X-rays (specific wavelengths for each metal).

#### Applications & Medical Use
X-rays penetrate soft tissue but are absorbed by dense materials (bone, metals). This allows medical imaging: X-ray radiography creates shadow images of internal structures. CT (computed tomography) scans use multiple X-ray angles and computer reconstruction. The photoelectric effect and Compton scattering are the primary X-ray absorption mechanisms in biological tissue.
