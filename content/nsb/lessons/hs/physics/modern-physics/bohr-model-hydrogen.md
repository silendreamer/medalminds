#### Postulates of Bohr Model
(1) Electrons orbit the nucleus in circular orbits with quantized angular momentum: L = nℏ = n(h/2π), where n = 1, 2, 3, ... (principal quantum number). (2) Electrons do not continuously radiate energy despite acceleration, defying classical electromagnetism. (3) Energy is released or absorbed when electrons transition between allowed orbits. Despite its conceptual inconsistencies, Bohr's model correctly predicts hydrogen's spectrum and energy levels.

#### Quantization Conditions
For a hydrogen atom with electron mass m_e, electron charge e, and nucleus charge Ze (Z = 1 for hydrogen), Bohr's quantization conditions yield the Bohr radius: a₀ = 4πε₀ℏ²/(m_e e²) ≈ 0.53 Å (5.3 × 10⁻¹¹ m). The energy levels are E_n = −Z²(13.6 eV)/n². For n = 1 (ground state), E₁ = −13.6 eV. For n = 2, E₂ = −3.4 eV. The energy difference for a transition from n = 2 to n = 1 is ΔE = −3.4 − (−13.6) = 10.2 eV, corresponding to a photon of frequency f = 10.2 eV / h ≈ 2.5 × 10¹⁵ Hz.

#### Spectral Lines
When an electron transitions from level n_i to n_f, the emitted photon's frequency is f = (Z²13.6 eV)(1/n_f² − 1/n_i²)/h. The Lyman series (n_f = 1) occurs in the ultraviolet; the Balmer series (n_f = 2) occurs in the visible; the Paschen series (n_f = 3) occurs in the infrared. These series are seen in hydrogen's emission spectrum and absorption spectrum. Hydrogen's line spectrum provided early evidence for quantization.

#### Limitations of Bohr Model
The Bohr model works only for hydrogen (single electron). For helium (two electrons) and heavier atoms, electron-electron repulsion and quantum effects make classical orbits invalid. The model does not explain why certain transitions are allowed and others forbidden. Modern quantum mechanics (using wave functions and the Schrödinger equation) supersedes Bohr's model but keeps its central insight: energy levels are quantized.
