#### The Two Types of Friction
**Static friction (fs)**: acts when surfaces are *not* sliding relative to each other. It adjusts to match the applied force up to a maximum: fs ≤ μsN. When applied force exceeds μsN, the object starts sliding. **Kinetic friction (fk)**: acts while surfaces slide. It is constant: fk = μkN. Critically: μs > μk, so it's harder to start sliding than to keep sliding.

#### Coefficients of Friction
μs and μk are dimensionless numbers that depend on the *pair* of surfaces in contact (not on speed, area of contact, or mass). Rubber on dry pavement: μs ≈ 0.8; ice on ice: μs ≈ 0.1. The coefficients multiply the *normal force* N, not the weight (they're equal only on level surfaces).

#### Normal Force and Its Relationship to Friction
Friction = μ × N. If you push a crate harder into the floor (increasing N), friction increases. If you push the crate at an upward angle, the vertical component reduces N, which reduces friction — that's why tilting a handle upward makes it easier to push something. On a horizontal surface: N = mg. On an incline or with a tilted applied force: N ≠ mg.

#### Finding the Coefficient Experimentally
Place block on surface. Gradually increase applied force F until block just slides. At that moment, fs,max = F → μs = F/N = F/mg (on level surface). Measure sliding force to find μk.

#### Review Questions
1. A 10 kg box has μs = 0.5 and μk = 0.35. (a) What is the maximum static friction? (b) Once sliding, what is the kinetic friction?
2. You push down at 30° on a 5 kg box. Applied force is 40 N. Find the normal force and maximum static friction (μs = 0.4).
3. Why does kinetic friction remain approximately constant regardless of sliding speed (for moderate speeds)?
