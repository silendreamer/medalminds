### Introduction to Linear Inequalities

Linear inequalities are mathematical expressions that describe a range of values rather than a single value. They take the form of \( ax + by < c \), \( ax + by \leq c \), \( ax + by > c \), or \( ax + by \geq c \), where \( a \), \( b \), and \( c \) are constants. Understanding linear inequalities is crucial for solving real-world problems involving constraints, such as maximizing profit or minimizing costs in various fields, including economics and engineering.

### Graphing Linear Inequalities

To graph a linear inequality, start by graphing the corresponding linear equation \( ax + by = c \). This equation represents the boundary line of the inequality. If the inequality is strict (using < or >), the boundary line is dashed, indicating that points on the line are not included in the solution set. If the inequality is non-strict (using ≤ or ≥), the line is solid, meaning points on the line are included in the solution set.

### Identifying Solution Sets

The solution set of a linear inequality consists of all the points in the coordinate plane that satisfy the inequality. After graphing the boundary line, select a test point not on the line (commonly the origin, (0,0), if it is not on the line) to determine which side of the line contains the solution set. If the test point satisfies the inequality, shade the region containing that point; if not, shade the opposite side. This shaded region represents all possible solutions to the inequality.

### Boundary Lines and Their Importance

Boundary lines are critical in defining the limits of the solution set for linear inequalities. They can be vertical, horizontal, or slanted lines depending on the coefficients of \( x \) and \( y \) in the inequality. The slope-intercept form \( y = mx + b \) is often used to easily identify the slope (m) and y-intercept (b) of the boundary line. Understanding the position and nature of these lines helps in visualizing the constraints imposed by the inequalities.

### Systems of Linear Inequalities

In many real-world scenarios, multiple linear inequalities are considered simultaneously, forming a system of inequalities. The solution to a system of linear inequalities is the region where the shaded areas of all inequalities overlap. This intersection represents the set of all points that satisfy all inequalities in the system. Solving these systems is essential for optimization problems, where one seeks to find the best solution under given constraints.

### Applications of Linear Inequalities

Linear inequalities have numerous applications across various fields. In economics, they can represent constraints on resources, such as budget limits or production capacities. In environmental science, they can model limits on pollution levels or resource usage. Understanding how to graph and interpret these inequalities allows for better decision-making and optimization in practical situations.

### Review Questions

2. How do you determine which side of the boundary line to shade when graphing a linear inequality?
3. In a system of linear inequalities, what does the overlapping shaded region represent?
