#### What Are Atoms?

An **atom** is the smallest piece of an element that still has the properties of that element. Every atom has a **nucleus** at its center containing **protons** (which carry a positive charge) and **neutrons** (which have no charge). Around the nucleus, **electrons** (which carry a negative charge) zoom around in regions called **electron shells**. In a neutral atom, the number of protons equals the number of electrons, so the atom has no overall charge.

#### How Do Atoms Become Ions?

An **ion** is an atom that has gained or lost one or more electrons. When an atom loses electrons, it has more protons than electrons, so it becomes positively charged—we call this a **cation**. For example, a sodium atom that loses one electron becomes a Na+ ion. When an atom gains extra electrons, it has more electrons than protons, making it negatively charged—we call this an **anion**. A chlorine atom that gains one electron becomes a Cl- ion. Ions form when atoms want to be more stable by having a full outer electron shell.

#### What Are Isotopes?

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element that have different numbers of **neutrons**. Since the number of protons stays the same, isotopes have the same chemical properties but different **atomic masses**. For example, carbon has two common isotopes: carbon-12 (with 6 neutrons) and carbon-14 (with 8 neutrons). We write them as ¹²C and ¹⁴C. Carbon-14 is radioactive and decays over time, which makes it useful for **radiocarbon dating** of ancient objects.

#### Why Does This Matter?

Understanding atoms, ions, and isotopes is crucial for explaining how substances react with each other and how the periodic table is organized. Ions explain why salt (sodium chloride) forms—a sodium ion bonds with a chloride ion. Isotopes explain why elements can have different forms and why some are radioactive. These concepts form the foundation of all chemistry.
