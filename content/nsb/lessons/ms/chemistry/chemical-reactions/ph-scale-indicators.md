#### Understanding Acids and Bases

An **acid** is a substance that tastes sour and can burn or corrode materials. **Hydrochloric acid** in your stomach helps digest food, while **acetic acid** gives vinegar its sour taste. A **base** is a substance that tastes bitter and feels slippery, like soap. **Sodium hydroxide** is used in cleaning products, and **ammonia** is found in many household cleaners. When an acid and a base mix together in just the right amounts, they **neutralize** each other and form a **salt** and water in a process called **neutralization**. Understanding acids and bases is essential for everything from cooking to laboratory safety.

#### The pH Scale: Measuring Acidity

The **pH scale** is a way to measure how acidic or basic a substance is. It ranges from 0 to 14, where:
- pH 0–6 = **acidic** (acids have more hydrogen ions)
- pH 7 = **neutral** (pure water)
- pH 8–14 = **basic** or **alkaline** (bases have fewer hydrogen ions)

Each step on the scale represents a tenfold change in acidity. For example, a substance with pH 3 is ten times more acidic than a substance with pH 4. **Lemon juice** has a pH around 2 (very acidic), while **baking soda** has a pH around 8 (slightly basic). Scientists use pH meters to measure exact values, but we can also estimate pH using simple tests.

#### Indicators: Colorful pH Detectives

An **indicator** is a substance that changes color to show whether something is acidic or basic. **Litmus paper** is one of the most common indicators: **red litmus** turns blue in bases, and **blue litmus** turns red in acids. **Phenolphthalein** is another popular indicator that stays colorless in acids and turns pink in bases. **Universal indicator paper** contains a mixture of dyes that produces different colors across the entire pH scale—from red (very acidic) to purple (very basic). You can even make natural indicators from **red cabbage juice**, which changes color from pink (acidic) to blue (basic) to yellow (very basic).

#### Why pH Matters in Real Life

pH is important in many contexts. **Soil pH** affects what plants can grow in a garden—some plants prefer acidic soil while others prefer basic soil. **Water pH** in aquariums must stay in a safe range for fish to survive. **Acid rain** (pH below 5.6) damages forests and buildings. In your body, **stomach acid** has a pH around 2 to break down food, while **blood** stays between pH 7.35 and 7.45 for your cells to function properly. Understanding and controlling pH is crucial in agriculture, medicine, industry, and environmental protection.
