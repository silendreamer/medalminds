#### Synthesis Reactions: Building Blocks Together

A **synthesis reaction** (or **combination reaction**) occurs when two or more simple substances join together to make a more complex compound. The general pattern is: A + B → AB. For example, when **hydrogen gas** and **oxygen gas** react, they combine to form water: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Another example is **sodium** metal reacting with **chlorine gas** to produce **table salt** (sodium chloride). Synthesis reactions release energy, making them **exothermic**, which is why they can happen explosively if the reactants are energetic enough.

#### Decomposition Reactions: Breaking Apart

**Decomposition reactions** are the opposite of synthesis—a complex compound breaks down into simpler substances. The pattern is: AB → A + B. When **water** is exposed to electricity (a process called **electrolysis**), it breaks apart into hydrogen and oxygen gases. Another common example is **photosynthesis** in plants, where carbon dioxide and water break down and recombine to form glucose and oxygen. Some decomposition reactions are slow (like rust forming), while others are fast and dramatic (like fireworks exploding).

#### Replacement Reactions: Swapping Partners

In a **replacement reaction** (or **displacement reaction**), one element takes the place of another element in a compound. In a **single replacement**, one element replaces another: AB + C → AC + B. For instance, if you drop **iron** into a solution of copper sulfate, the iron replaces the copper: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu. In a **double replacement**, two compounds swap their ions: AB + CD → AD + CB. When **barium chloride** mixes with **sodium sulfate**, they swap to form barium sulfate (a solid) and sodium chloride.

#### Combustion Reactions: Burning Away

**Combustion reactions** happen when a substance reacts with **oxygen gas** and releases energy in the form of heat and light. The most familiar example is burning: **methane gas** (natural gas) burns in air: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + energy. Combustion reactions always produce **carbon dioxide** and **water** when organic compounds burn, and they always release significant energy. This is why we use fuel combustion to heat homes and power cars—it's an efficient way to convert chemical energy into heat energy.
