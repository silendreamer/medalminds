### Understanding Empirical Formulas

### Definition of Empirical Formulas
An empirical formula represents the simplest whole-number ratio of the elements in a compound. For example, the empirical formula for hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) is HO, indicating that for every one hydrogen atom, there is one oxygen atom. This formula does not provide information about the actual number of atoms in a molecule but rather the ratio of the elements present.

### Calculating Empirical Formulas
To calculate an empirical formula, one must first determine the mass of each element in a sample of the compound. These masses can then be converted to moles by dividing by the atomic mass of each element. The resulting mole values are simplified to the smallest whole-number ratio, which gives the empirical formula. For example, if a compound contains 4 grams of carbon and 8 grams of hydrogen, the moles of carbon would be 4 g / 12 g/mol = 0.33 moles, and the moles of hydrogen would be 8 g / 1 g/mol = 8 moles, leading to an empirical formula of CH₂.

### Molar Mass and Its Importance
The molar mass of a compound is the mass of one mole of that substance, measured in grams per mole (g/mol). It is crucial for converting between grams and moles, which is essential in calculating empirical formulas. For instance, if you know the empirical formula of a compound and its molar mass, you can determine the molecular formula by dividing the molar mass by the mass of the empirical formula and multiplying the subscripts in the empirical formula by that ratio.

### Percent Composition of Compounds
Percent composition refers to the percentage by mass of each element in a compound. To calculate percent composition, divide the mass of each element in the compound by the total molar mass of the compound and multiply by 100. For example, in water (H₂O), the molar mass is approximately 18 g/mol, with hydrogen contributing about 11.1% and oxygen about 88.9% to the total mass. Understanding percent composition helps in determining empirical formulas from experimental data.

### Practical Applications of Empirical Formulas
Empirical formulas are widely used in chemistry to identify compounds and understand their properties. They are particularly useful in stoichiometry, where they help predict the outcomes of chemical reactions. Additionally, empirical formulas can assist chemists in determining the purity of a substance by comparing the expected percent composition to the actual results obtained from experiments.

### Review Questions
1. What is the empirical formula of a compound that contains 3 moles of carbon and 6 moles of hydrogen?
2. How do you calculate the percent composition of an element in a compound?
3. If the molar mass of a compound is 60 g/mol and its empirical formula is CH₂, what is the molecular formula?
