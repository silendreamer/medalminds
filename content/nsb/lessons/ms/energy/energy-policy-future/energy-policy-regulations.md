#### Energy Efficiency Standards
The government sets minimum efficiency standards for appliances, vehicles, and buildings. Refrigerators, air conditioners, and water heaters must meet efficiency minimums. Cars must meet CAFE (Corporate Average Fuel Economy) standards — manufacturers' average fleet must achieve ~35 mpg. These standards have driven innovation and prevented the market race-to-the-bottom that would result if efficiency was ignored.

#### Renewable Energy Standards
Many states require utilities to source a percentage of electricity from renewables (Renewable Portfolio Standards). California requires 60% by 2030. Texas has huge wind capacity due to state incentives. These standards create demand for renewable energy, spurring investment and cost reduction. Nationally, renewable energy has grown from 10% to 20% of U.S. electricity in the past decade, partly driven by standards.

#### Tax Incentives and Subsidies
The federal government offers tax credits for installing solar (30% investment tax credit), buying electric vehicles (up to $7,500 credit), and making home efficiency improvements. State incentives vary widely — California's rebates for appliances, Hawaii's solar incentives, etc. Subsidies reduce the initial cost of clean energy, making it more accessible. Some argue subsidies create dependency and distort markets; supporters say they level a playing field distorted by fossil fuel externalities (pollution, climate).

#### Carbon Regulations and Cap-and-Trade
Some states (California, Northeast) have cap-and-trade systems that limit total CO₂ emissions and allow companies to buy/sell allowances. Limits tighten over time, creating pressure to reduce emissions. Cap-and-trade allows flexibility — companies can choose least-cost abatement. The EU's Emissions Trading System is the world's largest carbon market. At federal level, there's no comprehensive carbon cap, though proposals exist.

#### Building Codes and Performance Standards
Energy codes dictate minimum insulation, HVAC efficiency, and lighting for new buildings. Increasingly, codes require energy performance standards — buildings must meet efficiency targets or owners face penalties. Cities like New York have building performance standards requiring carbon reductions. These policies drive efficiency retrofits of existing buildings, which consume 70% of total building energy.
