#### Cost of Electricity Generation
Different sources have different economics. Coal plants cost $1-3 billion to build but operate for 40+ years. Natural gas plants cost $500 million but have faster fuel price volatility. Solar costs ~$1-2 billion for a 1 GW farm but have zero fuel costs. Wind is similar. Nuclear costs $10+ billion and takes 10+ years to build. Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) — cost per MWh over the lifetime — is a standard metric for comparing sources.

#### LCOE by Technology
As of 2024 (approximate): wind $25-40/MWh, solar $20-30/MWh, nuclear $50-80/MWh, natural gas $30-60/MWh (depends on fuel price), coal $35-55/MWh. Wind and solar are now the cheapest electricity sources in most locations — this is a massive shift from 2010 when renewables cost 2-3x more. This economic change is driving rapid renewable adoption without subsidies in many regions.

#### The Cost of Coal
Coal costs are rising — environmental regulations drive up operating costs (pollution control equipment). New coal plants are rarely built in the U.S. because renewables are cheaper. Coal employment has declined from 80,000 workers to 50,000 as coal plants close. This creates economic hardship in coal regions — policy discussions focus on just transitions (retraining, economic diversification).

#### Job Creation and Employment
Renewable energy jobs outnumber fossil fuel jobs in the U.S. (>600,000 renewable vs. <100,000 coal). Solar installers, wind technicians, and battery technicians are fast-growing careers. Fossil fuel jobs are concentrated in a few regions (coal in Appalachia, oil in Texas). Energy efficiency jobs (insulation, HVAC, audits) are local and can't be outsourced, providing community economic development.

#### Energy Poverty and Access
In the U.S., ~10-15% of households are energy insecure — spending >10% of income on energy, sometimes choosing between heating and food. Low-income communities disproportionately have inefficient housing, leading to higher energy burdens. Policy solutions include efficiency assistance for low-income homes, community solar (low-income residents can benefit from solar without installing it), and utility programs supporting vulnerable customers.
