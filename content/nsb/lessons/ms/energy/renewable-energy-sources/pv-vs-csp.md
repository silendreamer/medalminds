#### How CSP Works
Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) uses mirrors or lenses to focus sunlight onto a receiver, generating intense heat that drives a conventional steam turbine — no photovoltaic effect involved. The most common design is the parabolic trough: curved reflective troughs focus sunlight onto a fluid-filled tube running along their focal line, heating synthetic oil to ~400°C. The hot oil transfers heat to water, generating steam. Power towers (central receiver systems) use hundreds of computer-controlled mirrors (heliostats) focused on a tower-top receiver; the Ivanpah Solar Electric Generating System in California's Mojave Desert uses this design and generates 392 MW. Some CSP plants store excess heat in molten salt tanks, enabling electricity generation hours after sunset — a significant advantage over PV.

#### Trade-offs Between PV and CSP
PV: simpler, cheaper, works in diffuse light, easy to scale (from rooftops to multi-GW farms), no moving parts in the panels. CSP: requires direct, intense sunlight (works poorly in cloudy regions), more complex, higher capital cost, but can store energy thermally for hours without expensive batteries. As battery costs have fallen dramatically since 2010, PV + battery storage is increasingly competitive with CSP for storage capability, and utility-scale PV costs have dropped below CSP in most markets. New CSP projects are rare outside of sunny desert regions; PV dominates new solar installations worldwide.
