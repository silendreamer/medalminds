#### Water, Height, and Energy
Hydropower converts the energy stored in elevated water into electricity. Water held behind a dam has gravitational potential energy — energy due to its height above the turbine. When released, this energy converts to kinetic energy (the energy of motion) as water falls and accelerates. The turbine's blades are struck by this fast-moving water, spinning the turbine; the turbine spins the generator. The power available is proportional to both the height (head) of water and the flow rate (volume per second): P = ρ × g × Q × h, where ρ is water density (1,000 kg/m³), g is gravitational acceleration (9.8 m/s²), Q is flow rate in cubic meters per second, and h is head height in meters.

#### Types of Hydropower Plants
Conventional hydropower dams store water in reservoirs, providing dispatchable power — operators can increase or decrease output by opening or closing gates. Run-of-river plants have no large reservoir; they divert a portion of a river's flow through turbines and return it downstream. They are less impactful on ecosystems but cannot store energy for peak demand. Pumped-storage hydropower works in reverse: during low electricity demand (often at night), excess power from the grid pumps water uphill into an upper reservoir; during peak demand, water flows back down through turbines. Pumped storage provides ~95% of all U.S. grid-scale energy storage capacity — the largest battery in existence.

#### The Scale of Hydropower
Hydropower is the world's largest source of renewable electricity, providing about 16% of global electricity. In the United States, hydropower provides about 6% of electricity and 38% of renewable electricity. The largest U.S. dam is the Robert Moses Niagara Power Plant (2.7 GW) on the Niagara River in New York; Grand Coulee Dam on the Columbia River in Washington (6.8 GW) is the largest by generating capacity on the mainland. China's Three Gorges Dam (22.5 GW) is by far the world's largest power plant of any type.
