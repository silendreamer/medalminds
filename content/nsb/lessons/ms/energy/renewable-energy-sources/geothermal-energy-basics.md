#### The Earth's Internal Heat
Earth's interior is intensely hot: the inner core reaches about 5,400°C, similar to the Sun's surface. This heat comes from two sources: primordial heat left over from Earth's formation 4.5 billion years ago (when gravitational compression heated the planetary material) and ongoing radioactive decay of isotopes like uranium-238, thorium-232, and potassium-40 in Earth's mantle and crust. Heat flows outward from this hot interior toward the cooler surface — the geothermal gradient averages about 25–30°C per kilometer of depth. Where tectonic plates interact (mid-ocean ridges, subduction zones, hot spots), the gradient is much steeper and geothermal energy is most accessible.

#### How Geothermal Plants Generate Electricity
The most accessible geothermal resources are hydrothermal systems — places where groundwater naturally circulates deep enough to become very hot (150–350°C) and is naturally permeable to fluid flow. Three types of power plants exploit these systems. Dry steam plants (the oldest type) pipe steam directly from underground fractures to turbines — the Geysers in Northern California, the world's largest geothermal facility (1,520 MW), uses this method. Flash steam plants are most common: water at 180°C or above is brought to the surface and depressurized, causing it to "flash" to steam that drives turbines. Binary cycle plants use lower-temperature water (100–180°C) to vaporize a secondary working fluid with a lower boiling point (like isobutane), which drives turbines — expanding geothermal viability to lower-temperature resources.

#### Enhanced Geothermal Systems
Conventional geothermal requires naturally hot, permeable, water-bearing rock — limiting it to geologically active regions. Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) inject water into hot dry rock anywhere, creating artificial permeability through hydraulic fracturing, then circulate water through the heated rock and back to the surface. EGS could make geothermal viable across most of the United States. DOE's Enhanced Geothermal Shot initiative (launched 2022) aims to reduce EGS costs by 90% by 2035. NREL and the Geothermal Rising organization estimate that EGS could provide over 90% of U.S. electricity demand — though significant technical challenges remain.
