#### Mining to Enrichment
Natural uranium is about 99.3% U-238 and only 0.7% U-235. Most reactors require enriched uranium — fuel in which the U-235 fraction has been increased to 3–5%. Uranium is mined in Kazakhstan (world's largest producer), Canada, and Australia, then converted to uranium hexafluoride gas (UF₆) for enrichment. Enrichment is performed by spinning UF₆ in high-speed centrifuges: the slightly heavier U-238 migrates outward, gradually increasing the U-235 fraction. The United States has enrichment facilities; DOE's Y-12 National Security Complex in Oak Ridge, Tennessee plays a key role in maintaining the nation's nuclear fuel and weapons programs.

#### Fuel Fabrication and In-Reactor Use
Enriched uranium is converted to uranium dioxide powder, pressed into ceramic pellets, and loaded into zirconium alloy cladding tubes to form fuel rods. Thousands of fuel rods are bundled into fuel assemblies loaded into the reactor core. Fuel typically remains in the reactor for 3–5 years, with one-third of the core replaced each refueling outage (every 18 months for most U.S. reactors). During operation, some U-238 absorbs neutrons and converts to plutonium-239, which itself fissions and contributes to energy output — in a typical reactor, about one-third of the total energy comes from fission of bred plutonium.

#### The Back End: Reprocessing vs. Direct Disposal
After removal, spent fuel contains unused uranium, plutonium, and fission products. Most countries (including the United States since 1977) do not reprocess spent fuel — it is stored directly and eventually buried. France, Japan, Russia, and the UK reprocess: chemically separating uranium and plutonium for reuse, reducing waste volume and recovering fuel value. Reprocessing is controversial because separated plutonium could be diverted for weapons. The United States chose direct disposal partly for nonproliferation reasons, and DOE manages a comprehensive spent fuel monitoring program.
