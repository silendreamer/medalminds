#### How the Sun Fuses Hydrogen
The Sun's core operates at about 15 million degrees Celsius — much cooler than fusion reactors aim for, because the Sun has 1.4 million km radius worth of gravity to confine its plasma. The dominant fusion process in sun-like stars is the proton-proton chain: four hydrogen nuclei fuse (in a multi-step sequence) to form one helium-4 nucleus, releasing 26.7 MeV of energy and two neutrinos. The Sun converts about 600 million tons of hydrogen to helium every second, losing about 4 million tons of mass as energy (E = mc²). It has been doing this for 4.6 billion years and has enough hydrogen to continue for another 5 billion.

Stars more massive than about 1.3 solar masses use the CNO cycle (carbon-nitrogen-oxygen cycle) as a catalyst for the same net hydrogen-to-helium fusion. Stars that exhaust their hydrogen fuse heavier elements — up to iron in the most massive stars — releasing progressively less energy per reaction. Iron fusion absorbs energy rather than releasing it, triggering the core collapse that leads to a supernova.

#### Why Earth Can't Use Gravity for Confinement
Earth cannot replicate stellar fusion using gravitational confinement — we lack the mass. Earth's gravity is roughly 330,000 times weaker than the Sun's at its core. Terrestrial fusion must instead use magnetic fields (tokamaks) or inertial confinement (laser implosion) to briefly achieve the densities and temperatures needed for fusion. This is why controlled fusion is 70 years in the making: we are attempting to bottle a star.

#### Review Questions

2. Why can Earth not use gravitational confinement for fusion like stars do?
