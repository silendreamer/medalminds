#### Splitting the Atom
Nuclear fission occurs when a heavy nucleus absorbs a neutron and becomes so unstable that it splits into two smaller nuclei (called fission fragments) plus 2–3 additional neutrons and a large release of energy — mostly as kinetic energy (heat) and gamma radiation. Uranium-235 is the most common fuel because it fissions readily when struck by a slow (thermal) neutron. A typical fission reaction splits U-235 into barium-141 and krypton-92 plus 3 neutrons and about 200 million electron volts of energy. For comparison, burning a carbon atom releases about 4 electron volts — fission releases 50 million times more energy per atom.

#### The Chain Reaction
The key to releasing useful nuclear energy is the chain reaction. Each fission event releases 2–3 neutrons, which can each trigger another fission event, which releases more neutrons, and so on. If the chain reaction is uncontrolled and grows exponentially, you get a nuclear explosion (as in a bomb). If it is carefully controlled — maintained at exactly one new fission per fission event — you get a steady, controllable release of heat: exactly what a power reactor does. The amount of fuel needed to sustain a chain reaction is called the critical mass. Below critical mass, too many neutrons escape without causing fission; above critical mass, the reaction accelerates.

#### Moderators: Slowing Neutrons Down
Freshly released fission neutrons travel very fast (fast neutrons). Uranium-235 is most likely to fission when struck by slow (thermal) neutrons. Reactors use a moderator — most commonly ordinary water — to slow neutrons down through repeated collisions, like a bowling ball slowing down by bumping into billiard balls. Heavy water (deuterium oxide, D₂O) is a more efficient moderator used in CANDU reactors. Graphite was used as a moderator in early reactors including the ill-fated Chernobyl RBMK design. The moderator is distinct from the coolant, though in light-water reactors they are the same substance (ordinary water).

#### Control Rods: Managing the Reaction
Operators control reactor power by inserting or withdrawing control rods made of materials that absorb neutrons — typically boron, cadmium, or hafnium. Fully inserting all control rods absorbs enough neutrons to shut the reaction down (SCRAM). Partially inserting them reduces power; withdrawing them allows power to increase. Control rods are the primary safety mechanism. In most Western reactors, a loss of coolant also reduces moderation, slowing the reaction automatically — this "negative void coefficient" is a built-in passive safety feature. The Chernobyl reactor had a dangerous positive void coefficient under certain conditions, which contributed to its catastrophic runaway.

#### Energy Output and Efficiency
A single uranium fuel pellet about the size of a pencil eraser contains as much energy as 17,000 cubic feet of natural gas, 1,780 pounds of coal, or 149 gallons of oil. The United States has about 93 operating nuclear reactors that together supply roughly 20% of U.S. electricity — with a capacity factor (fraction of time running at full power) of about 93%, the highest of any energy source. Because they generate electricity continuously day and night regardless of weather, nuclear plants provide reliable baseload power.
