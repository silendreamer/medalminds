#### What Fission Leaves Behind
When uranium-235 fissions, the two fragment nuclei — called fission products — are themselves radioactive isotopes of elements like cesium, strontium, iodine, and barium. Cesium-137 (half-life 30 years) and strontium-90 (half-life 29 years) are among the most problematic because they mimic potassium and calcium in biological systems, potentially concentrating in muscle and bone. After about 10 half-lives (300 years), cesium-137 and strontium-90 decay to negligible levels. But spent nuclear fuel also contains transuranic elements — isotopes of neptunium, plutonium, americium, and curium — with half-lives of thousands to millions of years.

#### High-Level Waste Storage
Spent fuel rods are initially stored in deep pools of water at the reactor site. Water provides both cooling (spent fuel still generates decay heat) and radiation shielding. After several years, cooled spent fuel can be transferred to dry casks — sealed steel and concrete containers that passively dissipate heat and block radiation. The United States has no permanent repository for high-level nuclear waste; spent fuel from decades of reactor operation is stored at more than 70 sites across 35 states. Yucca Mountain in Nevada was designated as the long-term repository site but has been mired in political disputes since the 1980s. Most nuclear nations are pursuing deep geological repositories — burying waste in stable rock formations a half-kilometer underground.
