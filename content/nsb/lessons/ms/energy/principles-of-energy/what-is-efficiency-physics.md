#### Defining Efficiency
Efficiency measures how much of the input energy becomes useful output energy. The formula is: Efficiency (%) = (Useful Energy Output / Total Energy Input) × 100. Or equivalently: Efficiency = Useful Power Output / Total Power Input. An efficiency of 60% means 60% of input energy does useful work; 40% is wasted (usually as heat). A perfectly efficient machine would have efficiency = 100%, but this is physically impossible for any real device — the Second Law of Thermodynamics guarantees some energy is always lost to thermal entropy.

#### Why 100% Efficiency Is Impossible
The Second Law of Thermodynamics states that in any spontaneous energy conversion, some energy becomes dispersed thermal energy (entropy increases). Even a "perfect" machine with no friction or air resistance generates some waste as electrons resist current flow (in electrical devices) or molecules collide (in thermal devices). This fundamental limit means engineers focus on minimizing losses rather than eliminating them. Real-world efficiencies: diesel engine ~45%, electric motor ~90%, LED bulb ~90%, incandescent bulb ~5%, gasoline engine ~20-25%, coal power plant ~35%.

#### What Happens to "Lost" Energy?
"Lost" energy doesn't disappear — it converts to thermal energy that spreads into the environment. A spinning motor warms the air around it. A light bulb filament glows AND heats the room. Friction between brake pads and rotors converts kinetic energy to heat (you can smell this). In power electronics, waste heat is so significant that chips require cooling fans. The energy is "lost" only in the sense that it's no longer in a useful, concentrated form.

#### Types of Energy Losses
**Friction losses:** Moving parts rubbing against each other convert kinetic energy to thermal. Minimized by lubrication, smooth surfaces, and bearings. **Electrical resistance losses:** Current through wires generates heat (P = I²R). High-voltage transmission reduces current and thus resistance losses. **Heat exhaust losses:** Heat engines cannot convert all thermal energy to work — they must dump some heat to a cold reservoir (Carnot's theorem). **Radiation losses:** Hot objects radiate infrared energy to surroundings.

#### Improving Real-World Efficiency
Engineers reduce losses through: insulation (reduce heat loss from buildings and pipes), lubrication (reduce friction), better materials (superconductors for zero electrical resistance), waste heat recovery (use exhaust heat for another purpose — "cogeneration"), and improved design (aerodynamic shapes reduce air resistance). The DOE's Advanced Manufacturing Office supports research on energy efficiency in industrial processes.
