#### Air Pollution from Coal Combustion
Burning coal releases multiple pollutants: (1) **SO₂** (sulfur dioxide) — causes acid rain, respiratory problems; controlled by scrubbers. (2) **NOₓ** (nitrogen oxides) — contributes to smog and acid rain; controlled by combustion modifications and selective catalytic reduction. (3) **Particulate matter** (PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀) — lung damage; controlled by electrostatic precipitators and fabric filters. (4) **Mercury** — neurotoxin that bioaccumulates in fish; controlled by activated carbon injection. (5) **CO₂** — greenhouse gas, primary driver of climate change; no cost-effective large-scale removal technology widely deployed yet (carbon capture still developing).

#### Coal Ash: A Solid Waste Challenge
Burning coal produces approximately 100 million tons of coal combustion residuals (CCR, or coal ash) in the U.S. annually. This includes fly ash (fine particles captured from flue gas), bottom ash (collected from the boiler floor), and flue gas desulfurization (FGD) gypsum. Coal ash contains heavy metals including arsenic, mercury, chromium, and cadmium. The 2014 EPA Coal Combustion Residuals Rule established first-ever federal standards for CCR storage and disposal, following the 2008 Kingston Fossil Plant ash spill in Tennessee — one of the largest industrial accidents in U.S. history.

#### Acid Mine Drainage
When coal mines expose iron sulfide minerals (pyrite, FeS₂) to air and water, sulfuric acid forms: FeS₂ + O₂ + H₂O → Fe²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ + H⁺ (simplified). This acid mine drainage (AMD) can acidify nearby streams and rivers, dissolving heavy metals and killing aquatic life. AMD is a long-term problem — mines can generate acidic drainage for decades or centuries after closure. Treatment involves adding lime (calcium hydroxide) to neutralize the acid and precipitate metals.

#### Land Disturbance from Surface Mining
Mountaintop removal mining — common in Appalachia — blasts off the tops of mountains to expose thin coal seams. The overburden (blasted rock) is pushed into adjacent valleys, burying headwater streams. Critics argue this practice causes irreversible ecosystem damage; proponents note it employs far fewer workers per ton of coal than underground mining and can reclaim land for economic uses. The EPA reviews permits under the Clean Water Act and Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act.
