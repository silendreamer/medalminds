#### The Six Criteria Air Pollutants
The EPA regulates six "criteria" air pollutants under the Clean Air Act (1970 and subsequent amendments): particulate matter (PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀), ground-level ozone (O₃), carbon monoxide (CO), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), and lead (Pb). Fossil fuel combustion is a major source of all except lead. National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) set maximum allowable concentrations to protect public health. Areas not meeting NAAQS are "nonattainment areas" and face additional regulations.

#### PM₂.₅: The Most Dangerous Pollutant
Fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅ — particles smaller than 2.5 micrometers) is the air pollutant most strongly linked to premature death. These particles are small enough to penetrate deep into the lungs and even enter the bloodstream. Long-term exposure increases risk of heart attack, stroke, lung cancer, and asthma. Coal combustion is a major source of PM₂.₅ (directly and through SO₂ → sulfate aerosol formation). Electrostatic precipitators and fabric filters capture >99% of particulates before they exit plant stacks.

#### Ground-Level Ozone: Secondary Pollution
Ground-level ozone (O₃) is not directly emitted — it forms when NOₓ and volatile organic compounds (VOCs, including unburned hydrocarbons from gasoline) react in sunlight. This is why ozone levels peak on hot, sunny, stagnant air days (summer smog). Ozone irritates airways, reduces lung function, and damages crops and ecosystems. It should not be confused with stratospheric ozone, which protects Earth from UV radiation. The Los Angeles basin has historically had the worst ozone air quality in the U.S. due to its geography, climate, and vehicle density.

#### Acid Rain: Sulfur and Nitrogen
SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (sulfurous acid); SO₂ + O₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid). NO₂ + H₂O → HNO₃ (nitric acid). These acids fall in rain, snow, or fog — "acid deposition." Acid rain lowers lake and stream pH, killing fish and amphibians; dissolves calcium from soils, harming forest growth; and corrodes stone buildings and monuments (including limestone and marble). The Clean Air Act's Acid Rain Program (1990) created a SO₂ cap-and-trade system that reduced U.S. SO₂ emissions by ~90% between 1990 and 2020.
