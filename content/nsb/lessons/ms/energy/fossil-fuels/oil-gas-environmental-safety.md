#### Major Oil Spills: Causes and Impacts
Oil spills cause severe ecological damage to marine and coastal ecosystems. The Deepwater Horizon explosion (April 2010) was the largest marine oil spill in U.S. history — a blowout preventer failure at a BP well 1.5 km below the Gulf of Mexico surface released approximately 4.9 million barrels (780 million liters) of crude oil over 87 days. The spill killed massive numbers of marine mammals, sea turtles, and birds, and devastated Gulf fisheries. The disaster led to sweeping offshore drilling safety reforms and a $65 billion total cost to BP.

#### Pipeline Leaks and Infrastructure
The U.S. has approximately 3.3 million kilometers of pipelines transporting oil, natural gas, and refined products. Pipeline failures — from corrosion, construction defects, or third-party damage — cause about 2,000 spills annually of varying sizes. The Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA) enforces pipeline safety regulations. Natural gas pipeline leaks release methane directly into the atmosphere — a significant greenhouse gas concern because methane is ~80× more potent than CO₂ over 20 years.

#### Hydraulic Fracturing: Water and Seismic Concerns
Fracking uses large volumes of water (2–8 million gallons per well) mixed with sand and chemicals, injected at high pressure to crack shale rock. Concerns include: (1) Water use in water-scarce regions. (2) Contamination of groundwater if well casings fail. (3) Disposal of produced water (brine that returns to the surface with the gas) — injecting it into disposal wells has been linked to induced seismicity (earthquakes) in Oklahoma and Kansas. (4) Air quality impacts from volatile organic compound (VOC) emissions at well sites.

#### Natural Gas as a "Bridge Fuel": The Debate
Natural gas produces about half the CO₂ of coal per unit of electricity (because it has more hydrogen per carbon than coal). This led to promotion of gas as a "bridge fuel" — lower emissions while renewables scale up. However, methane leakage rates matter enormously: if more than ~3.5% of natural gas leaks before combustion, its climate impact equals or exceeds coal (over a 20-year timeframe). Studies suggest U.S. methane leak rates range from 1–3%, making gas a modest improvement over coal but not a long-term climate solution.
