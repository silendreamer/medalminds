#### What Is Crude Oil?
Crude oil (petroleum) is a complex mixture of hydrocarbon molecules ranging from small (methane, 1 carbon) to very large (asphalt, 25+ carbons). It also contains sulfur, nitrogen, oxygen compounds, and traces of metals. Raw crude oil as pumped from the ground has limited use — it must be refined to separate and process its components into useful fuels and chemicals. Crude oil quality varies: "light sweet crude" (low density, low sulfur) is most valuable and easiest to refine; "heavy sour crude" requires more processing.

#### Fractional Distillation
The primary refining process is **fractional distillation**. Crude oil is heated to ~370°C in a furnace and pumped into a tall distillation tower. Different hydrocarbons condense at different temperatures (their **boiling points**) as vapors rise and cool up the tower. Smaller, lighter molecules have lower boiling points and rise higher; larger, heavier molecules condense lower. Fractions are drawn off at different heights:
- Top (lowest boiling point): liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), naphtha, aviation gasoline
- Middle: gasoline, jet fuel, kerosene, diesel
- Bottom (highest boiling point): fuel oil, lubricating oil, asphalt

#### Cracking: Making More Gasoline
Fractional distillation gives more heavy fractions (fuel oil, asphalt) than the market demands and less gasoline and diesel than needed. **Catalytic cracking** breaks large hydrocarbon molecules into smaller ones by heating them with a catalyst — converting heavy fuel oil into gasoline and diesel. **Hydrocracking** adds hydrogen under pressure to produce cleaner, higher-quality products. These processes allow refiners to tailor their product output to market demand.

#### Petroleum Products: From Gas to Plastics
Petroleum is not just fuel. Key products: (1) Gasoline (petrol) — transportation fuel. (2) Diesel — trucks, trains, ships. (3) Jet fuel (kerosene) — aviation. (4) Heating oil — residential heating in the Northeast U.S. (5) LPG (propane, butane) — cooking and heating fuel. (6) Petrochemicals (ethylene, propylene) — the building blocks of plastics, synthetic rubber, pharmaceuticals, and fertilizers. About 15% of a barrel of crude oil becomes petrochemical feedstocks rather than fuel.
