#### The Four Floral Whorls
Sepals (usually green) protect the flower bud. Petals (often colorful) attract pollinators. Stamens (male parts) produce pollen in anthers; filaments support anthers. The pistil (female part) has a stigma (receives pollen), style, and ovary (contains ovules). Together these four whorls form a complete flower.

#### Anther and Pollen Production
Inside an anther, pollen grains develop. Each pollen grain contains a nucleus — actually two nuclei in flowering plants (called the generative and vegetative nuclei). Pollen is surrounded by a tough, sculpted wall that's different for each species. The male gametophyte is essentially the mature pollen grain.

#### Ovule and Embryo Sac
Inside the ovary, ovules develop. Within each ovule is the female gametophyte, called the embryo sac. The embryo sac typically contains the egg cell, polar nuclei, and other cells. This female gametophyte is microscopic and stays in the ovule throughout its life.

#### Pollination: Moving Pollen
Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma. Self-pollination occurs when pollen from one flower pollinates the same flower. Cross-pollination occurs when pollen from one plant pollinates another. Most plants have mechanisms promoting cross-pollination because it increases genetic diversity.

#### Pollination Vectors: Wind, Water, and Animals
Wind-pollinated plants (grasses, many trees) have small, light pollen and reduced petals. Water-pollinated plants (seagrasses) have pollen that sinks. Animal-pollinated plants (most flowering plants) have showy flowers, sweet nectar, and sticky pollen. Bees, butterflies, hummingbirds, and bats are major animal pollinators.
