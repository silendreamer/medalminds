#### What Are Nonvascular Plants?
Nonvascular plants (mosses, liverworts, hornworts) lack xylem and phloem, so they cannot transport water efficiently over distances. They stay small and must live in moist environments. Water and nutrients move from cell to cell by diffusion. Nonvascular plants reproduce with spores, not seeds.

#### What Are Vascular Plants?
Vascular plants have xylem and phloem that move water and nutrients throughout the plant body. This allows them to grow taller and survive in drier environments. Vascular plants include ferns, gymnosperms (conifers), and angiosperms (flowering plants).

#### Ferns: The Ancient Vascular Plants
Ferns were among the first vascular plants, thriving for hundreds of millions of years. They reproduce using spores released from structures called sori on the underside of fronds (leaves). Ferns still need moisture to complete their life cycle.

#### Gymnosperms and Angiosperms
Gymnosperms (pines, firs, spruces) produce naked seeds in cones. Angiosperms (flowering plants) enclose seeds in fruits. Both have vascular tissue and can live in diverse climates. Angiosperms are the most diverse and dominant plants on Earth today.

#### Why Vascular Tissue Matters
Vascular tissue was a major evolutionary breakthrough. It allowed plants to grow taller, transport resources efficiently, and colonize drier lands. Without vascular tissue, plants cannot reach beyond a few inches in height.
