#### Seed Structure: One vs. Two Cotyledons
Monocots (grasses, lilies, orchids) have one cotyledon (seed leaf) in their seed. Dicots (beans, tomatoes, oak trees) have two cotyledons. The cotyledon provides nutrients to the germinating seed until the plant can photosynthesize.

#### Leaf Venation Patterns
Monocot leaves have parallel venation — veins run side-by-side from base to tip (think of corn or grass leaves). Dicot leaves have net venation — veins branch like a network (think of oak or maple leaves). These patterns reflect how water and sugar are distributed.

#### Root Systems
Monocots typically have fibrous root systems (many thin roots, like wheat). Dicots usually have taproots (one main root, like carrots or radishes). This difference relates to seed structure and how each class germinates.

#### Stem and Flower Structure
Monocot flower parts come in multiples of three (three petals, three sepals). Monocot stems have scattered vascular bundles. Dicot flower parts come in fours or fives, and dicot stems have a ring of vascular bundles. These differences reflect how each group evolved.

#### Common Monocots vs. Dicots
Monocots: grasses (wheat, corn, rice), lilies, tulips, orchids, palms. Dicots: beans, peas, roses, daisies, oak, maple, tomatoes. Monocots and dicots make up about 25% and 75% of flowering plants, respectively.
