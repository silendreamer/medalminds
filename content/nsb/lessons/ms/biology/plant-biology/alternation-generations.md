#### The Gametophyte: Haploid Gamete Producer
The gametophyte is haploid (n) — it has one set of chromosomes. It produces gametes (sperm and eggs) through mitosis. In ferns, the gametophyte is a small, heart-shaped structure called a prothallus, usually just a few millimeters. In flowering plants, the gametophyte is very small — the female gametophyte is the ovule's embryo sac, and the male gametophyte is pollen.

#### The Sporophyte: Diploid Spore Producer
The sporophyte is diploid (2n) — it has two sets of chromosomes. It produces spores through meiosis. Spores are haploid and disperse to new locations. In ferns, the sporophyte is the large fern plant. In flowering plants, the sporophyte is the adult plant.

#### Why This Alternation Evolved
Alternation of generations may have evolved from a strategy where a diploid body was more robust for harsh environments, while a haploid body produced many spores for dispersal. The pattern persists in all plants today, even though the two stages vary dramatically in size and prominence.

#### Ferns Show Clear Alternation
Fern sporophytes are the large plants with fronds. Sori (clusters of sporangia) on frond undersides produce millions of spores. A spore lands on moist soil, germinates, and grows into a tiny gametophyte (prothallus). The prothallus makes sperm and eggs. If moisture allows, sperm swim to an egg, fusing to form a diploid zygote that grows into a sporophyte.

#### Flowering Plants: Dominant Sporophyte
In flowering plants, the sporophyte (the plant) is large and complex. The gametophyte is reduced to a few cells — the female embryo sac in the ovule, and the male pollen grain. This reduction of the gametophyte allowed flowers and seeds to evolve, making flowering plants wildly successful.
