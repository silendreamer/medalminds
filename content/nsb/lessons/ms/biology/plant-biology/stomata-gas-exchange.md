#### Structure of a Stoma
A stoma is a pore surrounded by two bean-shaped guard cells. When guard cells fill with water (become turgid), they swell and the stoma opens. When they lose water (become flaccid), they collapse and close the stoma. Guard cells have thicker inner walls, so they bow outward when turgid, opening the pore.

#### How Stomata Open and Close
Stomata open during the day when light is available for photosynthesis. Potassium ions (K⁺) accumulate in guard cells, water follows by osmosis, making them turgid and opening the stoma. At night or during drought, guard cells lose water and close, conserving the plant's water supply.

#### Gas Exchange Through Stomata
CO₂ enters through open stomata for photosynthesis. O₂, a byproduct of photosynthesis, exits through the same stomata. This two-way gas exchange is essential for both photosynthesis and cellular respiration in all plant cells.

#### Transpiration and Water Loss
Transpiration is the evaporation of water from plant leaves through stomata. It's essential for transporting water and minerals from roots to leaves, but it also causes significant water loss. Plants lose water vapor passively when stomata are open — it's unavoidable during photosynthesis.

#### Adaptations to Reduce Water Loss
Waxy cuticle reduces cuticular transpiration. Stomata on leaf undersides (not tops) reduce direct sun exposure. Some plants close stomata at midday when temperatures peak. Desert plants have fewer stomata and thicker cuticles to minimize water loss in arid conditions.
