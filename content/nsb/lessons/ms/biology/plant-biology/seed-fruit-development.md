#### Double Fertilization in Flowering Plants
After pollination, pollen grows a pollen tube down the style. One sperm nucleus fuses with the egg, forming a diploid zygote (the embryo). The second sperm nucleus fuses with polar nuclei, forming a triploid nucleus that develops into endosperm (nutritive tissue). This double fertilization is unique to flowering plants.

#### Seed Development
The zygote undergoes mitosis to become an embryo with a radicle (future root), hypocotyl (future stem base), and cotyledons (seed leaves). Endosperm surrounds the embryo and stores starch, proteins, and oils. A seed coat forms from the integuments. The mature seed contains the embryo, endosperm, seed coat, and stored nutrients.

#### Types of Seeds
Monocot seeds (corn, wheat) have one cotyledon; the endosperm surrounds it. Dicot seeds (beans, peas) have two cotyledons that often store nutrients, reducing endosperm. Some seeds have little endosperm and large cotyledons; others have the reverse. Seed structure varies but always protects and nourishes the embryo.

#### Fruit Development
After fertilization, the ovary's walls develop into a fruit. The ovary may become fleshy (apple, berry) or dry (bean pod, acorn). A fruit's function is to protect seeds and aid in dispersal. A single ovary produces a simple fruit; several ovaries in a flower produce an aggregate fruit (raspberry).

#### Seed Dispersal
Seeds must disperse to avoid competing with the parent plant. Wind-dispersed seeds (maple, dandelion) are light and may have wings or parachutes. Water-dispersed seeds float or sink; some are buoyant (coconuts). Animal-dispersed seeds are in tasty fruits — animals eat the fruit and disperse seeds in feces. Explosive fruits (peas) hurl seeds away.
