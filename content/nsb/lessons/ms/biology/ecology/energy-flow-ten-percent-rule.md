#### Where Does Energy Go?
When a deer eats grass, it doesn't capture all the grass's energy. About 90% is lost: some grass is indigestible (cellulose), some energy is used in the deer's own metabolism (respiration), and some is lost as heat. Only ~10% is incorporated into new deer tissue (biomass) and available to a predator that eats the deer. This is the *10% rule* or *rule of trophic efficiency*.

#### The Energy Pyramid
An *energy pyramid* (or ecological pyramid) shows the amount of energy (usually measured in kilocalories or kilojoules) at each trophic level. The base (producers) is always widest. Each level above has ~10% of the energy of the level below. Example: if producers have 10,000 kcal, primary consumers have ~1,000 kcal, secondary consumers ~100 kcal, tertiary consumers ~10 kcal.

#### Why Carnivores Are Rare
The 10% rule means that a given area of land can support far more herbivores than carnivores, and far more carnivores than apex predators. A 10,000 kg area of grass can support ~1,000 kg of deer, which can support ~100 kg of wolves. Apex predators (great white sharks, Bengal tigers, polar bears) require enormous territories to find enough prey. This is why they are naturally rare and extremely vulnerable to habitat loss.

#### Biomass Pyramids
A *biomass pyramid* shows the total mass of living organisms at each trophic level. Like energy pyramids, they are usually widest at the base. However, some aquatic ecosystems have *inverted biomass pyramids* because phytoplankton reproduce so quickly that a small biomass supports a much larger biomass of zooplankton — the biomass measured at one moment doesn't capture the rapid turnover.
