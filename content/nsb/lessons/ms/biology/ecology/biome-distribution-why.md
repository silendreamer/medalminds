#### Reading a Climate Graph
A *climate graph* (climatograph) shows monthly average temperature (line graph) and monthly precipitation (bar graph) for one location. By examining temperature range (seasonal variation) and precipitation amount and timing, you can identify the likely biome: high, uniform temperature + uniform high precipitation → tropical rainforest; large temperature swings + moderate precipitation → temperate deciduous forest; extreme cold year-round + low precipitation → tundra.

#### The Rain Shadow Effect
Mountain ranges create *rain shadows*. As moist air rises over a mountain (windward side), it cools and releases precipitation (often as rain or snow). By the time the air descends on the other side (leeward side), it is dry and warm. The leeward side of the mountain becomes arid. The Cascade Mountains create a dramatic rain shadow: the Olympic Peninsula (windward) receives ~380 cm/year, while eastern Washington (leeward) receives ~20 cm/year — desert conditions. The Great Basin Desert exists in the rain shadow of the Sierra Nevada.

#### Applying Climate Data
If a climate graph shows: average temperature 25–30°C year-round, monthly precipitation 15–30 cm throughout the year → tropical rainforest. If it shows: summer 25°C, winter −5°C, summer maximum precipitation, 75 cm/year total → temperate deciduous forest. If it shows: average temperature −10 to 5°C, precipitation 15–25 cm/year → tundra.

#### Review Questions
1. What two variables are shown on a climate graph (climatograph)?
2. Explain the rain shadow effect. What happens to precipitation on the windward vs. leeward sides of a mountain?
3. If a location has 28°C temperatures year-round and 250 cm of rain annually, what biome is it likely in?
