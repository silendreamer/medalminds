#### The Ocean Food Web
Marine food webs begin with *phytoplankton* (microscopic algae and cyanobacteria) that photosynthesize near the surface. *Zooplankton* (tiny animals and animal-like protists, like copepods and krill) are primary consumers. Small fish (anchovies, herring, sardines) eat zooplankton. Larger fish (tuna, cod) eat small fish. Apex predators (sharks, orcas, polar bears) are at the top.

#### Applying the 10% Rule
If phytoplankton in a section of ocean have 1,000,000 kcal: → zooplankton have ~100,000 kcal → small fish have ~10,000 kcal → tuna have ~1,000 kcal → great white shark has ~100 kcal. This is why overfishing tuna has enormous consequences: removing one trophic level disrupts all others. It's also why it's ecologically "expensive" to eat tuna (4 trophic levels from producers) compared to eating anchovies (2 levels).

#### Why Oceans Are Productive but Fragile
Oceans cover 71% of Earth's surface and produce ~50% of global oxygen. Despite their vast size, ocean food webs can collapse rapidly — overfishing of a keystone species, ocean acidification reducing phytoplankton, or plastic pollution blocking sunlight can ripple through the entire web. The 1990s collapse of Atlantic cod in the Grand Banks (from millions of tons of fish to near-zero in decades) demonstrates how quickly overfishing can destroy a food web.
