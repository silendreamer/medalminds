#### Food Chain vs. Food Web
A *food chain* is a simple, linear sequence: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk. Real ecosystems are far more complex: a grasshopper might be eaten by a frog, a spider, a bird, or a lizard. A food *web* shows all feeding relationships in an ecosystem — interconnected food chains. Webs are more realistic and more stable: if one prey species declines, a predator with multiple prey options can switch.

#### Keystone Species
A *keystone species* has a disproportionately large effect on its ecosystem relative to its abundance. Remove a keystone species and the ecosystem dramatically changes. Sea otters are a classic example: they eat sea urchins, which eat kelp. Without sea otters, urchin populations explode, kelp forests are destroyed, and all the species that depend on kelp (fish, seabirds, seals) lose habitat. Sea otters are not the most abundant species, but they hold the community together.

#### Trophic Cascades
A *trophic cascade* occurs when a change at one trophic level ripples up or down the food web. When wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone in 1995, elk behavior changed — elk avoided grazing in open areas where wolves could easily catch them. This allowed vegetation to recover along riverbanks, which stabilized stream banks, changed water flow, and even affected fish populations. The wolf's reintroduction changed the physical geography of the park — a dramatic cascade.

#### Stability Through Diversity
Food webs with more species and more feeding connections are more stable. If a spider web has only one prey and it disappears, the spider starves. If it has many prey options, losing one is manageable. Biodiversity increases ecosystem resilience — the ability to recover from disturbance.
