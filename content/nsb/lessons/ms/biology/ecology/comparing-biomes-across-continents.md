#### Convergent Evolution in Biomes
The Sonoran Desert (North America) and the Namib Desert (Africa) have similar climate conditions, and their plants and animals show striking similarities: both have tall columnar cacti-like plants (though American saguaro cacti and African euphorbs are not related), both have burrowing rodents, both have venomous sit-and-wait predators. This is *convergent evolution* — similar environments select for similar adaptations in unrelated lineages.

#### Chaparral Around the World
Chaparral (California), maquis (Mediterranean), fynbos (South Africa), matorral (Chile), and mallee (Australia) all share the same climate: hot dry summers, mild wet winters. They all have fire-adapted, drought-tolerant shrubs with hard, waxy leaves. Yet each region has evolved its own unique set of plant species. The climate is the same; the evolutionary history is different.

#### Grasslands as Ecological Equivalents
Each continent's grasslands support large herbivores: Africa has wildebeest and zebra; North America historically had bison; South America has guanacos; Australia has kangaroos. These animals play ecologically equivalent roles — grazing, moving, fertilizing — but are not closely related. Their similarities reflect convergent adaptations to similar environments (grassland, seasonal drought, high plant productivity).

#### Review Questions
1. What is an ecological equivalent? Give one example from two different continents.
2. Why do chaparral biomes around the world have similar plant forms, even though their species are different?
3. How does convergent evolution in biomes support the idea that environment shapes adaptation?
