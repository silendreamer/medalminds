#### When Dominance Isn't Simple
Mendel's pea plants showed clean dominant/recessive patterns, but many genes don't work that way. Two major exceptions are incomplete dominance and codominance. Both occur when neither allele is fully dominant over the other.

#### Incomplete Dominance
In incomplete dominance, the heterozygous phenotype is a blend of the two homozygous phenotypes. Classic example: snapdragon flowers. Red (RR) × white (WW) = pink (RW). The pink phenotype is intermediate — you can't see the individual alleles, just the blended result. This looks like "blending" inheritance but isn't — the alleles themselves are unchanged and still separate in gametes.

#### Codominance
In codominance, both alleles are fully expressed simultaneously in the heterozygote — neither is blended away. The best human example is ABO blood type. The Iᴬ allele produces A antigens; the Iᴮ allele produces B antigens. A person with IᴬIᴮ has blood type AB — both A and B antigens are present on red blood cells at the same time.

#### ABO Blood Types in Detail
Blood type is determined by three alleles: Iᴬ (dominant, produces A antigen), Iᴮ (dominant, produces B antigen), and i (recessive, produces neither). Genotype IᴬIᴬ or Iᴬi = Type A. Genotype IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi = Type B. Genotype IᴬIᴮ = Type AB (codominant). Genotype ii = Type O. Blood type compatibility is critical for transfusions.

#### Rh Factor
The Rh factor is a separate protein on red blood cells. Rh+ (dominant) means you have it; Rh- means you don't. Most people are Rh+. Your complete blood type (e.g., O+, AB-) combines ABO type with Rh factor.
