#### The Orographic Effect: Mountains Make Weather
When moist air is forced upward by a mountain range, it cools (at 10°C per 1000 m), and water vapor condenses, producing heavy rainfall on the **windward side** (the side facing the prevailing wind). The air descends on the other side and warms, but it has lost most of its moisture — creating a dry **rain shadow** on the **leeward side**. The Cascades and Sierra Nevada create rain shadows that cause the deserts of the Great Basin and the Mojave. The Himalayas block monsoon moisture from reaching Central Asia, creating the Gobi and Taklamakan deserts to the north.

#### Mountain Life Zones
Mountains create stacked climate zones called **life zones** (or altitudinal zones) — like traveling from the equator toward the poles as you climb. At the base: **foothills/chaparral**. Moving up: **mixed forest** → **coniferous forest** → **subalpine forest** → **alpine tundra** → **permanent ice/rock**. Each zone has characteristic plants and animals adapted to the temperature, moisture, and UV radiation. The **timberline** (or treeline) is the altitude above which trees cannot grow; it's lower nearer the poles (about 3,500 m in Colorado, 1,500 m in Iceland).

#### Mountain Glaciers: The World's Water Towers
Mountain glaciers and snowpack store enormous amounts of freshwater that releases slowly through the summer, providing reliable water to rivers in dry months. The glaciers of the **Hindu Kush-Himalaya** (called the "Third Pole") supply water to major Asian rivers — the Indus, Ganges, Brahmaputra, Mekong, Yangtze — on which ~2 billion people depend. As climate change shrinks mountain glaciers, seasonal water flow is disrupted — rivers first flood (from meltwater) then run dry in summer (no glacier left to melt). Glacier retreat is one of the clearest physical signals of climate change.

#### Mountain Ecosystems and Biodiversity
Mountains are among Earth's most biodiverse regions because their life zones create many distinct habitats in a small horizontal distance. The **tropical Andes** of South America are one of the most biodiverse regions on Earth — over 30,000 plant species in the Andean-Amazonian region. Mountain ranges also act as **barriers** that cause **speciation** — populations separated by mountains evolve independently. Many species found only on one side of a mountain range (or only above or below a certain altitude) are the result of this isolation.

#### Human Settlement and Mountain Hazards
Mountains provide resources — timber, minerals, water, agriculture (terrace farming), recreation — but also hazards. **Avalanches** kill ~250 people per year globally. **Landslides** are triggered by earthquakes, heavy rain, deforestation, and glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs — when glacial dams fail, releasing massive floods downstream). The **2015 Nepal earthquake** (M7.8) triggered avalanches on Everest that killed 22 at base camp and caused massive landslides that killed hundreds in villages. Human activity — deforestation, road building on steep slopes — dramatically increases landslide risk.
