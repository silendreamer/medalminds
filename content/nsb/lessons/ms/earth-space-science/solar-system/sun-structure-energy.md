#### The Sun's Structure
The Sun is a ball of hot plasma (ionized gas) held together by gravity. Its layers:
- **Core**: innermost region, ~15 million °C; where nuclear fusion occurs
- **Radiative zone**: energy moves outward as photons, bouncing around for ~100,000 years
- **Convective zone**: hot plasma rises, cools, and sinks in convection cells
- **Photosphere**: the visible surface, ~5,500°C; what we see as the Sun's disk
- **Chromosphere**: thin layer above the photosphere; temperature rises dramatically
- **Corona**: outermost atmosphere; ~1–3 million °C; visible during total solar eclipses

#### Nuclear Fusion
In the Sun's core, hydrogen atoms (protons) are fused together under extreme temperature and pressure to form helium. This process — **nuclear fusion** — releases an enormous amount of energy according to Einstein's equation E = mc². Every second, the Sun converts about 600 million tons of hydrogen into helium, releasing energy equivalent to 10 billion nuclear bombs. The Sun has been doing this for ~4.6 billion years and has enough hydrogen for another ~5 billion years.

#### Solar Output
The Sun emits energy as **electromagnetic radiation** across the spectrum — mostly visible light, infrared (heat), and ultraviolet. The amount of solar energy reaching Earth's surface per unit area is about **1,361 watts per square meter** (the solar constant). Earth intercepts only a tiny fraction of the Sun's total output, yet it powers virtually all life and drives weather, ocean circulation, and the water cycle.

#### Sunspots, Solar Flares, and CMEs
**Sunspots** are cooler, darker areas on the photosphere caused by concentrated magnetic fields. They appear in cycles of about 11 years (the solar cycle). During solar maximum, more sunspots and **solar flares** (bursts of radiation) occur. **Coronal mass ejections (CMEs)** are massive blasts of plasma that can reach Earth and interact with its magnetic field, causing **geomagnetic storms** — auroras (Northern/Southern Lights) at high latitudes and, in severe cases, power grid disruptions.

#### The Sun and Earth's Seasons
The Sun's energy doesn't reach all parts of Earth equally. Near the equator, sunlight strikes more directly; near the poles, it arrives at a low angle, spreading over more surface area and delivering less energy per unit area. This difference drives Earth's climate zones. Earth's **axial tilt** (23.5°) causes seasons: when the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun (summer), days are longer and sunlight more direct.
