## Subtopic: The Sun

### Our Star: Structure and Energy
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** sun-structure-energy
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** nuclear fusion · core · photosphere · chromosphere · corona · electromagnetic radiation
**Summary:** The Sun is a middle-aged star that generates energy through nuclear fusion in its core, radiating light and heat that power nearly all life on Earth.

#### The Sun's Structure
The Sun is a ball of hot plasma (ionized gas) held together by gravity. Its layers:
- **Core**: innermost region, ~15 million °C; where nuclear fusion occurs
- **Radiative zone**: energy moves outward as photons, bouncing around for ~100,000 years
- **Convective zone**: hot plasma rises, cools, and sinks in convection cells
- **Photosphere**: the visible surface, ~5,500°C; what we see as the Sun's disk
- **Chromosphere**: thin layer above the photosphere; temperature rises dramatically
- **Corona**: outermost atmosphere; ~1–3 million °C; visible during total solar eclipses

#### Nuclear Fusion
In the Sun's core, hydrogen atoms (protons) are fused together under extreme temperature and pressure to form helium. This process — **nuclear fusion** — releases an enormous amount of energy according to Einstein's equation E = mc². Every second, the Sun converts about 600 million tons of hydrogen into helium, releasing energy equivalent to 10 billion nuclear bombs. The Sun has been doing this for ~4.6 billion years and has enough hydrogen for another ~5 billion years.

#### Solar Output
The Sun emits energy as **electromagnetic radiation** across the spectrum — mostly visible light, infrared (heat), and ultraviolet. The amount of solar energy reaching Earth's surface per unit area is about **1,361 watts per square meter** (the solar constant). Earth intercepts only a tiny fraction of the Sun's total output, yet it powers virtually all life and drives weather, ocean circulation, and the water cycle.

#### Sunspots, Solar Flares, and CMEs
**Sunspots** are cooler, darker areas on the photosphere caused by concentrated magnetic fields. They appear in cycles of about 11 years (the solar cycle). During solar maximum, more sunspots and **solar flares** (bursts of radiation) occur. **Coronal mass ejections (CMEs)** are massive blasts of plasma that can reach Earth and interact with its magnetic field, causing **geomagnetic storms** — auroras (Northern/Southern Lights) at high latitudes and, in severe cases, power grid disruptions.

#### The Sun and Earth's Seasons
The Sun's energy doesn't reach all parts of Earth equally. Near the equator, sunlight strikes more directly; near the poles, it arrives at a low angle, spreading over more surface area and delivering less energy per unit area. This difference drives Earth's climate zones. Earth's **axial tilt** (23.5°) causes seasons: when the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun (summer), days are longer and sunlight more direct.

#### Review Questions
1. In what layer of the Sun does nuclear fusion occur?
2. What is the photosphere, and approximately how hot is it?
3. What causes sunspots, and on what cycle do they occur?

### Solar Wind and Earth's Magnetic Field
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** solar-wind-magnetic-field
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** solar wind · magnetosphere · auroras · Van Allen belts · geomagnetic storm
**Summary:** The Sun constantly streams charged particles (solar wind) into space; Earth's magnetic field deflects most of them, creating the magnetosphere.

#### The Solar Wind
The Sun continuously emits a stream of charged particles — mostly electrons and protons — called the **solar wind**. It travels at 400–800 km/s and blows outward throughout the solar system, filling the **heliosphere**. The solar wind shapes comet tails (always pointing away from the Sun) and strips planets without magnetic fields (like Mars) of their atmospheres over time.

#### The Magnetosphere
Earth's **magnetosphere** is the region around Earth dominated by its magnetic field. The magnetosphere deflects most solar wind, compressing it on the sunward side and stretching it into a long tail on the opposite side. Without the magnetosphere, the solar wind would strip away Earth's atmosphere — as happened to Mars. Earth's magnetic field is generated by the motion of liquid iron in the outer core (**dynamo effect**).

#### Auroras
When solar wind particles slip through the magnetosphere near the poles and collide with atmospheric gases, they excite the gas molecules, which release energy as light — the **aurora borealis** (Northern Lights) and **aurora australis** (Southern Lights). The colors depend on which gas is excited at what altitude: oxygen at high altitude → red; oxygen at lower altitude → green (most common); nitrogen → blue/purple.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the solar wind?
2. Why are auroras concentrated near the polar regions?
3. What would happen to Earth's atmosphere without the magnetosphere?

### The Sun's Energy and Life on Earth
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** sun-energy-life
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** solar energy · photosynthesis · food web · ultraviolet radiation · vitamin D
**Summary:** Nearly all energy in Earth's biosphere originates from the Sun, captured first by photosynthetic organisms.

#### The Sun as the Foundation of Food Webs
Photosynthetic organisms — plants, algae, cyanobacteria — capture solar energy and convert it to chemical energy (glucose) via photosynthesis. This energy flows through food webs: primary producers are eaten by primary consumers (herbivores), which are eaten by secondary and tertiary consumers (carnivores). Energy is lost (as heat) at each step, which is why food chains are typically limited to 4–5 links and why you need far more plant biomass than animal biomass in an ecosystem.

#### Solar Energy Technology
Humans now harness solar energy directly through **photovoltaic (PV) cells**, which convert sunlight to electricity using the photoelectric effect (Einstein won his Nobel Prize for explaining this). Solar thermal systems use sunlight to heat water. Solar energy is renewable, produces no emissions during operation, and costs have dropped by 90% since 2010. The challenge is storage — the sun doesn't shine at night.

#### Ultraviolet Radiation: Benefits and Risks
UV radiation from the Sun has both beneficial and harmful effects. UVB radiation enables skin cells to produce **vitamin D**, essential for calcium absorption and bone health. However, excessive UV exposure damages DNA, causing sunburn and increasing skin cancer risk. The ozone layer in the stratosphere absorbs most harmful UV before it reaches Earth's surface — one reason protecting the ozone layer is so important.

#### Review Questions
1. How does solar energy enter food webs?
2. Why are food chains typically limited to 4–5 links?
3. What are two opposite effects of ultraviolet radiation on human health?

## Subtopic: Planets, Moons & Their Properties

### The Eight Planets: An Overview
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** eight-planets-overview
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** terrestrial planets · gas giants · ice giants · orbital period · Kepler's laws
**Summary:** The solar system's eight planets divide into terrestrial (rocky, inner) and giant (outer) planets with dramatically different properties.

#### Terrestrial Planets (Inner)
The four inner planets — **Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars** — are small, dense, rocky, and have few or no moons. They formed from rocky materials close to the Sun. Key facts:
- **Mercury**: smallest planet; no significant atmosphere; extreme temperature swings (-180°C to 430°C); 88-day year
- **Venus**: hottest planet (462°C average) due to extreme greenhouse effect; rotates backward (retrograde); 243-day rotation (longer than its 225-day year)
- **Earth**: only known planet with life; liquid water; protective magnetic field
- **Mars**: red from iron oxide (rust); largest volcano (Olympus Mons, 21 km high); Valles Marineris canyon system; two tiny moons (Phobos, Deimos)

#### Gas Giants (Outer)
**Jupiter** and **Saturn** are massive planets composed mostly of hydrogen and helium, with no solid surface:
- **Jupiter**: largest planet (1,300 Earths by volume); Great Red Spot (storm larger than Earth, ongoing for 350+ years); 95 moons (including Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto — the Galilean moons)
- **Saturn**: least dense planet (would float on water); spectacular ring system of ice and rock; 146 moons; Titan has a thick nitrogen atmosphere and liquid methane lakes

#### Ice Giants (Outer)
**Uranus** and **Neptune** are smaller than gas giants and contain more ices (water, ammonia, methane):
- **Uranus**: rotates on its side (98° axial tilt); has rings; methane gives blue-green color
- **Neptune**: strongest winds in solar system (up to 2,100 km/h); Great Dark Spot (storm system); has Triton (moon that orbits backward)

#### Orbital Periods and Distance
Planets farther from the Sun take longer to complete an orbit (Kepler's Third Law). Earth = 1 year; Mars = 1.88 years; Jupiter = 11.86 years; Saturn = 29.46 years; Neptune = 164.8 years. The pattern: as distance from Sun doubles, period increases by more than double (period ∝ distance^1.5).

#### Review Questions
1. Name the four terrestrial planets in order from the Sun.
2. Why is Venus the hottest planet despite being farther from the Sun than Mercury?
3. What makes Jupiter's Great Red Spot notable?

### Moons of the Solar System
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** solar-system-moons
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** Galilean moons · Titan · Europa · Enceladus · tidal heating
**Summary:** Many planets have moons with fascinating properties — including potential habitats for life in their subsurface oceans.

#### Earth's Moon
Earth's Moon is unusually large relative to its host planet. It formed ~4.5 billion years ago from debris ejected when a Mars-sized body (Theia) collided with early Earth. The Moon is tidally locked — the same side always faces Earth. The lunar surface shows heavy cratering (the Moon has no plate tectonics or weathering to erase craters), with darker lowland areas called **maria** (ancient lava flows) and brighter highland regions.

#### The Galilean Moons of Jupiter
Discovered by Galileo in 1610, Jupiter's four largest moons are: **Io** (most volcanically active body in the solar system, due to tidal heating from Jupiter's gravity); **Europa** (smooth ice surface hiding a subsurface liquid water ocean — a prime candidate for extraterrestrial life); **Ganymede** (largest moon in the solar system, larger than Mercury); **Callisto** (heavily cratered, ancient surface).

#### Saturn's Moons
**Titan** is Saturn's largest moon — bigger than Mercury — with a dense nitrogen atmosphere and lakes of liquid methane and ethane. It's the only moon with a substantial atmosphere. **Enceladus** shoots water vapor and ice from its south pole through cracks in its icy surface, suggesting a subsurface ocean. The Cassini spacecraft flew through these plumes and detected organic molecules — making Enceladus another candidate for life.

#### Review Questions
1. Why is Io the most volcanically active body in the solar system?
2. What makes Europa a candidate for extraterrestrial life?
3. What makes Titan unusual compared to other moons?

### Dwarf Planets and the Outer Solar System
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** dwarf-planets-outer-solar-system
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** dwarf planet · Pluto · Kuiper Belt · Eris · IAU definition
**Summary:** Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt, home to Pluto and thousands of icy dwarf planets, expanding our understanding of the solar system's outer reaches.

#### What Is a Dwarf Planet?
In 2006, the International Astronomical Union (IAU) defined a **planet** as a body that (1) orbits the Sun, (2) has enough gravity to be roughly spherical, and (3) has **cleared its orbital neighborhood** of other debris. Pluto fails the third criterion — it shares its orbit in the Kuiper Belt with many other objects. So Pluto was reclassified as a **dwarf planet**. Currently recognized dwarf planets include Pluto, Eris, Haumea, Makemake, and Ceres (in the asteroid belt).

#### Pluto
Pluto was considered the ninth planet from 1930 to 2006. It orbits in the Kuiper Belt, is about 2/3 the size of Earth's Moon, and has five moons (largest: Charon, nearly half Pluto's size — sometimes called a double dwarf planet). NASA's New Horizons spacecraft flew by Pluto in 2015, revealing a heart-shaped plain of frozen nitrogen ice (Tombaugh Regio), mountains of water ice, and a thin atmosphere.

#### The Kuiper Belt and Oort Cloud
The **Kuiper Belt** (30–50 AU from the Sun) is a disk of icy bodies beyond Neptune — the source of short-period comets. It contains Pluto, Eris, and thousands of other objects. Beyond the Kuiper Belt (up to ~100,000 AU) lies the **Oort Cloud** — a spherical shell of icy bodies thought to be the source of long-period comets. The solar system's reach extends much farther than the eight planets suggest.

#### Review Questions
1. What three criteria does the IAU use to define a planet?
2. Why was Pluto reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006?
3. What is the Kuiper Belt, and what types of objects does it contain?

## Subtopic: Comets, Asteroids & Meteors

### Comets: Visitors from the Outer Solar System
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** comets-structure-origin
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** comet · nucleus · coma · ion tail · dust tail · Halley's comet · Oort Cloud
**Summary:** Comets are icy bodies from the outer solar system that develop glowing tails when their orbits bring them close to the Sun.

#### Comet Structure
A comet consists of: **nucleus** (the solid core — dirty snowball of ice, dust, and rocky material, typically 1–10 km across); **coma** (cloud of gas and dust released by the nucleus when heated by sunlight); **ion tail** (always points directly away from the Sun, blown by solar wind); **dust tail** (curves along the comet's orbit path). Comets can be spectacular — Hale-Bopp (1997) was visible in daylight; Halley's Comet returns every ~75–76 years (last visible 1986, next 2061).

#### Origins
Short-period comets (orbital period < 200 years, like Halley's Comet) originate in the **Kuiper Belt**. Long-period comets (like Hale-Bopp, period thousands of years) originate in the **Oort Cloud**, the distant spherical shell surrounding the solar system. A gravitational nudge sends them falling inward on long elliptical orbits.

#### Famous Missions
- **Rosetta/Philae (ESA, 2014)**: first spacecraft to orbit a comet (67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko) and land on its surface; found organic molecules
- **Deep Impact (NASA, 2005)**: deliberately impacted comet Tempel 1 to study its interior
- **Stardust (NASA)**: collected comet particles and returned them to Earth

#### Review Questions
1. What is the nucleus of a comet?
2. Why does a comet always point its ion tail away from the Sun?
3. What is the difference between a short-period and long-period comet?

### Asteroids and the Asteroid Belt
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** asteroids-asteroid-belt
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** asteroid · asteroid belt · near-Earth asteroid · Ceres · planetary defense
**Summary:** Asteroids are rocky remnants from the solar system's formation, concentrated in the belt between Mars and Jupiter.

#### What Are Asteroids?
**Asteroids** are rocky, metallic, or mixed bodies left over from the formation of the solar system ~4.6 billion years ago. They range from tiny pebbles to hundreds of kilometers across. Unlike comets, they're rocky rather than icy and don't develop tails. The largest asteroid, **Ceres** (~945 km diameter), was reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006.

#### The Asteroid Belt
Most asteroids orbit in the **asteroid belt** between Mars (1.5 AU) and Jupiter (5.2 AU). Despite dramatic movie depictions, the belt is mostly empty — if you flew through it, you'd be unlikely to hit anything. Jupiter's gravity stirred up material in this region, preventing a planet from forming there and scattering asteroids. Total mass of all asteroid belt objects combined is less than 4% of Earth's Moon.

#### Near-Earth Asteroids and Planetary Defense
**Near-Earth asteroids (NEAs)** have orbits that bring them within 1.3 AU of the Sun, potentially crossing Earth's path. About 2,000 NEAs larger than 1 km are known; larger ones could cause catastrophic damage if they impacted Earth. NASA's **DART (Double Asteroid Redirection Test)** mission (2022) successfully changed the orbit of the asteroid Dimorphos by crashing a spacecraft into it — proving planetary defense is feasible.

#### Review Questions
1. Where is the asteroid belt located in the solar system?
2. Why didn't a planet form in the asteroid belt?
3. What did the DART mission demonstrate?

### Meteors, Meteorites, and Impact Craters
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** meteors-meteorites-craters
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** meteor · meteoroid · meteorite · meteor shower · Chicxulub · impact crater
**Summary:** When rocky debris from space enters Earth's atmosphere, it creates meteors; surviving fragments that reach the ground are meteorites, and past large impacts have dramatically altered Earth's history.

#### Meteoroids, Meteors, and Meteorites
- **Meteoroid**: a rocky or metallic object in space, ranging from dust to ~1 meter (larger = asteroid)
- **Meteor**: the streak of light when a meteoroid enters Earth's atmosphere and burns up — also called a "shooting star"; air friction heats the meteoroid to incandescence
- **Meteorite**: a meteoroid that survives the trip through the atmosphere and lands on Earth's surface; classified as stony, iron, or stony-iron

#### Meteor Showers
**Meteor showers** occur when Earth passes through debris trails left by comets. Every year, Earth crosses these debris streams predictably, creating annual showers: Perseids (August, from Comet Swift-Tuttle), Leonids (November, from Comet Tempel-Tuttle), Geminids (December, from asteroid 3200 Phaethon). During a shower, dozens to hundreds of meteors per hour appear to radiate from a single point in the sky (the **radiant**).

#### Chicxulub and Mass Extinction
About 66 million years ago, an asteroid ~10 km across struck the Yucatán Peninsula in what is now Mexico — the **Chicxulub impact**. The impact released energy equivalent to billions of nuclear bombs, triggering wildfires, mega-tsunamis, and a "nuclear winter" from ejected dust blocking sunlight. The result was a mass extinction event that killed about 75% of all species, including the non-avian dinosaurs. This event marks the boundary between the Cretaceous and Paleogene periods (K-Pg boundary).

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between a meteor and a meteorite?
2. Why do meteor showers occur at predictable times each year?
3. What evidence supports the asteroid impact theory for the extinction of the dinosaurs?

## Subtopic: Tides, Seasons & Earth's Motions

### Earth's Rotation and Revolution
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** earth-rotation-revolution
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** rotation · revolution · axial tilt · day · year · time zones
**Summary:** Earth's rotation on its axis causes day and night; its revolution around the Sun takes one year; its 23.5° axial tilt causes seasons.

#### Rotation: Day and Night
Earth rotates on its axis once every 24 hours (one **solar day**). Rotation causes day and night: the side facing the Sun is in daylight; the side facing away is in night. Earth rotates from west to east — which is why the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west. Earth's rotation speed at the equator is about 1,670 km/h, but decreases toward the poles.

#### Revolution: The Year
Earth **revolves** around the Sun in an elliptical orbit approximately once every 365.25 days (one year — hence the leap year every four years to account for the extra 0.25 day). Earth's closest approach to the Sun is **perihelion** (about January 3 — during Northern Hemisphere winter!), and farthest is **aphelion** (about July 4). This contradicts the common misconception that seasons are caused by distance from the Sun.

#### Axial Tilt and Seasons
Earth's axis is tilted 23.5° relative to its orbital plane. **Seasons are caused by axial tilt**, not distance from the Sun. When the Northern Hemisphere tilts toward the Sun (June), it receives more direct sunlight and has longer days — summer. When tilted away (December), it receives less direct sunlight and shorter days — winter. The Southern Hemisphere has opposite seasons.

#### Review Questions
1. What causes day and night?
2. Is Earth closer to or farther from the Sun during Northern Hemisphere summer?
3. What causes Earth's seasons?

### Solstices, Equinoxes, and Lunar Phases
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** solstices-equinoxes-lunar-phases
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** solstice · equinox · lunar phase · new moon · full moon · synodic period
**Summary:** Earth's axial tilt creates solstices and equinoxes; the Moon's orbit around Earth creates the cycle of lunar phases.

#### Solstices
The **summer solstice** (around June 21 in Northern Hemisphere) is the longest day of the year — when the Sun is highest in the sky and the Northern Hemisphere is tilted most directly toward it. The **winter solstice** (around December 21) is the shortest day. At the solstices, the Sun reaches its most extreme north or south position in the sky.

#### Equinoxes
The **vernal (spring) equinox** (around March 20) and **autumnal equinox** (around September 22) occur when Earth's axis is perpendicular to the Sun's rays — day and night are approximately equal (12 hours each) everywhere on Earth. The word "equinox" means "equal night."

#### Lunar Phases
The Moon orbits Earth approximately every 29.5 days (the **synodic period** — new moon to new moon). Phases: **new moon** (Moon between Earth and Sun, not visible); **waxing crescent** → **first quarter** → **waxing gibbous** → **full moon** (Moon opposite Sun, fully lit) → **waning gibbous** → **third quarter** → **waning crescent** → back to new moon. Phases result from changing angles between Moon, Earth, and Sun — not from Earth's shadow.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the summer solstice, and when does it occur in the Northern Hemisphere?
2. What causes lunar phases?
3. How many days does one complete cycle of lunar phases take?
