## Subtopic: The Water Cycle

### The Water Cycle: Earth's Continuous Loop
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** water-cycle-overview
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** evaporation · condensation · precipitation · runoff · infiltration · transpiration
**Summary:** The water cycle describes the continuous movement of water through Earth's atmosphere, land surface, and underground reservoirs.

#### The Major Processes
The **water cycle** (also called the hydrologic cycle) has no true beginning or end. Key processes:
- **Evaporation**: liquid water converts to water vapor when heated by the sun; most occurs from oceans (about 86% of all evaporation)
- **Transpiration**: plants release water vapor through leaf pores (stomata); combined with evaporation, this is called **evapotranspiration**
- **Condensation**: water vapor cools and converts back to liquid, forming clouds and fog
- **Precipitation**: water falls as rain, snow, sleet, or hail
- **Runoff**: water flows over land into streams and rivers
- **Infiltration**: water soaks into the soil, recharging groundwater

#### The Role of the Sun and Gravity
The water cycle is powered by two forces: **solar energy** drives evaporation and atmospheric circulation; **gravity** pulls precipitation down and drives runoff and groundwater flow. Without solar energy, water wouldn't evaporate; without gravity, it wouldn't fall back down.

#### Water Storage Reservoirs
Water is temporarily stored in **reservoirs**: oceans (97.5% of all water; mostly salt), glaciers and ice sheets (68.9% of fresh water), groundwater (30.8% of fresh water), lakes and rivers (0.3% of fresh water), and the atmosphere (0.04% of fresh water). Despite being the smallest reservoir, atmospheric water is the most actively cycling — average water vapor stays in the atmosphere for only about 9 days before precipitating.

#### Why the Water Cycle Matters
The water cycle distributes fresh water across Earth, regulates temperature (evaporation cools surfaces; condensation releases heat), erodes and shapes landforms, and transports nutrients and sediments. Disruptions — from drought, deforestation, or climate change — affect water availability for billions of people.

#### Residence Time
Different reservoirs hold water for very different amounts of time: the atmosphere ~9 days, rivers ~2–6 months, soil moisture ~1–2 months, groundwater ~months to thousands of years, glaciers ~20,000–100,000 years, and deep oceans up to 3,000 years. This concept of **residence time** helps explain why some water resources can be depleted much faster than they're replenished.

#### Review Questions
1. What two forces power the water cycle?
2. What percentage of Earth's water is found in the oceans, and is it fresh or salt water?
3. What is the difference between evaporation and transpiration?

### Groundwater: The Hidden Resource
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** groundwater-aquifers
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** aquifer · water table · recharge · artesian well · groundwater depletion
**Summary:** Groundwater fills the spaces in underground rock and sediment, forming aquifers that supply drinking water to billions of people.

#### How Groundwater Forms
When precipitation falls, some evaporates, some runs off, and some infiltrates the soil. Water percolates downward through soil and rock until it reaches the **zone of saturation**, where all pore spaces are filled with water. The top of this zone is the **water table**. Above the water table is the **zone of aeration** (vadose zone), where pore spaces contain both air and water.

#### Aquifers
An **aquifer** is an underground rock layer that stores and transmits significant amounts of water. **Unconfined aquifers** have a water table that rises and falls with recharge. **Confined aquifers** are sandwiched between impermeable layers (aquitards); water in them is under pressure. When you drill into a confined aquifer, water rises on its own — this is an **artesian well**. The Ogallala Aquifer beneath the Great Plains is one of the world's largest and supplies irrigation water for a significant portion of U.S. crops.

#### Groundwater Depletion
When water is pumped from an aquifer faster than it's recharged (refilled by infiltrating precipitation), the water table drops — **groundwater depletion**. This can cause land to sink (**subsidence**), dry up wells, and reduce streamflow. In parts of California's Central Valley, land has sunk by more than 8 meters due to groundwater pumping. The Ogallala Aquifer is being depleted far faster than it recharges (recharge rate: about 1 cm/year; pumping rate: ~0.5 m/year in some areas).

#### Review Questions
1. What is an aquifer, and how does water get into it?
2. What is the water table?
3. What problems result from pumping an aquifer faster than it recharges?

### The Water Cycle and Human Impact
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** water-cycle-human-impact
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** deforestation · urbanization · dam · irrigation · water scarcity
**Summary:** Human activities alter the water cycle by changing how water moves across landscapes, with consequences for water availability and quality.

#### Deforestation and the Water Cycle
Forests play a critical role in the water cycle through transpiration and by holding soil in place. When forests are cleared, infiltration decreases (compacted soil doesn't absorb water well), runoff increases (causing floods and erosion), and transpiration drops (less water vapor returns to the atmosphere). Deforestation of the Amazon basin is measurably reducing regional rainfall in a feedback loop — less forest → less transpiration → less rain → less forest.

#### Urbanization
Paved surfaces (roads, parking lots, buildings) replace permeable soil with **impervious surfaces**. When rain falls on pavement, it runs off almost immediately instead of infiltrating. This causes urban flooding, reduces groundwater recharge, and increases the speed and volume of water entering streams — exacerbating downstream flooding. Cities use **storm drains** and increasingly **green infrastructure** (rain gardens, permeable pavement) to manage urban runoff.

#### Dams and Irrigation
Dams store water in reservoirs for drinking, irrigation, and electricity generation. But they also trap sediment (starving deltas and beaches downstream), block fish migration, and evaporate significant water from reservoir surfaces. Irrigation accounts for about 70% of global fresh water withdrawal. **Drip irrigation** is far more efficient than flood irrigation — delivering water directly to plant roots rather than spraying it over fields.

#### Review Questions
1. How does deforestation affect the water cycle?
2. Why does urbanization increase flooding risk?
3. Why is drip irrigation more water-efficient than flood irrigation?

### Water Availability and the Global Crisis
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** global-water-scarcity
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** fresh water · water stress · desalination · virtual water · water rights
**Summary:** While Earth has abundant water, fresh water suitable for human use is limited and unevenly distributed, creating water scarcity for billions.

#### How Much Fresh Water Is There?
Of all Earth's water, 97.5% is salt water in the oceans. Of the 2.5% that's fresh, about 68.9% is locked in glaciers and ice sheets, 30.8% is groundwater, and less than 0.3% is in surface water (lakes, rivers, swamps). This means less than 1% of all Earth's water is readily accessible fresh water. And that small fraction is unevenly distributed — some regions receive abundant rainfall; others are chronically arid.

#### Water Stress
A region experiences **water stress** when demand for water exceeds available supply. About 2 billion people currently live in areas with high water stress. By 2050, over 5 billion people may face water shortages. Water stress causes competition between agriculture (the largest user), industry, and household needs, and can trigger geopolitical conflict.

#### Solutions and Technologies
- **Desalination**: removing salt from seawater using reverse osmosis or distillation; Saudi Arabia, Israel, and Southern California use it extensively — but it's energy-intensive
- **Water recycling**: treating and reusing wastewater for irrigation or even drinking (Singapore's NEWater program)
- **Conservation**: drip irrigation, low-flow fixtures, leak repair can dramatically reduce demand
- **Virtual water**: recognizing the water embedded in food production (1 kg of beef requires ~15,000 liters of water to produce) and importing water-intensive goods from water-rich regions

#### Review Questions
1. What percentage of Earth's water is readily accessible fresh water?
2. What is water stress, and approximately how many people currently live under high water stress?
3. What is desalination, and what is its main drawback?

### Water Cycle Review: Key Concepts
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** water-cycle-review
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** evaporation · condensation · precipitation · infiltration · runoff · groundwater
**Summary:** Connecting all parts of the water cycle and testing understanding across processes and reservoirs.

#### Process Identification
Match each description to its process:
- Sun heats ocean surface → water vapor enters atmosphere: **evaporation**
- Water vapor cools around dust particles → cloud forms: **condensation**
- Rain falls from cloud: **precipitation**
- Rain soaks into soil and percolates to water table: **infiltration**
- Water flows downhill over land into river: **runoff**
- Plant releases water vapor through leaves: **transpiration**

#### Reservoir Comparisons
Ocean water = salty (not directly drinkable) but largest reservoir. Glaciers = largest fresh water store, but frozen and difficult to access. Groundwater = second-largest fresh water store, accessible by wells but being depleted in many areas. Atmosphere = smallest but most actively cycling reservoir — water spends only ~9 days there on average before precipitating.

#### Human Influence on the Cycle
Key human interventions and their effects:
- Deforestation → less transpiration, more runoff, less infiltration
- Urbanization → more impervious surface, more runoff, less recharge
- Agriculture → largest fresh water user; irrigation draws heavily on rivers and groundwater
- Dams → control floods and store water, but alter downstream hydrology

## Subtopic: Oceans: Properties & Tides

### Ocean Properties: Temperature, Salinity, and Density
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ocean-properties
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** salinity · thermocline · halocline · density · ocean layers
**Summary:** Ocean water varies in temperature, salinity, and density with depth, creating layered structure that drives circulation.

#### Salinity
**Salinity** is the amount of dissolved salts in seawater, typically expressed in parts per thousand (ppt) or practical salinity units (PSU). Average ocean salinity is about 35 ppt — meaning 35 grams of salt per kilogram of seawater. The most abundant dissolved salt is sodium chloride (NaCl). Salinity varies by region: higher in subtropical regions (high evaporation, low rainfall), lower near rivers and polar regions (fresh water input). The Dead Sea has salinity of ~340 ppt — nearly 10x average ocean salinity.

#### Temperature Zones
Ocean temperature decreases with depth. The **surface layer** (top ~200 m) is warm and well-mixed by wind. Below this, the **thermocline** is a zone of rapid temperature decrease. In the **deep zone** (below ~1,000 m), temperatures hover near 2–4°C. In polar regions, the thermocline is weak or absent — water is cold all the way to the surface.

#### Density and Layering
Cold, salty water is denser than warm, fresh water and sinks. This creates **stratification** — lighter water on top, denser water below. Where density changes sharply with depth is called the **pycnocline**. Stratification limits mixing between layers, affecting nutrient distribution (nutrients often trapped in cold deep water where there's no sunlight for photosynthesis — upwelling is needed to bring them up).

#### Review Questions
1. What is the average salinity of ocean water?
2. What is a thermocline?
3. Why is cold, salty water denser than warm, fresh water?

### Tides: The Pull of the Moon
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** tides-lunar-gravity
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** tidal bulge · spring tide · neap tide · tidal range · intertidal zone
**Summary:** Tides are the periodic rise and fall of sea level caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon and, to a lesser extent, the Sun.

#### How Tides Form
The Moon's gravity pulls on Earth, creating a **tidal bulge** of water on the side facing the Moon. Simultaneously, a second bulge forms on the opposite side of Earth due to inertia (Earth and Moon orbit their common center of gravity). As Earth rotates through these two bulges, most coastal locations experience two high tides and two low tides per day (**semidiurnal tides**). Some locations experience one high and one low per day (**diurnal tides**).

#### Spring and Neap Tides
When the Moon, Earth, and Sun are aligned (during new and full moon), their gravitational pulls combine to create **spring tides** — higher high tides and lower low tides. When the Moon and Sun are at right angles to Earth (during quarter moons), their pulls partially cancel, creating **neap tides** — smaller tidal range. Spring tides occur about every two weeks.

#### Tidal Ranges
The difference between high and low tide varies dramatically by location. The Bay of Fundy in Canada has the world's largest tidal range — up to 16 meters — due to the resonance of water sloshing in the bay's particular shape and size. The Gulf of Mexico has a very small tidal range of less than 1 meter because of its enclosed basin geometry.

#### The Intertidal Zone
The **intertidal zone** is the coastal area between high and low tide marks. Organisms there must survive exposure to air and sun at low tide, and wave action and submersion at high tide. It's divided into zones based on exposure: the **spray zone** (always above water), **high intertidal**, **middle intertidal**, and **low intertidal**. Species like barnacles, mussels, sea stars, and limpets are specially adapted to this dynamic environment.

#### Review Questions
1. What causes the tidal bulge on the side of Earth facing the Moon?
2. What is a spring tide, and when does it occur?
3. Why do coastal locations typically experience two high tides per day?

### Ocean Zones and Marine Life
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ocean-zones-life
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** photic zone · aphotic zone · pelagic · benthic · hydrothermal vent
**Summary:** The ocean is divided into zones based on depth and light availability, each hosting distinct communities of organisms.

#### Zones by Light
The **photic zone** (surface to ~200 m) receives enough sunlight for photosynthesis. Nearly all marine primary production (by phytoplankton) occurs here. The **aphotic zone** (below 200 m) receives no sunlight — organisms here depend on sinking organic matter ("marine snow") from above or chemosynthesis.

#### Zones by Depth (Pelagic)
- **Epipelagic** (0–200 m): sunlit; most sea life
- **Mesopelagic** (200–1,000 m): twilight zone; bioluminescence common
- **Bathypelagic** (1,000–4,000 m): no light; extreme pressure
- **Abyssopelagic** (4,000–6,000 m): near-freezing, crushing pressure
- **Hadalpelagic** (below 6,000 m): trenches; the deepest point is the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench at ~11,000 m

#### Hydrothermal Vents
Discovered in 1977, **hydrothermal vents** are cracks in the seafloor where superheated, mineral-rich water erupts. Vent ecosystems are powered by **chemosynthesis** (bacteria use chemical energy from sulfur compounds) rather than sunlight — proving that life doesn't require solar energy. Vent communities include giant tube worms (up to 2 m long), clams, crabs, and shrimp.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the photic zone, and why is it biologically important?
2. At what depth does the mesopelagic zone begin?
3. What energy source supports life at hydrothermal vents?

## Subtopic: Rivers, Lakes & Groundwater

### Rivers: From Source to Sea
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** rivers-drainage-systems
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** watershed · tributary · drainage basin · erosion · deposition · delta
**Summary:** Rivers form drainage networks that carry water and sediment from highlands to the ocean, shaping the landscape as they go.

#### Watersheds and Drainage Basins
A **watershed** (or drainage basin) is the land area that drains into a particular river or body of water. All precipitation that falls within a watershed eventually flows to the same outlet. Watersheds are separated by **divides** — ridges where water flows in different directions on each side. The Continental Divide in North America separates water flowing to the Pacific from water flowing to the Atlantic/Gulf.

#### Tributaries and River Systems
Rivers grow by collecting water from smaller streams called **tributaries**. The Missouri River is the longest tributary of the Mississippi. Together, the Mississippi-Missouri river system drains 41% of the contiguous United States — one of the largest drainage basins in the world.

#### Erosion, Transport, and Deposition
Rivers erode material from their banks and beds, carry it as **sediment load**, and deposit it downstream. Faster water carries more and larger sediment; slower water drops its load. Where a river slows dramatically — typically where it meets the ocean — it deposits sediment in a **delta** (Mississippi River Delta, Nile Delta). **Meanders** form when rivers swing back and forth across their floodplain; eventually a meander can be cut off, forming an **oxbow lake**.

#### Review Questions
1. What is a watershed?
2. What happens to sediment when a river slows down?
3. How does an oxbow lake form?

### Lakes: Freshwater Ecosystems
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** lakes-freshwater-ecosystems
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** lake zones · eutrophication · thermal stratification · turnover · oligotrophic
**Summary:** Lakes are freshwater bodies with distinct vertical zones and a lifecycle that includes natural aging through nutrient accumulation.

#### Lake Zones
Lakes are divided into zones:
- **Littoral zone**: shallow nearshore area; aquatic plants grow here
- **Limnetic zone**: open, sunlit water away from shore; where phytoplankton thrive
- **Profundal zone**: deep water below the light zone; low oxygen, high decomposition
- **Benthic zone**: bottom sediments; detritivores (decomposers) dominate

#### Thermal Stratification and Turnover
In summer, lake surface water warms and becomes less dense than cold bottom water, creating **stratification**: a warm **epilimnion** above a temperature gradient called the **thermocline**, above a cold **hypolimnion**. In fall, surface water cools and sinks — triggering **turnover**, which mixes nutrients from bottom to top and oxygen from top to bottom. This biannual mixing is critical for lake health.

#### Eutrophication
**Eutrophication** is excessive nutrient enrichment of a water body — usually from agricultural fertilizer runoff (nitrogen and phosphorus). Excess nutrients cause explosive algae growth (**algal blooms**). When algae die and decompose, bacteria consume oxygen, creating **hypoxic (dead) zones** where most aquatic animals cannot survive. The Gulf of Mexico "dead zone" — fed by Mississippi River agricultural runoff — can exceed 20,000 km² in summer.

#### Review Questions
1. Name the four major zones of a lake.
2. What triggers lake turnover in autumn?
3. What is eutrophication, and what causes it?

## Subtopic: Ice, Glaciers & Polar Ice

### Glaciers: Rivers of Ice
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** glaciers-ice-formation
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** glacier · accumulation zone · ablation zone · glacial erosion · moraine · ice shelf
**Summary:** Glaciers form where snowfall exceeds melting, slowly flowing downhill and dramatically reshaping landscapes.

#### How Glaciers Form and Move
A glacier forms when snow accumulates faster than it melts over many years. As layers pile up, pressure compresses lower snow into granular **firn**, then into dense glacial ice. Once thick enough, glaciers flow under their own weight — both by **internal deformation** (ice crystals deforming) and **basal sliding** (meltwater at the base lubricates movement). Valley glaciers flow down mountain valleys; ice sheets spread outward in all directions.

#### Accumulation and Ablation
The **accumulation zone** is the upper glacier where snowfall exceeds melting — the glacier gains mass. The **ablation zone** is the lower glacier where melting, calving, and evaporation exceed snowfall — the glacier loses mass. The **equilibrium line** separates the two. A glacier advances when accumulation exceeds ablation; retreats when the reverse is true. Most of the world's glaciers are currently retreating due to rising temperatures.

#### Glacial Erosion and Deposition
Glaciers are powerful erosional agents. They carve **U-shaped valleys** (compared to the V-shape carved by rivers), create **cirques** (bowl-shaped hollows at glacier heads), and produce **fjords** (drowned glacial valleys). Rock debris carried by glaciers is deposited as **moraines**: lateral moraines form along the sides, medial moraines where two glaciers merge, and terminal moraines mark the glacier's farthest advance.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between the accumulation and ablation zones of a glacier?
2. What shape of valley does a glacier carve, compared to a river?
3. What is a moraine?

### Polar Ice and Sea Level
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** polar-ice-sea-level
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** ice sheet · sea ice · Greenland · Antarctica · sea level rise
**Summary:** The Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets contain enough ice to raise global sea levels dramatically if melted, making their stability a critical climate concern.

#### Ice Sheets vs. Sea Ice
**Ice sheets** are continent-scale glaciers sitting on land: Greenland (1.7 million km²) and Antarctica (14 million km²). Melt from these ice sheets raises sea level. **Sea ice** is frozen ocean water — it floats, so when it melts, sea level doesn't rise (like ice cubes melting in a glass). However, sea ice loss has major effects on climate: the Arctic Ocean's dark water absorbs more solar energy than white ice (lower **albedo**), amplifying warming.

#### How Much Would Sea Level Rise?
If the Greenland Ice Sheet melted entirely, sea level would rise about **7 meters**. Complete melting of the Antarctic Ice Sheet would add about **60 meters**. Full melting is unlikely in the near term, but even partial melting is significant: the IPCC projects global sea level rise of 0.3–1.0 m by 2100, with higher estimates possible. This would inundate coastal cities worldwide, including Miami, Mumbai, Dhaka, and Shanghai.

#### Observed Changes
The Arctic sea ice minimum extent has declined by about 13% per decade since satellite records began in 1979. Greenland is losing ice at an accelerating rate — currently about 280 billion tons per year. The West Antarctic Ice Sheet contains marine ice (resting below sea level) and is considered potentially unstable; the Thwaites Glacier ("Doomsday Glacier") alone could raise sea level by 65 cm if destabilized.

#### Review Questions
1. Why does melting sea ice NOT raise sea level, while melting land ice does?
2. How much would sea level rise if the Greenland Ice Sheet melted completely?
3. What is the significance of the Thwaites Glacier?
