#### Key Facts
- Star spectral classes (hot to cool): **O, B, A, F, G, K, M**
- Sun's spectral class: **G** (yellow dwarf)
- Nearest star (excluding Sun): **Proxima Centauri**, 4.24 light-years
- Brightest star in night sky: **Sirius** (Alpha Canis Majoris)
- Our Sun's fate: **red giant → planetary nebula → white dwarf**
- Massive star fate (>8 M☉): **supernova → neutron star or black hole**
- Coldest/reddest stars: **M-type (red dwarfs)** — most common in galaxy
- Hottest/brightest: **O-type** — rarest
- H-R diagram axes: **temperature (x) vs. luminosity (y)**

#### Toss-Up Stems
- "Name the nearest star to our Solar System, approximately 4.24 light-years away..." → **Proxima Centauri**
- "This type of star, with a period related to its luminosity, is used as a standard candle for measuring cosmic distances..." → **Cepheid variable**
- "A star like the Sun will eventually expand into this type of star before shedding its outer layers..." → **red giant**
- "For 10 points — what is the final stage of a Sun-like star after shedding its outer layers?" → **white dwarf**
- "On the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram, what do stars on the main sequence have in common?" → they are fusing hydrogen in their cores

#### Common Traps
- Red giants are COOL and large; white dwarfs are HOT and small
- The Sun is a G-type star — neither the hottest (O, B) nor the coolest (M)
- Neutron stars are incredibly dense but still have mass; black holes have gravity so strong light cannot escape

#### Review Questions
1. What spectral class is our Sun, and what color is it?
2. What is the final evolutionary stage of a massive star (>8 solar masses)?
3. On the H-R diagram, what does position on the main sequence tell you about a star?
