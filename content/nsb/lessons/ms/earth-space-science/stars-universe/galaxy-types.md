#### Galaxy Classification
Edwin Hubble developed the **tuning fork diagram** to classify galaxies:
- **Spiral galaxies** (like the Milky Way and Andromeda): flat disk with central bulge and spiral arms; contain older stars in bulge and young stars/gas in arms; actively forming new stars
- **Barred spiral galaxies**: spiral galaxies with a bar of stars across the center (the Milky Way is barred)
- **Elliptical galaxies**: spherical to elongated; contain mostly older stars; little gas and dust; minimal star formation; range from dwarf ellipticals to giant ellipticals (the largest galaxies)
- **Irregular galaxies**: no regular shape; often result from galactic collisions; the Magellanic Clouds are irregular

#### Galaxy Clusters and Superclusters
Galaxies are not uniformly distributed — they gather in **groups** (like the Local Group), **clusters** (hundreds to thousands of galaxies bound by gravity), and **superclusters** (the largest known structures). The Virgo Cluster (~1,500 galaxies) is the nearest large cluster. On the largest scales, galaxies form vast filaments and walls surrounding enormous voids — the **cosmic web**.

#### Active Galaxies and Quasars
Some galaxies have **active galactic nuclei (AGN)** — supermassive black holes actively consuming gas and radiating enormous energy. **Quasars** (quasi-stellar objects) are the most luminous AGN, visible across billions of light-years. They were common in the early universe when black holes had more material to consume. Quasar 3C 273 was the first identified (1963).
