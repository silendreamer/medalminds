### Introduction to Inequalities

Inequalities are mathematical expressions that show the relationship between two values when they are not equal. They use specific symbols to indicate whether one value is less than, greater than, less than or equal to, or greater than or equal to another value. The four primary inequality symbols are: `<` (less than), `>` (greater than), `≤` (less than or equal to), and `≥` (greater than or equal to). Understanding these symbols is crucial for solving and graphing inequalities.

### Solving Linear Inequalities

Solving linear inequalities is similar to solving linear equations, but there are key differences, especially when multiplying or dividing by a negative number. When you multiply or divide both sides of an inequality by a negative value, you must reverse the inequality symbol. For example, if you have `-2x > 6`, dividing both sides by -2 changes the inequality to `x < -3`. This reversal is a critical step that students must remember when solving inequalities.

### Graphing Inequalities on a Number Line

Graphing inequalities involves representing the solutions on a number line. For example, to graph `x > 2`, you would draw an open circle at 2 (indicating that 2 is not included) and shade to the right to show all numbers greater than 2. Conversely, for `x ≤ 3`, you would draw a closed circle at 3 (indicating that 3 is included) and shade to the left. This visual representation helps students understand the range of possible solutions.

### Compound Inequalities

Compound inequalities combine two inequalities into one statement, often using the words "and" or "or." For example, the compound inequality `1 < x < 5` means that `x` is greater than 1 and less than 5 simultaneously. Graphically, this would be represented by shading the interval between 1 and 5 on a number line. Understanding compound inequalities is essential for solving problems that involve multiple conditions.

### Interval Notation

Interval notation is a concise way to represent the set of solutions to an inequality. For instance, the inequality `x > 3` can be expressed in interval notation as `(3, ∞)`, indicating that 3 is not included, and the solutions extend infinitely to the right. Closed intervals, such as `[2, 5]`, indicate that the endpoints are included in the solution set. Familiarity with interval notation is important for accurately communicating the results of inequalities.

### Applications of Inequalities

Inequalities are widely used in real-world applications, such as in finance, science, and engineering. For example, if a budget must not exceed $500, this can be expressed as an inequality: `x ≤ 500`, where `x` represents the total expenses. Understanding how to set up and solve inequalities allows students to model and solve practical problems effectively. This skill is not only useful in academics but also in everyday decision-making.

### Review Questions
