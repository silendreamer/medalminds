#### What a Ratio Tells You
A ratio compares two quantities of the same or different types. "3 red to 5 blue" is a part-to-part ratio: red:blue = 3:5. But the ratio of red to total = 3:(3+5) = 3:8 — a part-to-whole ratio. These are completely different numbers, and confusing them is the #1 error in ratio problems. Always ask: "Is this comparing part to part, or part to whole?"

#### Writing Ratios Three Ways
A ratio of 4 to 7 can be written as 4:7, 4/7, or "4 to 7." In Science Bowl, all three notations appear. The fraction form is most useful for computation. Ratios can always be simplified by dividing both terms by their GCF: 12:18 = 2:3 (GCF = 6). Unlike fractions, ratios don't have to be less than 1.

#### Scaling Ratios
A recipe uses flour and sugar in ratio 5:2. To make a version using 15 cups of flour, scale both: multiply both terms by 3 → 15:6. So you need 6 cups of sugar. The key insight: ratios scale by the same factor on both sides. Always find the scale factor first: 15 ÷ 5 = 3. Then apply: 2 × 3 = 6.

#### Ratios with Three or More Quantities
If the ratio of A:B:C = 2:3:5, and the total is 100, then: A = 2/10 × 100 = 20; B = 3/10 × 100 = 30; C = 5/10 × 100 = 50. The sum of ratio parts (2+3+5 = 10) becomes the denominator. This technique handles any multi-quantity ratio problem.

#### Units in Ratios
Ratios comparing the same units simplify to pure numbers (no units remain): 6 feet : 9 feet = 2:3. Ratios comparing different units keep their units and become rates: 60 miles per 2 hours = 60 miles : 2 hours = 30 miles/hour. This distinction defines where ratio ends and rate begins.

#### Review Questions
1. A bag has 4 green, 6 red, and 2 blue marbles. Write the ratio of green to total in lowest terms.
2. The ratio of boys to girls in a class is 3:4, and there are 28 girls. How many boys are there?
3. In a ratio A:B:C = 1:4:5, if B = 24, what is C?
