## Subtopic: Angles, Lines & Polygons

### What Is an Angle? Measuring and Naming Angles
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-math-angles-naming
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** angle · degree · vertex · protractor · acute · obtuse · right · straight
**Summary:** Learn how angles are formed, named, and classified by their measure.

#### What Is an Angle?
An angle is formed when two rays share the same endpoint, called the **vertex**. We measure angles in **degrees** (°). A full rotation is 360°. Angles are named by their vertex or by three points (e.g., ∠ABC, where B is the vertex). You use a **protractor** to measure an angle's degree. Angles fall into four types: **acute** (less than 90°), **right** (exactly 90°, shown with a small square), **obtuse** (between 90° and 180°), and **straight** (exactly 180°, forming a line).

#### Angle Relationships
Pairs of angles have special names based on how they relate. **Complementary** angles sum to 90°. **Supplementary** angles sum to 180°. **Vertical** angles are across from each other when two lines cross — they are always equal. **Adjacent** angles share a vertex and a side but don't overlap. Knowing these relationships lets you solve for unknown angles without a protractor.

#### Angles in Parallel Lines
When a **transversal** crosses two parallel lines, it creates 8 angles. **Corresponding** angles (same position at each intersection) are equal. **Alternate interior** angles (between the lines, on opposite sides) are equal. **Co-interior** (same-side interior) angles are supplementary, summing to 180°. These rules are essential for geometry proofs and problems.

#### Lines and Their Relationships
**Parallel** lines never intersect and are always the same distance apart (symbol: ∥). **Perpendicular** lines intersect at exactly 90° (symbol: ⊥). **Skew** lines don't intersect but also aren't parallel (they exist in 3D space). A **transversal** is a line that crosses two or more other lines. Identifying line relationships is the first step in many geometry problems.

#### Sum of Interior Angles in Polygons
The interior angles of any triangle sum to **180°**. For any polygon with n sides, the interior angle sum is **(n − 2) × 180°**. So a quadrilateral sums to 360°, a pentagon to 540°, a hexagon to 720°. For a **regular polygon** (all sides and angles equal), each interior angle equals (n − 2) × 180° ÷ n. This formula is one of the most tested geometry facts in Science Bowl.

#### Review Questions
1. Two angles are supplementary. One angle measures 63°. What is the measure of the other angle?
2. What is the sum of interior angles of an octagon (8 sides)?
3. If two parallel lines are cut by a transversal and one alternate interior angle is 47°, what is the other alternate interior angle?

### Triangles: Types, Properties, and the Pythagorean Theorem
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-math-triangles-pythagorean
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** triangle · equilateral · isosceles · scalene · Pythagorean theorem · hypotenuse · right triangle
**Summary:** Explore triangle classifications and the most famous theorem in geometry.

#### Classifying Triangles
Triangles are classified two ways: by **sides** and by **angles**. By sides: **equilateral** (all three sides equal, all angles 60°), **isosceles** (two sides equal, two base angles equal), **scalene** (no sides equal, no angles equal). By angles: **acute** (all angles < 90°), **right** (one angle = 90°), **obtuse** (one angle > 90°). Note: a triangle can be both isosceles and right, or equilateral and acute — these categories combine.

#### The Pythagorean Theorem
In a **right triangle**, the two shorter sides are called **legs** (a and b) and the longest side (opposite the right angle) is the **hypotenuse** (c). The relationship is: **a² + b² = c²**. This only works for right triangles. Example: if a = 3 and b = 4, then c² = 9 + 16 = 25, so c = 5. The triple (3, 4, 5) is a **Pythagorean triple** — whole numbers that satisfy the theorem. Others: (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17).

#### Pythagorean Triples and the Converse
Memorizing common Pythagorean triples speeds up competition math significantly. Multiples work too: (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), (10, 24, 26). The **converse** of the theorem states: if a² + b² = c², then the triangle IS a right triangle. If a² + b² > c², it's acute. If a² + b² < c², it's obtuse. This lets you classify a triangle given only its side lengths.

#### Triangle Inequality Theorem
Not any three lengths can form a triangle. The **Triangle Inequality Theorem** says the sum of any two sides must be greater than the third side. For sides a, b, c: a + b > c, a + c > b, and b + c > a. Example: sides of 3, 4, and 8 cannot form a triangle because 3 + 4 = 7 < 8. This theorem appears in both competition math and real-world applications.

#### Triangle Area and the Exterior Angle Theorem
The area of any triangle is **A = ½ × base × height**, where height is perpendicular to the base. The **Exterior Angle Theorem** states that an exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two non-adjacent interior angles. For example, if two interior angles are 50° and 70°, the exterior angle at the third vertex is 120°. This is a quick shortcut for many angle-finding problems.

#### Review Questions
1. A right triangle has legs of length 5 and 12. What is the length of the hypotenuse?
2. Can a triangle have sides of length 4, 7, and 12?
3. Two interior angles of a triangle are 38° and 75°. What is the third interior angle?

### Quadrilaterals and Other Polygons
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-math-quadrilaterals-polygons
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** quadrilateral · parallelogram · rectangle · rhombus · square · trapezoid · regular polygon
**Summary:** Understand the hierarchy of quadrilaterals and properties of common polygons.

#### The Quadrilateral Family
A **quadrilateral** is any 4-sided polygon with an interior angle sum of 360°. The key types form a hierarchy: a **parallelogram** has two pairs of parallel sides and opposite angles equal. A **rectangle** is a parallelogram with four right angles. A **rhombus** is a parallelogram with four equal sides. A **square** is both a rectangle and a rhombus. A **trapezoid** has exactly one pair of parallel sides. Understanding this hierarchy (every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square) is commonly tested.

#### Properties of Parallelograms
In any **parallelogram**: opposite sides are equal in length, opposite angles are equal, consecutive angles are supplementary (sum to 180°), and the diagonals **bisect** each other (cut each other in half). These properties hold for rectangles, rhombuses, and squares as well. In a **rectangle**, diagonals are equal in length. In a **rhombus**, diagonals are perpendicular. In a **square**, diagonals are equal, perpendicular, and bisect each other at 90°.

#### Regular Polygons
A **regular polygon** has all sides equal and all interior angles equal. The interior angle of a regular polygon with n sides = (n − 2) × 180° ÷ n. Regular triangle = 60°, regular quadrilateral (square) = 90°, regular pentagon = 108°, regular hexagon = 120°, regular octagon = 135°. The **exterior angle** of any regular polygon = 360° ÷ n. These values appear frequently in competition questions.

#### Diagonals of Polygons
The number of diagonals in a polygon with n sides = n(n − 3) ÷ 2. A triangle has 0 diagonals, a quadrilateral has 2, a pentagon has 5, a hexagon has 9. This formula is derived from choosing 2 vertices from n (that's C(n,2) = n(n−1)/2 combinations) and subtracting the n sides. Understanding this formula shows up in counting-based competition problems.

#### Perimeter of Polygons
The **perimeter** of any polygon is the sum of all its side lengths. For a regular polygon with n sides of length s: P = n × s. For a rectangle with length l and width w: P = 2l + 2w = 2(l + w). Always check whether the problem gives the side, the perimeter, or requires you to apply the Pythagorean theorem to find an unknown side before calculating perimeter.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the interior angle measure of a regular hexagon?
2. A rhombus has all sides of length 7. What is its perimeter?
3. How many diagonals does a pentagon have?

### Finding Area and Perimeter: Worked Examples
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-math-area-perimeter-application
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** area · perimeter · composite figures · formula application · units
**Summary:** Apply area and perimeter formulas to triangles, quadrilaterals, and composite figures through worked examples.

#### Key Area Formulas
Master these formulas: **Rectangle**: A = l × w. **Triangle**: A = ½bh (height is perpendicular). **Parallelogram**: A = bh. **Trapezoid**: A = ½(b₁ + b₂)h. **Circle**: A = πr². **Square**: A = s². Always include **square units** (cm², m², ft²) in your answer. If the problem gives a diameter, remember r = d ÷ 2 before applying the circle formula.

#### Worked Example: Triangle in a Rectangle
A rectangle is 10 cm long and 6 cm wide. A diagonal divides it into two triangles. Find the area of one triangle. **Step 1:** Area of rectangle = 10 × 6 = 60 cm². **Step 2:** Each triangle is half the rectangle = 60 ÷ 2 = **30 cm²**. Alternatively, the triangle has base 10 cm and height 6 cm, so A = ½ × 10 × 6 = 30 cm². Both approaches confirm the answer.

#### Worked Example: Composite Figure
A shape consists of a rectangle (8 m × 5 m) with a triangle on top (base 8 m, height 3 m). Find total area. **Step 1:** Rectangle area = 8 × 5 = 40 m². **Step 2:** Triangle area = ½ × 8 × 3 = 12 m². **Step 3:** Total = 40 + 12 = **52 m²**. For composite figures: identify the component shapes, find each area separately, then add (or subtract if a piece is cut out).

#### Worked Example: Trapezoid
A trapezoid has parallel sides of 6 cm and 10 cm and a height of 4 cm. Find its area. **A = ½(b₁ + b₂)h = ½(6 + 10)(4) = ½ × 16 × 4 = ½ × 64 = 32 cm²**. The trapezoid formula averages the two bases and multiplies by height. Think of it as finding the "average width" times the height.

#### Worked Example: Finding Perimeter with the Pythagorean Theorem
A right triangle has legs of 6 cm and 8 cm. Find its perimeter. **Step 1:** Find hypotenuse: c² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100, so c = 10 cm. **Step 2:** Perimeter = 6 + 8 + 10 = **24 cm**. Notice that (6, 8, 10) is a multiple of (3, 4, 5). Recognizing Pythagorean triples saves time in competition settings.

#### Review Questions
1. A parallelogram has a base of 9 m and a height of 5 m. What is its area?
2. A composite figure consists of a square with side 4 cm and a semicircle of diameter 4 cm on one side. Find the total area. (Use π ≈ 3.14)
3. A trapezoid has parallel sides of 5 and 9, and height 6. What is its area?

### Angle Reasoning in Multi-Step Problems
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-math-angle-reasoning-application
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** angle relationships · parallel lines · transversal · solving for unknowns · algebra with angles
**Summary:** Use angle relationships and algebra to solve multi-step angle problems.

#### Setting Up Angle Equations
Many angle problems require algebra. If two supplementary angles are (3x + 10)° and (2x − 5)°, set up: (3x + 10) + (2x − 5) = 180. Simplify: 5x + 5 = 180 → 5x = 175 → x = 35. Then each angle: 3(35) + 10 = 115° and 2(35) − 5 = 65°. Check: 115 + 65 = 180 ✓. The strategy is always: write the relationship, substitute expressions, solve for the variable, and verify.

#### Multi-Step: Parallel Lines and Transversal
Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. One angle is (4x + 20)° and its alternate interior angle is (6x − 10)°. Since alternate interior angles are equal: 4x + 20 = 6x − 10 → 30 = 2x → x = 15. The angles each measure 4(15) + 20 = **80°**. Alternate interior angles equal each other — set them equal and solve. Co-interior (same-side interior) angles are supplementary — set their sum to 180.

#### Multi-Step: Finding All Angles in a Triangle
In a triangle, one angle is twice the smallest angle, and the third angle is 30° more than the smallest. Set smallest angle = x: x + 2x + (x + 30) = 180 → 4x + 30 = 180 → 4x = 150 → x = 37.5°. The three angles are 37.5°, 75°, and 67.5°. Check: 37.5 + 75 + 67.5 = 180 ✓. Always verify that the three angles sum to 180°.

#### Using the Exterior Angle Theorem
The exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles. If a triangle has angles x, 2x, and an exterior angle of 105°: the exterior angle = x + 2x (the two non-adjacent interior angles) → 3x = 105 → x = 35°. The interior angles are 35°, 70°, and 75° (since the third angle = 180 − 105 = 75°). The exterior angle theorem is a powerful shortcut.

#### Competition Strategy: Work from What You Know
In complex figures, label every angle you can determine immediately, then use those values to find others. Work outward from known angles. Use the following in order: (1) vertical angles are equal, (2) supplementary angles sum to 180°, (3) triangle angles sum to 180°, (4) parallel line relationships. Don't jump to algebra immediately — direct reasoning is often faster in timed settings.

#### Review Questions
1. Two complementary angles are in the ratio 2:3. Find each angle.
2. A triangle has an exterior angle of 130°. One non-adjacent interior angle is 55°. Find the other non-adjacent interior angle.
3. In a parallelogram, one angle is 72°. Find all four angles of the parallelogram.

### Geometry Mixed Review: Angles and Polygons
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** ms-math-geometry-mixed-review-angles
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** review · angle sums · polygon properties · Pythagorean theorem · problem synthesis
**Summary:** Synthesize angle, triangle, and polygon concepts through varied review problems.

#### Quick Recall: Essential Formulas
Run through these before timed practice: Interior angle sum of n-gon = (n−2)×180°. Each angle of regular n-gon = (n−2)×180°/n. Exterior angle of regular n-gon = 360°/n. Triangle angle sum = 180°. Pythagorean theorem: a² + b² = c² (right triangles only). Supplementary angles sum to 180°; complementary to 90°. Alternate interior angles (parallel lines) are equal.

#### Mixed Problem Set A
1. A regular polygon has interior angles of 140°. How many sides does it have? *Solution:* (n−2)×180/n = 140 → 180n−360 = 140n → 40n = 360 → n = **9 sides**.
2. A right triangle has a hypotenuse of 13 and one leg of 5. Find the other leg. *Solution:* 5² + b² = 13² → 25 + b² = 169 → b² = 144 → b = **12**.
3. Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. A co-interior angle pair measures (5x + 15)° and (3x + 5)°. Find x. *Solution:* Co-interior angles are supplementary: 5x+15+3x+5 = 180 → 8x+20 = 180 → x = **20**.

#### Mixed Problem Set B
1. A rhombus has one angle of 65°. Find all four angles. *Solution:* Opposite angles are equal; consecutive angles are supplementary. Angles: 65°, 115°, 65°, 115°.
2. In △ABC, ∠A = 3x, ∠B = 2x, ∠C = x. Find all three angles. *Solution:* 6x = 180 → x = 30. Angles: 90°, 60°, 30°. This is a right triangle!
3. How many diagonals does a hexagon have? *Solution:* n(n−3)/2 = 6(3)/2 = **9 diagonals**.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid
(1) Using the Pythagorean theorem on non-right triangles — always confirm there's a right angle first. (2) Confusing interior and exterior angles. (3) Forgetting that "regular" means BOTH all sides equal AND all angles equal. (4) Applying parallel-line rules to non-parallel lines — check that the lines are explicitly stated as parallel.

#### Timed Practice Strategy
For Science Bowl geometry questions: first, identify what type of angle or polygon problem it is (takes 2–3 seconds). Then recall the relevant formula or relationship. Set up the equation. Solve. In toss-up format, you must interrupt when confident — practice recognizing key clue words: "regular polygon," "parallel lines," "right triangle," "exterior angle."

#### Review Questions
1. What is the exterior angle of a regular pentagon?
2. A right triangle has legs in the ratio 3:4. If the hypotenuse is 20, find both legs.
3. Two angles are supplementary. The larger is 4 times the smaller. Find both angles.

### Geometry Competition Extension: Polygon and Angle Patterns
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** ms-math-geometry-competition-angles
**Estimated time:** 8 min
**Key concepts:** competition toss-up patterns · polygon angle formulas · Pythagorean triples · speed recognition
**Summary:** High-yield competition patterns for polygon and angle problems with bowl-style toss-up practice.

#### Bowl-Style Toss-Up Practice
*"For 10 points — what is the sum of the interior angles of a decagon?"* Answer: (10−2)×180 = 8×180 = **1440°**. *"For 10 points — a right triangle has legs of 7 and 24. What is the length of the hypotenuse?"* Answer: 7²+24² = 49+576 = 625 = 25². **25**. *"For 10 points — each interior angle of a regular polygon measures 150°. How many sides does the polygon have?"* Answer: exterior angle = 30°, sides = 360/30 = **12**.

#### Pythagorean Triple Speed Chart
Memorize: (3,4,5), (5,12,13), (8,15,17), (7,24,25), (20,21,29). And their multiples: (6,8,10), (9,12,15), (10,24,26), (15,36,39). When you see two sides of a right triangle, immediately check if they fit a known triple — this bypasses all arithmetic. If one leg is 15 and the hypotenuse is 17: recognize (8,15,17) → other leg is **8**.

#### Angle-Chase Shortcuts
Vertical angles: equal, always. Angles on a straight line: sum to 180°, always. Exterior angle of triangle: equals sum of two remote interior angles. Interior angles of equilateral triangle: 60° each. Base angles of isosceles triangle: equal. These five facts solve about 80% of angle-chase problems in MS Science Bowl. Identify which rule applies, don't over-calculate.

#### Common Trap: Exterior vs Interior
Questions often ask for the exterior angle when you calculated the interior, or vice versa. Interior + Exterior = 180° (they form a straight line). If a regular hexagon's interior angle is 120°, its exterior angle is 60°. Double-check which one the question asked for before buzzing.

#### Review Questions
1. For 10 points: what is the measure of each interior angle of a regular octagon?
2. A right triangle has legs of 9 and 40. What is the hypotenuse?
3. The exterior angle of a regular polygon is 24°. How many sides does the polygon have?

### Bowl Toss-Up: Pythagorean Theorem Speed Drill
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** ms-math-pythagorean-speed-drill
**Estimated time:** 7 min
**Key concepts:** Pythagorean theorem · speed recognition · triple identification · mental calculation
**Summary:** Build speed with Pythagorean theorem toss-up recognition and triple identification.

#### The Recognition Pattern
Most Science Bowl Pythagorean problems use either (a) common triples scaled up, or (b) simple perfect squares. When given two sides, your mental checklist: (1) Is it a 3-4-5 family? (2) Is it a 5-12-13 family? (3) Is it an 8-15-17 family? (4) Otherwise, compute a²+b²=c² directly. For c given: find a²=c²−b². Practice until identifying triples feels automatic.

#### Speed Drill Problems
Solve each in under 10 seconds: (1) Legs 6 and 8 → hyp = **10** [3-4-5 ×2]. (2) Leg 5 and hyp 13 → other leg = **12** [5-12-13]. (3) Legs 20 and 21 → hyp = **29** [20-21-29 triple]. (4) Legs 9 and 40 → hyp = **41** [9-40-41 triple]. (5) Leg 15 and hyp 17 → other leg = **8** [8-15-17]. (6) Legs 30 and 40 → hyp = **50** [3-4-5 ×10].

#### When It's Not a Triple: Direct Calculation
Legs 4 and 6: c² = 16+36 = 52. c = √52 = 2√13 ≈ 7.21. If the answer isn't a whole number, leave it as a simplified radical unless the problem asks for a decimal. Competition problems are usually designed to produce whole numbers — if you're getting an ugly answer, re-read the problem.

#### Toss-Up Clue Words
Listen for: "right triangle," "legs," "hypotenuse," "right angle," "perpendicular," "ladder against a wall," "diagonal of a rectangle." All of these signal the Pythagorean theorem. The diagonal of a rectangle with length l and width w is √(l²+w²) — the diagonal splits the rectangle into two right triangles.

#### Review Questions
1. For 10 points: a rectangle is 5 units long and 12 units wide. What is the length of its diagonal?
2. For 10 points: a right triangle has a hypotenuse of 25 and one leg of 20. Find the other leg.
3. For 10 points: legs of a right triangle are both equal to 6. What is the hypotenuse? Express as a simplified radical.

## Subtopic: Area, Perimeter & Volume

### Understanding Area vs. Perimeter
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-math-area-vs-perimeter
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** area · perimeter · square units · linear units · two-dimensional measurement
**Summary:** Distinguish area and perimeter conceptually and apply both to common shapes.

#### Perimeter: Measuring the Boundary
**Perimeter** is the total distance around the outside of a shape. It's a **one-dimensional** measurement expressed in linear units (cm, m, ft). To find perimeter: add up all the side lengths. For common shapes: Rectangle: P = 2(l + w). Square: P = 4s. Triangle: P = a + b + c. Regular polygon with n sides of length s: P = ns. Perimeter tells you how much fencing you need, or how far you'd walk around a shape.

#### Area: Measuring the Interior
**Area** is the amount of space inside a 2D figure. It's measured in **square units** (cm², m², ft²). Key formulas: Rectangle = l×w. Triangle = ½bh. Parallelogram = bh (h is perpendicular height, not the slanted side). Trapezoid = ½(b₁+b₂)h. Circle = πr². Area tells you how much paint you need to cover a wall, or how much carpet fits in a room.

#### Why the Formulas Work
The rectangle formula (l×w) counts how many unit squares fill the interior — l columns of w squares each. The triangle formula is ½ that of the enclosing rectangle because a diagonal always splits a rectangle into two equal triangles. The parallelogram formula (bh) works because you can cut a triangle from one end and attach it to the other to form a rectangle of the same base and height. Understanding WHY the formulas work makes them easier to remember and apply.

#### Common Misconceptions
(1) Perimeter and area are not proportional — doubling the sides of a square doubles the perimeter but **quadruples** the area. (2) The height in triangle and parallelogram formulas must be **perpendicular** to the base — not the slanted side. (3) Area is NOT always bigger than perimeter numerically — a 1×1 square has area 1 and perimeter 4. (4) Units: area always gets squared (cm²), perimeter does not.

#### Worked Example: Maximizing Area with Fixed Perimeter
A farmer has 24 m of fencing. What rectangle maximizes the enclosed area? Try different rectangles: 1×11 = 11 m², 2×10 = 20 m², 3×9 = 27 m², 4×8 = 32 m², 5×7 = 35 m², **6×6 = 36 m²**. The square always gives the maximum area for a fixed perimeter. This is a classic result in optimization.

#### Review Questions
1. A rectangle has perimeter 36 cm and length 11 cm. Find its width and area.
2. A triangle has base 8 m and height 5 m. Find its area.
3. True or false: if you double all the sides of a rectangle, its perimeter doubles and its area doubles.

### Volume and Surface Area of 3D Figures
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-math-volume-surface-area
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** volume · surface area · rectangular prism · cylinder · cube · cubic units
**Summary:** Learn volume and surface area formulas for common 3D figures and understand when to use each.

#### Volume: Filling 3D Space
**Volume** measures the amount of space inside a 3D object. It's expressed in **cubic units** (cm³, m³). Key formulas: **Rectangular prism** (box): V = l×w×h. **Cube**: V = s³. **Cylinder**: V = πr²h. **Triangular prism**: V = (area of triangular base) × h. Think of volume as layering 2D area — a cylinder is just a circle stacked h units high, so its volume is (area of circle) × height = πr²h.

#### Surface Area: Wrapping the Outside
**Surface area** is the total area of all the faces of a 3D object. It's measured in **square units** (cm²). **Rectangular prism**: SA = 2(lw + lh + wh) — there are 3 pairs of identical faces. **Cube**: SA = 6s² — six identical square faces. **Cylinder**: SA = 2πr² + 2πrh — two circular caps plus the rectangular lateral face (when "unrolled," it's a rectangle of width 2πr and height h).

#### Worked Example: Rectangular Prism
A box is 5 cm long, 4 cm wide, and 3 cm tall. Find volume and surface area. **Volume:** 5×4×3 = **60 cm³**. **Surface area:** 2(5×4 + 5×3 + 4×3) = 2(20+15+12) = 2(47) = **94 cm²**. For surface area, list all three distinct pairs of faces, sum their areas, and multiply by 2.

#### Worked Example: Cylinder
A cylinder has radius 3 cm and height 10 cm. Find volume and surface area. (Use π ≈ 3.14.) **Volume:** π×3²×10 = π×90 ≈ 3.14×90 ≈ **282.6 cm³**. **Surface area:** 2π×3² + 2π×3×10 = 18π + 60π = 78π ≈ **244.92 cm²**. Notice that the lateral surface area (the side of the cylinder) is the biggest contributor for tall, narrow cylinders.

#### Volume vs. Capacity: Real-World Applications
Volume tells you how much a container **holds** (capacity). A fish tank 60 cm × 30 cm × 40 cm holds 60×30×40 = 72,000 cm³ = **72 liters** (since 1 L = 1000 cm³). Volume problems often disguise themselves as rate problems: if a pipe fills a tank at 5 L/min and the tank holds 200 L, it takes 200/5 = 40 minutes to fill.

#### Review Questions
1. A cube has side length 4 cm. Find its volume and surface area.
2. A cylindrical can has radius 2 cm and height 7 cm. Find its volume. (Use π ≈ 3.14)
3. A rectangular prism has volume 120 cm³, length 5 cm, and width 4 cm. Find its height.

### Area and Volume Calculations: Worked Examples
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-math-area-volume-worked
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** area · volume · composite figures · unit conversion · problem setup
**Summary:** Solve multi-step area and volume problems using systematic, step-by-step approaches.

#### Worked: Shaded Region
A circle of radius 5 cm is inscribed in a square (the circle touches all four sides). Find the shaded area outside the circle. **Step 1:** Square side = diameter = 10 cm. Square area = 10² = 100 cm². **Step 2:** Circle area = π×5² = 25π ≈ 78.54 cm². **Step 3:** Shaded area = 100 − 78.54 ≈ **21.46 cm²**. Shaded-region problems always require: (total region) minus (the unshaded sub-region).

#### Worked: Painting a Room
A rectangular room is 6 m × 4 m with a ceiling height of 2.5 m. You paint all four walls and the ceiling (not the floor). Find the area to paint. **Step 1:** Two long walls: 2 × (6×2.5) = 30 m². **Step 2:** Two short walls: 2 × (4×2.5) = 20 m². **Step 3:** Ceiling: 6×4 = 24 m². **Total:** 30+20+24 = **74 m²**. Real-world surface area problems require identifying which faces to include.

#### Worked: Volume with a Hole
A rectangular block (8×6×4 cm) has a cylindrical hole drilled through it (radius 1 cm, all the way through the 4 cm depth). Find the remaining volume. **Step 1:** Block volume = 8×6×4 = 192 cm³. **Step 2:** Cylinder volume = π×1²×4 = 4π ≈ 12.57 cm³. **Step 3:** Remaining = 192 − 12.57 ≈ **179.43 cm³**. For "removed" volumes, calculate and subtract.

#### Worked: Scale Factor and Area
A map scale is 1 cm = 5 km. A lake on the map covers 3 cm². What is the actual area of the lake? **Step 1:** Linear scale factor = 5 km/1 cm = 5000 m/1 cm. **Step 2:** Area scale factor = 5000² = 25,000,000. **Step 3:** Actual area = 3 × 25,000,000 m² = 75,000,000 m² = **75 km²**. When scaling areas, square the linear scale factor.

#### Worked: Filling a Pool
A pool is 10 m × 5 m × 2 m. It currently holds water to a depth of 0.5 m. How many more cubic meters of water are needed to fill it? **Step 1:** Total capacity = 10×5×2 = 100 m³. **Step 2:** Current water = 10×5×0.5 = 25 m³. **Step 3:** Additional water needed = 100−25 = **75 m³**. Always read carefully whether the question asks for total or remaining volume.

## Subtopic: Coordinate Geometry

### The Coordinate Plane: Points, Distance, and Midpoint
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-math-coordinate-plane-basics
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** coordinate plane · ordered pair · quadrants · distance formula · midpoint formula
**Summary:** Navigate the coordinate plane and calculate distances and midpoints between two points.

#### The Coordinate Plane
The coordinate plane has two perpendicular number lines: the horizontal **x-axis** and the vertical **y-axis**, intersecting at the **origin** (0, 0). Points are located by **ordered pairs** (x, y): x measures horizontal position, y measures vertical. The plane is divided into four **quadrants**: QI (x>0, y>0), QII (x<0, y>0), QIII (x<0, y<0), QIV (x>0, y<0). A point ON an axis has one coordinate equal to zero.

#### The Distance Formula
To find the distance between two points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂): **d = √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²]**. This comes directly from the Pythagorean theorem — the horizontal and vertical differences are the legs, and d is the hypotenuse. Example: distance from (1, 2) to (4, 6): d = √[(4−1)²+(6−2)²] = √[9+16] = √25 = **5**. Always square the differences — subtracting order doesn't matter because they get squared.

#### The Midpoint Formula
The midpoint M of a segment from (x₁, y₁) to (x₂, y₂) is: **M = ((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2)**. This is simply the average of the x-coordinates and the average of the y-coordinates. Example: midpoint of (2, 4) and (8, 10): M = ((2+8)/2, (4+10)/2) = (5, 7). Midpoint problems often give you the midpoint and one endpoint, asking for the other — reverse the formula.

#### Finding Missing Endpoints
If M = (3, 5) is the midpoint of a segment with one endpoint (1, 2), find the other endpoint (x₂, y₂). Set up: (1+x₂)/2 = 3 → 1+x₂ = 6 → x₂ = 5. (2+y₂)/2 = 5 → 2+y₂ = 10 → y₂ = 8. Answer: (5, 8). Strategy: multiply the midpoint coordinate by 2, then subtract the known endpoint.

#### Plotting Geometric Figures
Points on a coordinate plane can form geometric figures. To find the perimeter of a polygon: find the length of each side using the distance formula, then add. To find area: sometimes you can use the coordinate bounding box method (enclosing rectangle minus corner triangles) or directly apply area formulas if the shape is regular.

#### Review Questions
1. Find the distance between (−3, 1) and (1, 4).
2. Find the midpoint of (−4, 6) and (2, −2).
3. The midpoint of a segment is (4, 3). One endpoint is (7, 5). Find the other endpoint.

### Graphing Linear Equations
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-math-graphing-linear-equations
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** slope · y-intercept · slope-intercept form · x-intercept · linear equation
**Summary:** Understand slope and y-intercept and use them to graph linear equations on the coordinate plane.

#### Slope: Rise over Run
The **slope** of a line measures its steepness: **m = (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁) = rise/run**. A positive slope goes up left-to-right; negative slope goes down. Slope = 0 is horizontal; undefined slope is vertical. Example: from (1, 3) to (4, 9): m = (9−3)/(4−1) = 6/3 = **2**. For every 1 unit right, the line rises 2 units. Slope is constant anywhere along a line — it doesn't matter which two points you choose.

#### Slope-Intercept Form
The equation y = mx + b is **slope-intercept form**: m is the slope and b is the **y-intercept** (where the line crosses the y-axis). To graph: (1) plot the point (0, b) on the y-axis, (2) use the slope to find a second point (rise up m, run right 1), (3) draw the line through both points. Example: y = 2x − 3: plot (0, −3), then go up 2, right 1 to (1, −1), then draw the line.

#### Finding Intercepts
**y-intercept**: set x = 0 and solve for y. **x-intercept**: set y = 0 and solve for x. For y = 3x + 6: y-intercept = 6 (plug in x=0). x-intercept: 0 = 3x+6 → x = −2. So the line crosses the y-axis at (0, 6) and the x-axis at (−2, 0). Always verify by plugging the intercept back into the equation.

#### Slope from a Graph
To read slope from a graph: pick two clear lattice points (where the line crosses grid intersections). Count the vertical rise and horizontal run between them. Rise/run = slope. If the line falls from left to right, slope is negative. Horizontal lines have slope 0; vertical lines have undefined (infinite) slope. Be careful to read the scale on the axes — gridlines may represent increments other than 1.

#### Parallel and Perpendicular Lines
**Parallel** lines have the **same slope** but different y-intercepts. **Perpendicular** lines have slopes that are **negative reciprocals** of each other — if one line has slope m, the perpendicular line has slope −1/m. Example: a line with slope 2/3 is perpendicular to a line with slope −3/2. Product of perpendicular slopes = −1. These relationships are key for coordinate geometry proofs.

#### Review Questions
1. Find the slope of the line through (2, 5) and (6, 13).
2. Write the equation of a line with slope 3 and y-intercept −4 in slope-intercept form.
3. What is the slope of a line perpendicular to y = ½x + 3?

### Coordinate Geometry Application: Area and Distance
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-math-coordinate-geometry-application
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** distance formula · midpoint · coordinate area · collinearity · geometric figures on a plane
**Summary:** Use coordinate methods to find areas, distances, and properties of geometric figures.

#### Worked: Finding Area of a Triangle on the Coordinate Plane
Triangle with vertices A(1,1), B(5,1), C(3,5). **Step 1:** Base AB is horizontal: length = 5−1 = 4 units. **Step 2:** Height from C to line AB (the x-axis level y=1): height = 5−1 = 4 units. **Step 3:** Area = ½ × 4 × 4 = **8 square units**. When one side is horizontal or vertical, finding base and height is straightforward. For tilted triangles, use the enclosing rectangle method.

#### Worked: Enclosing Rectangle Method
Triangle with vertices P(1,2), Q(4,6), R(6,2). Enclose in rectangle from x=1 to x=6, y=2 to y=6 (width 5, height 4, area 20). Subtract the three corner right triangles: left triangle (base 3, height 4): ½×3×4=6. Right triangle (base 2, height 4): ½×2×4=4. Bottom triangle (base 5, height 0... wait — P and R are on the same horizontal line y=2). Bottom "triangle" is flat with area 0. Triangle area = 20 − 6 − 4 = **10 square units**.

#### Worked: Verifying a Right Triangle
Given A(0,0), B(3,4), C(−4,3). Verify that angle A is 90°. **Step 1:** Slope of AB = 4/3. **Step 2:** Slope of AC = 3/(−4) = −3/4. **Step 3:** Product of slopes = (4/3)(−3/4) = −1. Since the product is −1, the lines are perpendicular → angle A = **90°**. This is the coordinate geometry proof for right angles.

#### Worked: Midpoint as Center
A diameter of a circle has endpoints (2, 4) and (8, 10). Find the center and radius. **Center** = midpoint = ((2+8)/2, (4+10)/2) = **(5, 7)**. **Radius** = half the diameter = ½ × distance between endpoints. Distance = √[(8−2)²+(10−4)²] = √[36+36] = √72 = 6√2. Radius = **3√2 ≈ 4.24 units**.

#### Checking if Three Points Are Collinear
Points A(1,2), B(3,6), C(5,10) — are they collinear (on the same line)? Slope AB = (6−2)/(3−1) = 4/2 = 2. Slope BC = (10−6)/(5−3) = 4/2 = 2. Same slope → **collinear**. Alternatively, check that C lies on the line through A and B: line y = 2x gives y = 2(5) = 10 ✓.

#### Review Questions
1. Find the area of a rectangle with vertices (1,1), (5,1), (5,4), (1,4).
2. A triangle has vertices (0,0), (6,0), and (0,8). Find its area and the length of its hypotenuse.
3. Are the points (1,3), (3,7), and (5,11) collinear? Explain.

## Subtopic: Transformations (translations, reflections, rotations)

### Understanding Transformations
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-math-transformations-intro
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** transformation · translation · reflection · rotation · dilation · congruence · pre-image · image
**Summary:** Understand the four types of geometric transformations and their properties.

#### What Is a Transformation?
A **transformation** moves or changes a geometric figure. The original figure is the **pre-image**; the result is the **image**. The image is labeled with prime marks: A → A', B → B'. There are four main transformations: **Translation** (slide), **Reflection** (flip), **Rotation** (turn), and **Dilation** (resize). The first three are **rigid motions** (isometries) — they preserve size and shape, so the pre-image and image are **congruent**.

#### Translation
A **translation** slides every point of a figure the same distance in the same direction. It's described by a rule: (x, y) → (x + a, y + b), where a is the horizontal shift and b is the vertical shift. Example: translate point (3, 4) by (2, −3) → (3+2, 4+(−3)) = **(5, 1)**. Positive a moves right; negative moves left. Positive b moves up; negative moves down. Every point in the figure moves identically.

#### Reflection
A **reflection** flips a figure over a **line of reflection** (mirror line). Key reflection rules over common lines: over the **x-axis**: (x, y) → (x, −y). Over the **y-axis**: (x, y) → (−x, y). Over the line **y = x**: (x, y) → (y, x). Over the line **y = −x**: (x, y) → (−y, −x). The image is a mirror copy — same shape and size, but reversed. The line of reflection is equidistant from each point and its image.

#### Rotation
A **rotation** turns a figure around a fixed point called the **center of rotation**. Rotations are measured in degrees and can be clockwise or counterclockwise. Common rotations about the origin: **90° counterclockwise**: (x,y) → (−y, x). **180°**: (x,y) → (−x, −y). **270° counterclockwise** (= 90° clockwise): (x,y) → (y, −x). A 360° rotation brings the figure back to its original position.

#### Dilation
A **dilation** scales a figure larger or smaller relative to a center point using a **scale factor** k. If k > 1, the figure enlarges. If 0 < k < 1, the figure shrinks. If k = 1, no change. Rule: (x, y) → (kx, ky) when the center is the origin. Dilation is **NOT** a rigid motion — it doesn't preserve size (unless k = 1), but it DOES preserve shape. Pre-image and image are **similar**, not congruent.

#### Review Questions
1. Translate point (−2, 5) by the rule (x, y) → (x + 4, y − 3). What is the image?
2. Reflect point (3, −4) over the x-axis. What is the image?
3. Rotate point (4, 2) by 90° counterclockwise about the origin. What is the image?

### Applying Transformations and Symmetry
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-math-transformations-symmetry
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** line symmetry · rotational symmetry · composition of transformations · congruence
**Summary:** Explore symmetry and multiple-transformation sequences in geometric figures.

#### Line Symmetry (Reflective Symmetry)
A figure has **line symmetry** (or reflective symmetry) if a line divides it into two mirror-image halves. The line is called the **axis of symmetry**. Regular polygons: equilateral triangle has 3 lines, square has 4, regular pentagon has 5, regular hexagon has 6. A circle has infinitely many. Letters: A, M, T, U, V, W, Y have vertical symmetry; B, C, D, E, K have horizontal symmetry; H, I, O, X have both.

#### Rotational Symmetry
A figure has **rotational symmetry** if it looks the same after a rotation of less than 360°. The **order** of rotational symmetry is the number of positions in one full rotation where the figure looks identical. A square has order 4 (looks same at 90°, 180°, 270°, 360°). An equilateral triangle has order 3. A regular hexagon has order 6. The angle of rotation = 360° ÷ order. A figure with no rotational symmetry has order 1.

#### Composition of Transformations
A **composition** applies two or more transformations in sequence. The result is often a single equivalent transformation. A reflection over two parallel lines = a translation. A reflection over two intersecting lines = a rotation. A glide reflection is a translation followed by a reflection over the line of translation. When composing, work right-to-left (or first transformation first) — order matters! (AB ≠ BA in general.)

#### Identifying Transformations from Coordinates
Given pre-image and image coordinates, identify the transformation: If all coordinates shifted by the same amount → **translation**. If x-coordinates changed sign but y stayed → **reflection over y-axis**. If y changed sign but x stayed → **reflection over x-axis**. If x and y swapped → **reflection over y = x**. If both signs changed → **180° rotation**. If x,y swapped and one sign changed → **90° rotation**.

#### Tessellations
A **tessellation** is a tiling of the plane using one or more shapes with no gaps or overlaps. Regular polygons that tessellate alone: equilateral triangle, square, regular hexagon. These are the only three. M.C. Escher created famous art using transformation-based tessellations. A shape tessellates if its interior angles can sum to exactly 360° around each vertex.

#### Review Questions
1. How many lines of symmetry does a regular pentagon have?
2. What is the order of rotational symmetry of a rectangle (not a square)?
3. Point A is at (2, 3). First, reflect over the y-axis. Then rotate 90° counterclockwise. Where does A end up?

When a problem says "rotated 90°" without specifying direction, assume counterclockwise (the math convention) unless told otherwise. Clockwise 90° is the same as counterclockwise 270°.
