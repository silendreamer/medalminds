#### What Is a Free-Body Diagram?
A free-body diagram (FBD) shows all the forces acting on one object as arrows pointing in the direction of each force, with length proportional to magnitude. The object is drawn as a box or dot. Common forces: weight (W = mg, downward), normal force (perpendicular to surface, upward on horizontal), friction (opposing motion), tension (along a rope, away from object), applied force (in the direction pushed or pulled). Label each arrow with the force name and magnitude.

#### Example: Box on a Horizontal Surface
A 5 kg box is pushed right with 30 N. Friction opposes motion with 10 N. Draw the FBD: Weight = 50 N down; Normal = 50 N up; Applied = 30 N right; Friction = 10 N left. Vertical: 50 − 50 = 0 N (no vertical acceleration, as expected). Horizontal: 30 − 10 = 20 N right. Net force = 20 N right. a = F/m = 20/5 = 4 m/s² right.

#### Example: Hanging Object
A 2 kg ball hangs from a rope. Weight pulls down: W = 20 N. Tension pulls up. Since the ball doesn't accelerate, tension = weight = 20 N. If the ball accelerates upward at 3 m/s², then net force = ma = 2 × 3 = 6 N upward. Tension − Weight = 6 N → Tension = 26 N. The rope must exert more force when the ball accelerates upward.

#### Review Questions
1. Draw a free-body diagram for a book at rest on a table. Label all forces.
2. A 10 kg box on a table has an applied force of 40 N right and friction of 15 N left. What is the net force and acceleration?
3. Why does the normal force on an object on a horizontal surface equal the object's weight only when acceleration is zero?
