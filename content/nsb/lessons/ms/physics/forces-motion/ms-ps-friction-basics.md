#### Defining Friction
Friction is a force that opposes relative motion (or attempted motion) between two surfaces in contact. It acts parallel to the surfaces and opposite to the direction of motion (or intended motion). Friction is caused by microscopic roughness at the contact surface — even surfaces that look smooth have tiny bumps and valleys that catch on each other. Friction is not always bad: without it, you couldn't walk, tires couldn't grip roads, and you couldn't hold a pencil.

#### Static vs. Kinetic Friction
**Static friction** acts when surfaces are not moving relative to each other. It prevents objects from starting to move. Static friction can vary up to a maximum value. **Kinetic friction** (sliding friction) acts when surfaces are sliding. It is constant and always less than the maximum static friction. This is why it's harder to start sliding a heavy box than to keep it sliding — you must overcome greater static friction to get it moving.

#### What Affects Friction?
Friction depends on: (1) the **normal force** (how hard the surfaces press together) — more weight means more friction; (2) the **materials** in contact, described by the coefficient of friction (μ). Rougher surfaces have higher μ. Friction does NOT depend on the contact area — a wide brick and a narrow brick with the same weight have the same friction. This surprises many students and appears in Science Bowl.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between static and kinetic friction?
2. You press a book against a wall with your hand. Which type of friction keeps the book from sliding down?
3. Does doubling the surface area of a sliding object double the friction? Explain.
