#### Understanding Density

**Density** measures how tightly packed material is in a space. It's the amount of matter (mass) packed into a volume. For example, a rock is dense—lots of material squeezed into a small space. A sponge is less dense because it has much air mixed with material, taking up more space. Objects made of the same material can have different densities if they have different amounts of air inside. Density = Mass ÷ Volume. Water has a density of 1 gram per cubic centimeter, which scientists use as a reference standard.

#### Pressure in Fluids

**Pressure** is the force pushing on a surface. In fluids (liquids and gases), pressure comes from the weight of material above pushing down. The deeper you go underwater, the greater the pressure because more water weight sits above you. Pressure acts in all directions—not just down. Air at sea level pushes with atmospheric pressure; the air above creates weight that we measure as about 14.7 pounds per square inch. Submarines need thick walls to resist the crushing pressure at ocean depths.

#### Buoyancy: Why Things Float

**Buoyancy** is the upward force a fluid exerts on an object. An object floats when the buoyant force equals or exceeds its weight. Whether something sinks or floats depends on its density compared to the fluid. An object less dense than water (like wood or a boat) will float. An object denser than water (like a rock) will sink. A submarine can control whether it floats or sinks by adjusting the water inside its tanks—adding water increases density, making it sink; pumping water out decreases density, making it rise.

#### The Relationship Between Density and Floating

Dense objects sink in less-dense liquids, and less-dense objects float in denser liquids. Ice floats on water because ice (frozen water) is slightly less dense than liquid water. Oil floats on water because oil is less dense. Steel ships float because they're shaped hollow—their overall density (including the air inside) remains less than water. A solid steel ball would sink, but a steel bowl with air inside floats.
