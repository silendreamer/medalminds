## Subtopic: Plant Structures & Functions

### What Are the Main Parts of a Plant?
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** main-plant-parts
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** roots · stems · leaves · vascular tissue
**Summary:** Plants have specialized structures designed for water uptake, support, and photosynthesis.

#### The Root System
Roots anchor plants and absorb water and nutrients from soil. Most plants have either a taproot (one main root, like carrots) or fibrous roots (many thin roots spreading out, like grasses). Root hair cells increase surface area for absorption. Water moves up through the xylem, a vascular tissue that transports water from roots to leaves.

#### The Stem and Vascular Tissue
Stems support leaves and flowers above ground and contain vascular bundles — xylem carries water up, and phloem carries sugars down from leaves. In woody plants, stems thicken over time with annual rings. Herbaceous plants (like grass) have soft, green stems, while woody plants (like trees) have thick, hard wood.

#### Leaves and Photosynthesis
Leaves are the plant's food factories. The waxy upper surface (cuticle) reduces water loss. Tiny pores called stomata (singular: stoma) on the leaf's underside allow gas exchange. Inside leaves, photosynthesis happens in cells packed with chloroplasts.

#### Chloroplasts and Photosynthesis Overview
Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, the green pigment that captures light energy. The photosynthesis equation is: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose) + 6O₂. This process converts light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the main function of a plant's roots?
2. Which vascular tissue carries water from roots to leaves?
3. What green pigment in chloroplasts captures light for photosynthesis?

### Vascular vs. Nonvascular Plants
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** vascular-nonvascular-plants
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** vascular plants · nonvascular plants · xylem · phloem
**Summary:** Plants are classified by whether they have specialized tissues to transport water and nutrients.

#### What Are Nonvascular Plants?
Nonvascular plants (mosses, liverworts, hornworts) lack xylem and phloem, so they cannot transport water efficiently over distances. They stay small and must live in moist environments. Water and nutrients move from cell to cell by diffusion. Nonvascular plants reproduce with spores, not seeds.

#### What Are Vascular Plants?
Vascular plants have xylem and phloem that move water and nutrients throughout the plant body. This allows them to grow taller and survive in drier environments. Vascular plants include ferns, gymnosperms (conifers), and angiosperms (flowering plants).

#### Ferns: The Ancient Vascular Plants
Ferns were among the first vascular plants, thriving for hundreds of millions of years. They reproduce using spores released from structures called sori on the underside of fronds (leaves). Ferns still need moisture to complete their life cycle.

#### Gymnosperms and Angiosperms
Gymnosperms (pines, firs, spruces) produce naked seeds in cones. Angiosperms (flowering plants) enclose seeds in fruits. Both have vascular tissue and can live in diverse climates. Angiosperms are the most diverse and dominant plants on Earth today.

#### Why Vascular Tissue Matters
Vascular tissue was a major evolutionary breakthrough. It allowed plants to grow taller, transport resources efficiently, and colonize drier lands. Without vascular tissue, plants cannot reach beyond a few inches in height.

#### Review Questions
1. Why are nonvascular plants restricted to moist environments?
2. Name three types of vascular plants.
3. What advantage does vascular tissue give plants?

### Plant Tissues: Dermal, Ground, and Vascular
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** plant-tissue-systems
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** dermal tissue · ground tissue · vascular tissue · tissue systems
**Summary:** Plants have three tissue systems, each with specific functions.

#### Dermal Tissue: The Plant's Skin
The epidermis is a single layer of tightly packed cells covering leaves, stems, and roots. It protects the plant and reduces water loss through a waxy cuticle. Stomata, found mainly on leaf undersides, are tiny pores surrounded by guard cells that open and close to control gas exchange and water loss.

#### Ground Tissue: Storage and Support
Ground tissue (mesophyll in leaves, cortex in roots and stems) stores food, provides structural support, and conducts photosynthesis. In leaves, palisade mesophyll (tall cells just under the epidermis) absorbs light, while spongy mesophyll (loose cells with air spaces) facilitates gas exchange.

#### Vascular Tissue: Transport System
The two vascular tissues are xylem (transports water and minerals upward) and phloem (transports sugars downward). Xylem cells are dead at maturity, forming hollow tubes. Phloem cells are alive and have sieve-tube elements connected by perforated sieve plates for sugar transport.

#### How Tissues Work Together
The dermal tissue protects and controls what enters/exits. Ground tissue provides structure and processes materials. Vascular tissue distributes water and food. Together, these three tissue systems enable plants to live in varied environments.

#### Plant Tissue Organization in Different Organs
Leaves have epidermis on top and bottom, mesophyll in the middle, and veins (vascular tissue) running through. Roots have epidermis on the outside, cortex for storage, and a central vascular cylinder. Stems have a ring of vascular bundles surrounded by cortex.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the main function of dermal tissue?
2. Why are xylem cells dead while phloem cells are alive?
3. How does ground tissue differ in leaves versus roots?

### Plant Adaptations for Growth
**Type:** Application
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** adaptation · efficient design · resource allocation
**Summary:** Plants show remarkable adaptations in structure that reflect their environment and growth strategy.

#### Desert Plants: Water-Saving Adaptations
Cacti have thick, waxy cuticles to reduce water loss and few stomata that open only at night. Their leaves are modified into spines to minimize surface area. Roots spread wide and shallow to capture rare rainfall. Example: Saguaro cactus survives Arizona's heat with these features.

#### Aquatic Plants: Floating and Submerged Strategies
Water lilies have floating leaves with waxy tops and stomata on the upper surface only. Submerged plants like pondweed lack a thick cuticle since water surrounds them. Some aquatic plants have air-filled spaces (aerenchyma) for buoyancy. These adaptations let plants thrive in water where oxygen is scarce.

#### Shade Plants: Maximum Light Capture
Plants in forests have large, thin leaves to capture dim light. They grow tall to reach canopy light or spread leaves horizontally to intercept any available light. Example: Hostas have enormous leaves and live on forest floors beneath trees.

#### Climbing Plants: Structures for Support
Ivy uses adhesive rootlets to cling to walls. Peas use tendrils (modified stems) to twine around supports. Clematis uses leaf petioles as graspers. These adaptations allow weak-stemmed plants to reach light by climbing stronger structures.

#### Review Questions
1. How do desert plant adaptations reduce water loss?
2. What are three climbing plant adaptations?
3. Why do shade plants have larger leaves than sun plants?

### Monocots vs. Dicots
**Type:** Application
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** monocots · dicots · seed structure · leaf venation
**Summary:** Flowering plants are divided into two classes based on seed and structural differences.

#### Seed Structure: One vs. Two Cotyledons
Monocots (grasses, lilies, orchids) have one cotyledon (seed leaf) in their seed. Dicots (beans, tomatoes, oak trees) have two cotyledons. The cotyledon provides nutrients to the germinating seed until the plant can photosynthesize.

#### Leaf Venation Patterns
Monocot leaves have parallel venation — veins run side-by-side from base to tip (think of corn or grass leaves). Dicot leaves have net venation — veins branch like a network (think of oak or maple leaves). These patterns reflect how water and sugar are distributed.

#### Root Systems
Monocots typically have fibrous root systems (many thin roots, like wheat). Dicots usually have taproots (one main root, like carrots or radishes). This difference relates to seed structure and how each class germinates.

#### Stem and Flower Structure
Monocot flower parts come in multiples of three (three petals, three sepals). Monocot stems have scattered vascular bundles. Dicot flower parts come in fours or fives, and dicot stems have a ring of vascular bundles. These differences reflect how each group evolved.

#### Common Monocots vs. Dicots
Monocots: grasses (wheat, corn, rice), lilies, tulips, orchids, palms. Dicots: beans, peas, roses, daisies, oak, maple, tomatoes. Monocots and dicots make up about 25% and 75% of flowering plants, respectively.

#### Review Questions
1. How many cotyledons does a monocot seed have?
2. What is the difference between parallel and net venation?
3. Name two monocots and two dicots.

## Subtopic: Photosynthesis & Gas Exchange

### The Photosynthesis Equation and Inputs
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** photosynthesis-equation
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** photosynthesis equation · inputs · carbon dioxide · water · light
**Summary:** Photosynthesis converts light energy, water, and carbon dioxide into glucose and oxygen.

#### The Chemical Equation
6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. This equation summarizes the entire process. Six molecules of carbon dioxide combine with six molecules of water using energy from light to produce one molecule of glucose (a sugar) and six molecules of oxygen.

#### Where Does Carbon Dioxide Come From?
Plants absorb CO₂ from the air through tiny pores called stomata on their leaves. During the day, stomata open to let CO₂ in. At night, they close to reduce water loss. In heavily populated areas or indoors, CO₂ levels around plants are similar to atmospheric CO₂ (~420 ppm).

#### How Plants Absorb Water
Plant roots absorb water from soil through root hair cells. Water moves up through the xylem from the roots to the leaves. Some water is used in photosynthesis, but most is lost through transpiration (evaporation from leaves). A single corn plant may lose 200+ liters of water during a growing season.

#### Why Light Is Essential
Only light energy can break apart water molecules and CO₂ to build glucose. Without light, photosynthesis cannot happen. Different wavelengths of light are absorbed differently — chlorophyll absorbs blue and red light most efficiently, which is why plants appear green (green light is reflected).

#### The Purpose of Photosynthesis
Photosynthesis serves two main purposes: (1) produce glucose for energy and growth, and (2) release oxygen as a waste product. All the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere comes from photosynthesis by plants, algae, and cyanobacteria over billions of years.

#### Review Questions
1. What are the three main inputs to photosynthesis?
2. Where does most water absorbed by roots go?
3. Why does photosynthesis need light energy?

### The Light-Dependent and Light-Independent Reactions
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** light-reactions-dark-reactions
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** light reactions · Calvin cycle · ATP · NADPH · chloroplast
**Summary:** Photosynthesis has two main stages: light reactions in the thylakoid and the Calvin cycle in the stroma.

#### The Light Reactions in the Thylakoid
The thylakoid is a flattened sac inside the chloroplast where light reactions happen. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy, exciting electrons. These energized electrons move through an electron transport chain, pumping protons and generating ATP and NADPH. Water is split in this process, releasing oxygen as a byproduct.

#### What ATP and NADPH Do
ATP and NADPH are energy carriers produced by the light reactions. They carry energy to the Calvin cycle, where this energy is used to build glucose. Think of ATP as a coin and NADPH as a gift card — both carry energy in forms the plant can "spend."

#### The Calvin Cycle (Light-Independent Reactions)
The Calvin cycle, occurring in the stroma, uses ATP and NADPH to fix CO₂ into glucose. It occurs in three phases: (1) CO₂ fixation combines CO₂ with RuBP (a 5-carbon sugar) to form unstable 6-carbon molecules; (2) reduction uses ATP and NADPH to reduce these molecules; (3) regeneration uses ATP to regenerate RuBP. The cycle repeats about 3 times to produce one glucose molecule.

#### Why They're Called Light-Dependent and Light-Independent
Light reactions absolutely require light. Calvin cycle is light-independent (occurs in dark too), but it depends on the ATP and NADPH from light reactions. Without light reactions running, the Calvin cycle slows dramatically.

#### Location in the Chloroplast
Thylakoids are stacked into grana. Light reactions happen in thylakoid membranes. The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma, the fluid-filled space around the thylakoids. This compartmentalization keeps the two stages organized and efficient.

#### Review Questions
1. Where do light reactions occur inside the chloroplast?
2. What two energy carriers are produced in light reactions?
3. What does the Calvin cycle use ATP and NADPH for?

### Stomata and Gas Exchange
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** stomata-gas-exchange
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** stomata · guard cells · transpiration · gas exchange
**Summary:** Stomata are specialized pores that allow plants to exchange gases while conserving water.

#### Structure of a Stoma
A stoma is a pore surrounded by two bean-shaped guard cells. When guard cells fill with water (become turgid), they swell and the stoma opens. When they lose water (become flaccid), they collapse and close the stoma. Guard cells have thicker inner walls, so they bow outward when turgid, opening the pore.

#### How Stomata Open and Close
Stomata open during the day when light is available for photosynthesis. Potassium ions (K⁺) accumulate in guard cells, water follows by osmosis, making them turgid and opening the stoma. At night or during drought, guard cells lose water and close, conserving the plant's water supply.

#### Gas Exchange Through Stomata
CO₂ enters through open stomata for photosynthesis. O₂, a byproduct of photosynthesis, exits through the same stomata. This two-way gas exchange is essential for both photosynthesis and cellular respiration in all plant cells.

#### Transpiration and Water Loss
Transpiration is the evaporation of water from plant leaves through stomata. It's essential for transporting water and minerals from roots to leaves, but it also causes significant water loss. Plants lose water vapor passively when stomata are open — it's unavoidable during photosynthesis.

#### Adaptations to Reduce Water Loss
Waxy cuticle reduces cuticular transpiration. Stomata on leaf undersides (not tops) reduce direct sun exposure. Some plants close stomata at midday when temperatures peak. Desert plants have fewer stomata and thicker cuticles to minimize water loss in arid conditions.

#### Review Questions
1. What causes guard cells to open and close a stoma?
2. What two gases are exchanged through stomata?
3. What is transpiration?

### Photosynthesis in Different Light Conditions
**Type:** Application
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** light intensity · wavelength · photosynthetic rate · compensation point
**Summary:** Photosynthesis rates vary with light intensity and quality, and plants have adapted to their native light conditions.

#### Light Intensity and Photosynthetic Rate
As light intensity increases, photosynthetic rate increases up to a saturation point. Beyond this point, other factors (like CO₂ availability or enzyme capacity) limit photosynthesis. Shade plants saturate at lower light intensities than sun plants, reflecting their native environment.

#### The Compensation Point
The light compensation point is the light intensity where photosynthesis equals respiration — no net gas exchange occurs. Below this point, plants lose more CO₂ through respiration than they gain through photosynthesis. Below the compensation point, plants cannot survive long-term without stored energy reserves.

#### Light Wavelength and Absorption
Chlorophyll absorbs blue light (400-450 nm) and red light (600-700 nm) most effectively. Green light (500-600 nm) is reflected, which is why plants are green. Carotenoids (orange-yellow pigments) and anthocyanins (red-purple pigments) absorb green light and enhance overall light capture.

#### Sun Plants vs. Shade Plants
Sun plants (oak, sunflower) have high compensation points and high light saturation points. Leaves are often smaller and thicker to withstand intense light. Shade plants (hosta, fern) have low compensation points and low light saturation points. Leaves are often larger and thinner to capture dim light efficiently.

#### Artificial Light for Growing Plants
Grow lights use LEDs tuned to blue and red wavelengths since those are most efficient for photosynthesis. A mix of blue and red light (50-50 or 30-70 ratio) supports different growth stages. Plants under artificial light can thrive indoors, enabling year-round gardening.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the light compensation point?
2. Why are plants green if green light can be absorbed?
3. How do sun plants differ from shade plants in light requirements?

### Respiration and Energy Use in Plants
**Type:** Application
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** cellular respiration · aerobic respiration · ATP · glucose breakdown
**Summary:** Plants use glucose from photosynthesis as fuel, breaking it down through respiration to release energy.

#### Photosynthesis Makes Glucose; Respiration Uses It
Photosynthesis stores energy in glucose. Respiration releases that energy to make ATP, which powers all plant activities. The simplified respiration equation is the reverse of photosynthesis: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy (ATP).

#### Glycolysis: Breaking Down Glucose
Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm and splits one glucose into two pyruvate molecules, releasing a small amount of ATP and electrons (carried by NADH). This is the first stage, common to both aerobic and anaerobic respiration.

#### The Citric Acid Cycle and Electron Transport
In mitochondria, pyruvate enters the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle), producing more NADH and FADH₂. These electron carriers donate electrons to the electron transport chain, where energy powers ATP synthesis. This is where most ATP is made — one glucose yields about 30-32 ATP.

#### Why Plants Need Respiration
Plants use ATP for growth, cell division, active transport, protein synthesis, and stress response. Roots especially rely on respiration since they cannot photosynthesize in the dark soil. At night, all plant cells rely entirely on stored glucose and respiration to power activities.

#### Respiration Rate and Temperature
Respiration increases with temperature (roughly doubling for every 10°C increase, until proteins denature). In warm climates, plants respire faster, using more glucose. Cool nights favor plant growth by reducing respiration, so stored glucose can accumulate for growth rather than being burned for heat.

#### Review Questions
1. Where does most ATP from respiration get made?
2. Why do plant roots need to respire?
3. How does temperature affect respiration rate?

### Photosynthetic Efficiency and Productivity
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** efficiency · gross vs. net productivity · limiting factors
**Summary:** Not all light energy becomes stored glucose; plants vary widely in photosynthetic efficiency.

#### Gross vs. Net Photosynthetic Productivity
Gross Primary Productivity (GPP) is total glucose made by photosynthesis. Net Primary Productivity (NPP) is GPP minus glucose lost to respiration — the energy available for growth and reproduction. In most plants, respiration uses 30-60% of gross photosynthesis.

#### Factors Limiting Photosynthesis
Light, CO₂, temperature, and water all limit photosynthesis. Whichever is scarcest becomes the limiting factor. In a forest, light is limiting. In a closed greenhouse, CO₂ may be limiting. In the desert, water is limiting. Identifying the limiting factor helps explain why plants grow slowly in certain conditions.

#### C3, C4, and CAM Plants
C3 plants (wheat, rice, beans) fix CO₂ directly into a 3-carbon compound. They are less efficient in hot, dry conditions because photorespiration wastes energy. C4 plants (corn, sugarcane) concentrate CO₂ before fixing it, reducing photorespiration — very efficient in heat. CAM plants (cacti) open stomata at night when it's cool, storing CO₂ to use during the day.

#### Why Efficiency Matters
Agricultural productivity depends on photosynthetic efficiency. Crop yields increase when light, water, CO₂, and temperature are optimized. Genetic engineering aims to increase C3 plant efficiency toward C4 levels, which could boost global food production.

#### Competition for Light in Ecosystems
In forests, tall trees shade understory plants, reducing their available light. This creates a strong selection pressure for shade tolerance (large, thin leaves) or rapid height growth to reach the canopy. Light competition shapes forest structure and biodiversity.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between gross and net photosynthetic productivity?
2. Name one limiting factor for photosynthesis and explain its effect.
3. How do C4 plants differ from C3 plants?

### Photosynthesis and Human Applications
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** biofuels · food crops · oxygen production · carbon cycling
**Summary:** Photosynthesis underpins agriculture, renewable energy, and all terrestrial life.

#### Photosynthesis Powers Agriculture
All crops depend on photosynthesis. Wheat, rice, corn, soybeans, and potatoes are optimized for high photosynthetic productivity. Breeding programs select plants with higher photosynthetic rates. Controlled environment agriculture (greenhouses) manipulates light, CO₂, and temperature to maximize photosynthesis.

#### Biofuels from Photosynthesis
Ethanol from corn and biodiesel from soybeans capture photosynthetic energy. These biofuels are renewable but compete with food crops for land. Algae-based biofuels are under development — algae have very high photosynthetic efficiency and don't compete with food crops.

#### Photosynthesis and Atmospheric Oxygen
All atmospheric O₂ comes from photosynthesis. Without photosynthesis, Earth's atmosphere would be mostly nitrogen and CO₂, with no free oxygen. The oxygen-rich atmosphere supports aerobic life, including all animals.

#### Carbon Cycle and Climate
Photosynthesis removes CO₂ from the atmosphere and stores it in plant biomass and soil. Forests are major carbon sinks — a mature forest sequesters tons of carbon per acre per year. Deforestation reduces carbon uptake, accelerating climate change. Reforestation is a key climate solution.

#### Indoor Farming and Controlled Environments
Vertical farms use artificial lights tuned to red and blue wavelengths for maximum efficiency. Stacking plants in trays increases production per square foot. Hydroponics (growing in nutrient solution rather than soil) eliminates soil diseases and reduces water use. These methods will feed growing urban populations.

#### Review Questions
1. Where does all atmospheric oxygen come from?
2. How do biofuels relate to photosynthesis?
3. How can forests help address climate change?

### Competition for Light in Photosynthesis
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** light-competition-plants
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** competitive advantage · shade tolerance · light capture strategies
**Summary:** Different plants have evolved different strategies to win the competition for light energy.

#### Height as a Competitive Strategy
Tall plants shade shorter plants, capturing more light for their own photosynthesis. This favors evolution of height. In temperate forests, trees reach 80-130 feet. In tropical rainforests, emergent trees exceed 200 feet. But height requires energy and creates structural stress.

#### Leaf Size and Position Strategy
Large leaves capture more light but are mechanically weaker and lose more water. Small leaves are robust but capture less light. Some plants position leaves horizontally to intercept sunlight directly. Others angle them to reduce heat stress at midday.

#### Pigment Diversity and Light Capture
Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red light most. Carotenoids (orange) and xanthophyll (yellow) capture green light. Anthocyanins (red, purple) protect from excess light. Plants with diverse pigments capture more total light. This is why some leaves turn red in fall — anthocyanins protect leaves before they drop.

#### Chloroplast Arrangement in Leaves
In shade plants, chloroplasts are arranged toward the leaf surface to capture dim light. In sun plants, chloroplasts are arranged deeper in cells to avoid photoinhibition (light damage). This subtle adaptation reflects the plant's native environment.

#### Allelopathy: Chemical Competition for Light
Some plants release chemicals that inhibit neighbors' growth, reducing competition. Walnut trees release juglone, killing many plants nearby. This "chemical warfare" is less energetically costly than growing tall. It's a Science Bowl favorite — watch for juglone and allelopathy questions.

#### Review Questions
1. Why do plants evolve to be tall?
2. What do carotenoids do in photosynthesis?
3. What is allelopathy?

## Subtopic: Reproduction & Life Cycles

### Asexual vs. Sexual Reproduction in Plants
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** asexual-sexual-reproduction-plants
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** asexual reproduction · sexual reproduction · genetic diversity · clones
**Summary:** Plants reproduce both asexually (producing identical copies) and sexually (combining genetic material).

#### Asexual Reproduction: No Sex Involved
Asexual reproduction produces offspring genetically identical to the parent (clones). Methods include fragmentation (a piece breaks off and grows), vegetative propagation (runners, bulbs, tubers), and budding. Potatoes, strawberries, and many ferns reproduce asexually.

#### Advantages of Asexual Reproduction
Asexual reproduction is fast — no need to find a mate or wait for pollination. It produces many copies of a successful genotype. Clones are guaranteed to have the parent's traits. This is why farmers propagate valuable crop varieties asexually.

#### Disadvantages of Asexual Reproduction
All clones are identical, so genetic diversity is zero. If a disease or pest evolves to attack the genotype, all clones are equally vulnerable. A monoculture of asexually reproduced plants is a disease waiting to happen — the Irish Potato Famine happened partly because potatoes were asexually reproduced clones.

#### Sexual Reproduction: Combining Genetic Material
Sexual reproduction involves a male gamete (pollen) and female gamete (ovule) fusing to form a zygote. The offspring has genetic material from both parents, making it genetically unique. This genetic recombination creates variation in the offspring.

#### Advantages of Sexual Reproduction
Genetic variation increases the chance that some offspring will survive new diseases, pests, or environmental conditions. Variation is the raw material for evolution. Sexual reproduction is slower but produces more robust, adaptable offspring.

#### Life Cycles: Haploid and Diploid Stages
In plants, both sexual and asexual reproduction can occur in a single life cycle. Flowering plants produce seeds (sexual) but can also propagate vegetatively (asexual). This flexibility is a major reason plants are so successful.

#### Review Questions
1. Why are asexual clones vulnerable to disease?
2. What does genetic recombination mean?
3. Name two methods of asexual plant reproduction.

### The Alternation of Generations in Plants
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** alternation-generations
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** alternation of generations · sporophyte · gametophyte · spores
**Summary:** Plants have two multicellular forms in their life cycle: the sporophyte (diploid, spore-producing) and gametophyte (haploid, gamete-producing).

#### The Gametophyte: Haploid Gamete Producer
The gametophyte is haploid (n) — it has one set of chromosomes. It produces gametes (sperm and eggs) through mitosis. In ferns, the gametophyte is a small, heart-shaped structure called a prothallus, usually just a few millimeters. In flowering plants, the gametophyte is very small — the female gametophyte is the ovule's embryo sac, and the male gametophyte is pollen.

#### The Sporophyte: Diploid Spore Producer
The sporophyte is diploid (2n) — it has two sets of chromosomes. It produces spores through meiosis. Spores are haploid and disperse to new locations. In ferns, the sporophyte is the large fern plant. In flowering plants, the sporophyte is the adult plant.

#### Why This Alternation Evolved
Alternation of generations may have evolved from a strategy where a diploid body was more robust for harsh environments, while a haploid body produced many spores for dispersal. The pattern persists in all plants today, even though the two stages vary dramatically in size and prominence.

#### Ferns Show Clear Alternation
Fern sporophytes are the large plants with fronds. Sori (clusters of sporangia) on frond undersides produce millions of spores. A spore lands on moist soil, germinates, and grows into a tiny gametophyte (prothallus). The prothallus makes sperm and eggs. If moisture allows, sperm swim to an egg, fusing to form a diploid zygote that grows into a sporophyte.

#### Flowering Plants: Dominant Sporophyte
In flowering plants, the sporophyte (the plant) is large and complex. The gametophyte is reduced to a few cells — the female embryo sac in the ovule, and the male pollen grain. This reduction of the gametophyte allowed flowers and seeds to evolve, making flowering plants wildly successful.

#### Review Questions
1. Is a gametophyte haploid or diploid?
2. Where does meiosis occur in the plant life cycle?
3. How do fern gametophytes differ from flowering plant gametophytes?

### Flower Structure and Pollination
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** flower-structure-pollination
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** flower parts · pollination · self-pollination · cross-pollination
**Summary:** Flowers are reproductive structures that facilitate pollination, the transfer of pollen to the pistil.

#### The Four Floral Whorls
Sepals (usually green) protect the flower bud. Petals (often colorful) attract pollinators. Stamens (male parts) produce pollen in anthers; filaments support anthers. The pistil (female part) has a stigma (receives pollen), style, and ovary (contains ovules). Together these four whorls form a complete flower.

#### Anther and Pollen Production
Inside an anther, pollen grains develop. Each pollen grain contains a nucleus — actually two nuclei in flowering plants (called the generative and vegetative nuclei). Pollen is surrounded by a tough, sculpted wall that's different for each species. The male gametophyte is essentially the mature pollen grain.

#### Ovule and Embryo Sac
Inside the ovary, ovules develop. Within each ovule is the female gametophyte, called the embryo sac. The embryo sac typically contains the egg cell, polar nuclei, and other cells. This female gametophyte is microscopic and stays in the ovule throughout its life.

#### Pollination: Moving Pollen
Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma. Self-pollination occurs when pollen from one flower pollinates the same flower. Cross-pollination occurs when pollen from one plant pollinates another. Most plants have mechanisms promoting cross-pollination because it increases genetic diversity.

#### Pollination Vectors: Wind, Water, and Animals
Wind-pollinated plants (grasses, many trees) have small, light pollen and reduced petals. Water-pollinated plants (seagrasses) have pollen that sinks. Animal-pollinated plants (most flowering plants) have showy flowers, sweet nectar, and sticky pollen. Bees, butterflies, hummingbirds, and bats are major animal pollinators.

#### Review Questions
1. What are the four whorls of a flower?
2. What is the difference between self-pollination and cross-pollination?
3. Name three pollination vectors.

### Seed and Fruit Development
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** seed-fruit-development
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** fertilization · seed development · fruit development · dispersal
**Summary:** After pollination and fertilization, seeds and fruits develop to protect and disperse the next generation.

#### Double Fertilization in Flowering Plants
After pollination, pollen grows a pollen tube down the style. One sperm nucleus fuses with the egg, forming a diploid zygote (the embryo). The second sperm nucleus fuses with polar nuclei, forming a triploid nucleus that develops into endosperm (nutritive tissue). This double fertilization is unique to flowering plants.

#### Seed Development
The zygote undergoes mitosis to become an embryo with a radicle (future root), hypocotyl (future stem base), and cotyledons (seed leaves). Endosperm surrounds the embryo and stores starch, proteins, and oils. A seed coat forms from the integuments. The mature seed contains the embryo, endosperm, seed coat, and stored nutrients.

#### Types of Seeds
Monocot seeds (corn, wheat) have one cotyledon; the endosperm surrounds it. Dicot seeds (beans, peas) have two cotyledons that often store nutrients, reducing endosperm. Some seeds have little endosperm and large cotyledons; others have the reverse. Seed structure varies but always protects and nourishes the embryo.

#### Fruit Development
After fertilization, the ovary's walls develop into a fruit. The ovary may become fleshy (apple, berry) or dry (bean pod, acorn). A fruit's function is to protect seeds and aid in dispersal. A single ovary produces a simple fruit; several ovaries in a flower produce an aggregate fruit (raspberry).

#### Seed Dispersal
Seeds must disperse to avoid competing with the parent plant. Wind-dispersed seeds (maple, dandelion) are light and may have wings or parachutes. Water-dispersed seeds float or sink; some are buoyant (coconuts). Animal-dispersed seeds are in tasty fruits — animals eat the fruit and disperse seeds in feces. Explosive fruits (peas) hurl seeds away.

### Germination and Early Growth
**Type:** Application
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** germination · water uptake · radicle emergence · growth
**Summary:** Seed germination is the growth of the embryo into a seedling after conditions become favorable.

#### Conditions for Germination
Seeds need the right temperature, water, oxygen, and light. Most seeds germinate in a narrow temperature range (60-75°F for temperate crops). Water uptake (imbibition) triggers metabolic activity — enzymes activate, respiration increases, and growth resumes. Most seeds need oxygen for aerobic respiration; waterlogged soil may prevent germination.

#### Water Uptake and Activation
A dry seed is metabolically dormant, so it survives for years. When water is absorbed, seeds swell as water enters cells and spaces. Enzymes reactivate, hydrolysis breaks down stored nutrients, and cell division resumes. Within hours to days, the radicle (root) emerges through the seed coat.

#### Radicle and Shoot Emergence
The radicle usually emerges first, so the root establishes before the shoot. Roots grow downward (positive geotropism) toward water and minerals. Shoots grow upward (negative geotropism) toward light. Cotyledons provide nutrients and may photosynthesize once exposed to light.

#### The Seedling Stage
A seedling is a young plant with a root system, stem, and leaves. Seedlings are vulnerable to drying, pests, and shading. They must grow fast to reach light and establish enough root to find water. Seedling survival rate is low — most seeds never become seedlings, and most seedlings don't survive to adulthood.

#### Germination Timing and Dormancy
Seeds germinate at specific times because of photoperiod, cold exposure, or seed-coat breakdown. This prevents germination in fall (after seed production) when the seedling would die in winter. Dormancy ensures germination occurs when conditions favor survival. Example: winter wheat requires a cold period before it will germinate.

1. Why does germination require water?
2. What does "positive geotropism" mean?
3. Why do roots emerge before shoots?

### The Complete Life Cycle of a Flowering Plant
**Type:** Application
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** life cycle · germination to reproduction · phenology
**Summary:** A complete flowering plant life cycle spans from seed germination to flower production and seed dispersal.

#### Seedling Stage (weeks 1-4)
The seedling establishes its root system and grows its first true leaves. Cotyledons provide initial nutrients but fade as the plant photosynthesizes. The seedling is vulnerable and requires consistent moisture, light, and protection from pests. Many seedlings die during this stage.

#### Vegetative Growth (weeks 4 - months)
The plant grows leaves and stems, expanding its above-ground structure and root system. Photosynthesis now provides all energy. In annuals, this stage may last weeks. In perennials, it may last years. The plant accumulates reserves in roots, bulbs, or wood.

#### Flowering Stage (weeks to months depending on species)
Environmental cues (day length, temperature, moisture) trigger flowering. The plant produces flowers to attract pollinators. Flower production is energetically expensive, so plants only flower when reserves are adequate. Annual plants flower once; perennials flower repeatedly.

#### Pollination and Fertilization (hours to weeks)
Pollinators visit flowers, transferring pollen. Pollen tubes grow, sperm reaches the egg, double fertilization occurs. Some plants self-pollinate, but cross-pollination produces more genetic diversity.

#### Seed Development and Dispersal (weeks to months)
Seeds develop inside fruits. Once mature, fruits and seeds are dispersed by wind, water, or animals. Seed dispersal spreads the species, reducing local competition. An annual plant completes its life cycle here. A perennial plant persists and flowers again.

#### In Perennials: The Cycle Repeats
Perennial plants live for years or decades. They produce seeds year after year. Some die after one seed production (monocarpic); others die as they age (senescent). Perennials can also reproduce asexually, spreading through runners, bulbs, or fragmentation.

### Plant Reproduction and Evolution
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** coevolution · adaptation · reproductive success
**Summary:** Plant reproduction has shaped flowers, pollinators, and entire ecosystems through coevolution.

#### Coevolution of Flowers and Pollinators
Flowers and their pollinators have coevolved — each shaped the other. Bright red flowers attract hummingbirds. Sweet-smelling night-blooming flowers attract moths. Orchids have shapes that physically fit specific bees. This specificity ensures efficient pollination but also means some plants depend entirely on one pollinator species.

#### Why Flowers Evolved
Early plants reproduced by spores; flowers evolved to attract animal pollinators. Animals are more efficient than wind or water at moving pollen over long distances. A single insect can pollinate many flowers quickly. Flowers are thus an adaptation for more reliable pollination.

#### Seed Dispersal Coevolution
Fleshy fruits attracted animals, which ate them and dispersed seeds. Over time, fruits became tastier and seeds more hardy. Animals evolved to seek out fruits. Migratory birds carry seeds long distances, allowing plants to colonize new areas.

#### Genetic Diversity and Evolution
Sexual reproduction provides genetic variation, the raw material for evolution. Genetic diversity increases survival when conditions change. Populations with high genetic diversity are more resistant to pests, disease, and climate change. This is why crop scientists preserve wild relatives of domesticated plants — they carry valuable genetic variation.

#### Asexual Reproduction in Agriculture
Many valuable crops are asexually reproduced (potatoes, grapes, apples) because their genotypes are stable and productive. But this creates vulnerability. Plant breeders must breed new varieties with disease resistance and adapt to changing climates. Combining genes from wild relatives through sexual reproduction is essential.

### Plant Reproductive Adaptations for Competition
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** reproductive-timing-strategy
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** reproductive strategy · phenology · allocation · trade-offs
**Summary:** Plants adopt different reproductive strategies — early vs. late flowering, big seeds vs. many seeds — to compete effectively.

#### Synchronous Flowering and Pollinator Attraction
Some plants flower at the same time, creating showy displays. Understory plants time flowering when trees leaf out, before shade becomes too dense. This synchronized timing takes advantage of pollinator availability and reduces competition for pollinator attention. Watch for questions about specific plant communities' flowering times.

#### Semelparous vs. Iteroparous Reproduction
Semelparous plants (like agave and bamboo) flower once then die, investing all reserves in one massive seed crop. Iteroparous plants flower multiple times over their lifetime, spreading reproductive effort. Each strategy has trade-offs — massive seed crops can overwhelm predators, but repeated flowering guarantees some offspring even if one year is poor.

#### Seed Number vs. Seed Size Trade-off
Plants allocate limited resources to seeds — they can make many small seeds or few large seeds. Small seeds disperse far but are vulnerable. Large seeds (nuts, acorns) face high predation but provide seedlings abundant reserves. Oaks produce many acorns in "mast years" to overwhelm squirrels and jays, ensuring some acorns escape predation.

#### Flowering Time and Climate
Early-flowering plants bloom before trees leaf out, capturing spring light. Late-flowering plants avoid frost but compete for pollinators and face shorter growing seasons. Climate change is shifting optimal flowering times — plants that don't adjust lose pollinator synchrony or flower into unseasonable frost.

#### Allelopathy in Reproductive Timing
Some plants chemically inhibit neighbors' growth, then flower when competition is reduced. Others time flowering to minimize competition by blooming when others don't. This temporal partitioning of flowering reduces direct competition for pollinators and allows different species to coexist.

## Subtopic: Plant Responses & Tropisms

### What Are Tropisms?
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** tropisms-definition
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** tropism · directional growth · stimulus · plant hormone
**Summary:** Tropisms are directional growth responses where plants grow toward or away from stimuli like light and gravity.

#### Phototropism: Growth Toward Light
Phototropism is directional growth toward light. Shoots exhibit positive phototropism (grow toward light). Roots exhibit negative phototropism (grow away from light). This makes sense: shoots need light for photosynthesis; roots need darkness to avoid drying.

#### Gravitropism: Growth With or Against Gravity
Gravitropism is directional growth in response to gravity. Roots exhibit positive gravitropism (grow downward). Shoots exhibit negative gravitropism (grow upward). Gravity sensing occurs in special cells (statocytes) containing starch-filled amyloplasts that settle downward.

#### Hydrotropism: Growth Toward Water
Roots grow toward moisture (positive hydrotropism). Sensing likely occurs in root caps where osmoreceptors detect water gradients. Hydrotropism helps roots find soil moisture, critical for water uptake and survival in dry environments.

#### Thigmotropism: Response to Touch
Climbing vines wrap around supports in response to contact (positive thigmotropism). Some plants close leaflets when touched (the Venus flytrap snaps shut). Touch-sensitive plants may detect mechanical stress or chemical signals released by damage.

#### Hyponasty and Epinasty: Unequal Growth
Hyponasty is faster growth on the lower surface of a leaf, causing it to curl upward. Epinasty is faster growth on the upper surface, causing downward curvature. These differential growth rates are controlled by plant hormones and respond to temperature, humidity, and light.

### Plant Hormones and Their Functions
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** plant-hormones
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** auxin · gibberellin · cytokinin · ethylene · abscisic acid
**Summary:** Plant hormones are signaling molecules that coordinate growth, development, and responses to the environment.

#### Auxin: Growth and Tropism
Auxin (mainly indoleacetic acid, IAA) promotes cell elongation in shoots and inhibits growth in roots. It's produced in shoot tips and transported downward. Unidirectional light causes auxin to accumulate on the shaded side, promoting cell elongation there and causing phototropism. Auxin is used commercially as a rooting hormone.

#### Gibberellin: Stem Elongation and Seed Germination
Gibberellins promote stem elongation and break dormancy in seeds. High gibberellin levels trigger germination, growth of new leaves, and flowering. Dwarf pea plants were used by Mendel; some dwarf mutants are deficient in gibberellin. Adding gibberellin makes dwarf plants grow tall — Mendel didn't know this!

#### Cytokinin: Cell Division and Senescence Delay
Cytokinins promote cell division (especially in roots) and delay leaf senescence (aging). A cytokinin-to-auxin ratio determines whether cells divide (high cytokinin) or elongate (high auxin). Cytokinins are produced mainly in roots and transported upward, coordinating root and shoot growth.

#### Ethylene: Fruit Ripening and Stress Response
Ethylene gas promotes fruit ripening, accelerates senescence, and triggers stress responses. Apples and bananas produce ethylene to ripen. Ethylene levels increase when plants are stressed (flooding, disease, drought). High ethylene can cause leaves to drop and flowers to wilt — a protective response to severe stress.

#### Abscisic Acid: Stress and Dormancy
Abscisic acid (ABA) accumulates during drought, promoting stomatal closure to reduce water loss. It also induces dormancy in seeds and buds, preventing germination during unfavorable seasons. ABA levels increase when plants wilt; this triggers root growth to find water. It's sometimes called the "stress hormone."

1. Why does auxin promote phototropism?
2. How do gibberellins affect seed germination?
3. What does ethylene do in ripening fruit?

### Phototropism and Light Response Mechanisms
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** phototropism-mechanism
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** phototropin · signal transduction · auxin redistribution · cell elongation
**Summary:** Phototropism involves light sensing, auxin redistribution, and differential cell elongation in response to unidirectional light.

#### Light Sensing: Phototropin Protein
Phototropin is a blue-light receptor protein in plant cells. When blue light hits phototropin (especially directional blue light from one side), it activates a signal transduction cascade inside the cell. This cascade amplifies the light signal, allowing the plant to sense the direction of the light source.

#### Auxin Redistribution: The Cholodny-Went Hypothesis
When a shoot tip receives directional light, the light-sensing cells asymmetrically pump auxin toward the shaded side. Auxin accumulates on the shaded side and is transported downward in the ensheathing tissues. High auxin concentration on the shaded side promotes cell elongation there, bending the shoot toward the light.

#### Differential Cell Elongation
Cells on the shaded side elongate more than cells on the lit side, causing the shoot to bend toward the light. This bending is a mechanical result of hormone-driven differences in cell elongation. Roots show the opposite — they bend away from light (negative phototropism) because high auxin inhibits root cell elongation.

#### Time Course of Phototropism
Phototropism takes hours to develop. Light sensing occurs quickly (minutes), but cell elongation takes hours. The process is reversible early on — if light direction changes, auxin redistributes and the bend changes direction. The response is elegant: a simple mechanism creates precise directional growth.

#### Evolutionary Advantage
Phototropism is adaptive: shoots reaching light maximize photosynthesis, improving energy capture. The mechanism is so fundamental that all plants show phototropism. It's one of the most studied tropisms, making it a frequent Science Bowl question — know the Cholodny-Went hypothesis by name.

2. How does auxin cause directional bending?
3. Why is phototropism adaptive?

### Gravitropism: The Starch-Statolith Hypothesis
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** gravitropism-mechanism
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** starch · amyloplast · statolith · sedimentation · gravity sensing
**Summary:** Roots sense gravity through specialized cells containing starch-filled plastids that settle downward, triggering auxin redistribution.

#### Statocytes and Amyloplasts
The root cap contains statocytes — specialized cells with large amyloplasts (starch-storing plastids). These amyloplasts are denser than cytoplasm and sediment downward in response to gravity. This sedimentation creates a pressure on the cell's lower surface that's detected by mechanoreceptors.

#### The Starch-Statolith Hypothesis
The starch-statolith hypothesis proposes that amyloplasts settling downward simulate gravity sensing. When a root is horizontal, amyloplasts settle to the lower side, stimulating mechanoreceptors and initiating signal transduction. This signal triggers auxin redistribution, causing the root to bend downward (positive gravitropism).

#### Auxin Redistribution in Roots
When gravity is sensed, auxin redistributes to the lower side of the root. Unlike in shoots (where auxin promotes elongation), high auxin concentrations inhibit root cell elongation. The lower side grows less than the upper side, causing the root to bend downward. This is the opposite of shoot responses — roots are "sensitive" to auxin in the opposite direction.

#### Experimental Evidence
If amyloplasts are prevented from settling (in a rotating clinostat that tumbles the root), gravitropism is lost. Mutations affecting amyloplast formation eliminate gravitropism. Artificial starch grains implanted into root cap cells restore gravitropism in amyloplast-deficient mutants. This evidence strongly supports the starch-statolith hypothesis.

#### Wisdom of the Roots
Positive gravitropism in roots is adaptive: roots must grow downward to reach water and minerals in deeper soil. Downward growth also anchors the plant against wind. This adaptive response is so reliable that it's built into root development — even in the dark, roots grow downward.

### Nastic Movements: Rapid Responses to Stimuli
**Type:** Application
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** nastic movement · nyctinasty · thigmonasty · turgor pressure
**Summary:** Nastic movements are rapid, non-directional responses mediated by turgor pressure changes rather than growth.

#### The Venus Flytrap: Rapid Thigmonastic Response
The Venus flytrap (Dionaea muscipula) snaps its leaves shut in response to touch, trapping insects. This response happens in milliseconds — far too fast for growth-based tropisms. Instead, specialized cells rapidly lose turgor pressure when stimulated, causing the leaf to collapse. This is a thigmonastic (touch-responsive) movement.

#### Mechanism of Rapid Leaf Movement
Touch stimulates ion channels in specialized cells (motor cells) at the leaf's hinge. Ions rush out of the cells, water follows by osmosis, and turgor pressure drops. The leaf collapses inward. After the trigger is removed, ions are pumped back in, turgor pressure is restored, and the leaf opens again.

#### Nyctinasty: Sleep Movements
Legume leaves (beans, clovers) fold upward at night (pulvini movements). This nyctinasty (night movement) might conserve water or protect leaves from nocturnal pests. The mechanism is the same — turgor pressure changes in motor cells at leaf joints. These movements are governed by a circadian rhythm independent of light alone.

#### Stomatal Movements: Turgor-Driven
Stomata open and close through changes in guard cell turgor pressure. When guard cells fill with water (become turgid), they swell and bow outward, opening the stoma. When they lose water (become flaccid), they collapse and close the stoma. This is an extremely rapid and reversible response to light and stress.

#### Adaptive Value of Rapid Responses
Rapid nastic movements allow plants to respond to immediate threats without the slow, growth-based approach of tropisms. The Venus flytrap's snap is an extreme adaptation to carnivory. Stomatal closure within minutes can reduce water loss during sudden drought. These responses show plants are active, dynamic organisms.

### Plant Circadian Rhythms and Photoperiodism
**Type:** Application
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** circadian rhythm · photoperiod · flowering · dormancy
**Summary:** Plants have internal biological clocks (circadian rhythms) synchronized to day-night cycles, controlling development and flowering.

#### Circadian Rhythms: The Internal Clock
Circadian rhythms are roughly 24-hour cycles of behavior/physiology. Leaf movements, stomatal opening, and hormone levels follow circadian rhythms. Even in constant light or darkness, these rhythms persist, driven by an internal "clock." This clock is synchronized (entrained) to environmental light-dark cycles.

#### Photoperiod: Day Length as a Signal
Photoperiod is day length (duration of daylight). Many plants measure photoperiod to time development and flowering. Short-day plants flower when days are short (e.g., fall). Long-day plants flower when days are long (e.g., spring). Day-neutral plants flower regardless of photoperiod. This classification is by behavior, not actual day length.

#### Phytochrome: The Red/Far-Red Light Sensor
Phytochrome is a blue-green pigment that exists in two interconvertible forms: PR (red-absorbing) and PFR (far-red absorbing). When PR absorbs red light (dominant at sunrise), it converts to PFR. At night, PFR slowly reverts to PR (or is rapidly converted by far-red light). The PR/PFR ratio signals the time of day and duration of night.

#### How Plants Measure Night Length
The red/far-red light ratio during twilight doesn't change quickly, but the duration of night (time as PR) changes seasonally. Plants may measure the duration of darkness by the stability/accumulation of PR. Some models suggest plants literally "count" the hours of darkness. However, the exact mechanism remains debated.

#### Flowering and Photoperiodic Responses
Short-day plants need a minimum night length to flower; they "count" long nights (winter/fall). Long-day plants need a minimum day length to flower; they require short nights (spring/summer). This photoperiodic control ensures flowering at ecologically appropriate times. Farmers manipulate light to force flowering year-round (e.g., mums).

1. What is a circadian rhythm?
2. How does phytochrome measure photoperiod?
3. Why do short-day plants flower in fall?

### Stress Responses in Plants
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** stress · hormone signaling · adaptation · trade-offs
**Summary:** Plants respond to drought, heat, cold, and mechanical stress through hormone signaling and physiological changes.

#### Drought Stress and ABA Signaling
Drought increases abscisic acid (ABA) levels. ABA closes stomata, reducing water loss. It also triggers root growth to seek water. High ABA levels cause wilting as a protective mechanism — a wilted plant loses less water. Prolonged drought kills most plants, but survivors quickly recover photosynthesis when water returns.

#### Heat Stress: HSP Production
High temperatures denature proteins and damage chloroplasts. Plants respond by producing heat-shock proteins (HSPs) that refold damaged proteins and provide cytoprotection. Short-term heat increases HSP production, improving heat tolerance. Repeated mild heat stress acclimate plants to survive hotter conditions.

#### Cold Stress and Dormancy Induction
Cold temperatures trigger dormancy in many plants. Short days + cold temperatures = deep dormancy. During dormancy, growth stops and cell sap concentrates, lowering the freezing point. Sugars accumulate as anti-freeze compounds. Hardy plants can survive temperatures far below their freezing point while dormant.

#### Mechanical Stress and Thigmomorphogenesis
Strong winds or physical contact cause plants to reduce height, increase stem thickness, and produce more roots — thigmomorphogenesis. This response involves ethylene signaling. A "windy" plant is shorter and more robust, better surviving strong wind. This response trades growth for strength and stability.

#### Disease and Pathogen-Triggered Responses
When pathogens attack, plants detect them via pattern recognition receptors and produce salicylic acid (SA). SA triggers systemic acquired resistance (SAR) — immune responses spread throughout the plant. Plants can "remember" past pathogens and respond faster to future challenges, similar to immune memory in animals.

### Tropisms and Competition for Resources
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** competitive advantage · plastic responses · phenotypic plasticity
**Summary:** Tropisms allow plants to adjust growth direction and shape, competing effectively for light and water.

#### Shade Avoidance Syndrome
In shade, the red/far-red light ratio shifts (more far-red light penetrates canopy). Phytochrome detects this and triggers shade avoidance: stems elongate rapidly, leaves stay small, and branches above grow outward toward light gaps. This response helps seedlings escape shade, but at the cost of reduced branching and weaker stems.

#### Root Competition in Soil
Roots compete for water and nutrients in soil. When roots encounter localized resources (water, nitrate patches), they proliferate there through differential growth — branches grow more toward the resource. This phenotypic plasticity allows roots to "track" mobile resources without walking.

#### Competitive Interactions: Winners and Losers
Tall plants shade short plants, reducing light. Deep-rooted plants monopolize deep soil water, outcompeting shallow-rooted plants. Allelopathic plants poison competitors with chemical compounds. Plants that respond flexibly (plastic responses) often outcompete inflexible plants in variable environments.

#### Evolutionary Trade-offs
Allocating resources to competition (height, root size) leaves less for reproduction. Plants in low-competition environments often grow short and flower quickly. Plants in high-competition environments grow tall but flower late, after reaching reproductive size. These strategies reflect evolutionary solutions to different competitive regimes.

#### Measuring Competitive Success
Competitive success in plants is measured by reproductive output (seeds produced). A tall, light-capturing plant loses if it doesn't produce seeds before being eaten or falling over. The "winning" strategy depends on the environment's specific challenges. This is why plant diversity persists — different strategies win in different conditions.

### Tropism Questions: High-Yield Science Bowl Topics
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** tropism-bowl-facts
**Estimated time:** 8 min
**Key concepts:** key terminology · specific examples · experimental setup
**Summary:** High-frequency Science Bowl tropism questions test terminology and experimental understanding.

#### The Cholodny-Went Experiment (Classic Bowl Question)
Went isolated auxin from decapitated coleoptile tips and showed it caused bending. This proved that a chemical (auxin) causes phototropism, not a mechanical signal. Key fact: auxin on one side causes bending toward the light source (toward high auxin on the shaded side). Know this experiment by name and result.

#### Phototropism vs. Gravitropism vs. Hydrotropism
Common bowl question: "A root bends downward toward moisture and against light — which tropism is this?" Answer: positive gravitropism (gravity dominates) and negative phototropism, but hydrotropism (toward water) might also be operating. The strongest stimulus usually wins, but multiple tropisms can interact.

#### Starch-Statolith Hypothesis Specifics
Key facts: amyloplasts are heavier than cytoplasm and sediment downward; statocytes are root-cap cells containing amyloplasts; sedimentation applies pressure to mechanoreceptors; this triggers signal transduction and auxin redistribution. The hypothesis predicts that preventing amyloplast settling should eliminate gravitropism (true in experiments).

#### Red/Far-Red Light Ratio in Photoperiodism
Shaded plants under trees receive more far-red light (red light is filtered out by chlorophyll above). Phytochrome PR/PFR ratio shifts, signaling "shade." Plants respond with shade-avoidance syndrome. This explains why forest understory plants are tall and thin — they've evolved to escape shade even at the cost of fragility.

#### Ethylene and Ripening, Abscission, Senescence
Ethylene is the "ripening hormone" — it promotes fruit ripening, flower senescence, and leaf abscission (dropping). High ethylene causes competitive stress responses. This is why rotting fruit "ripens" nearby fruit (ethylene diffuses and triggers ripening in neighbors). Key distinction: gibberellins promote growth; ethylene accelerates maturation and senescence.

1. What did Went prove about phototropism?
2. How do PR and PFR phytochrome forms differ?
3. Why do shaded plants produce tall, thin shoots?
