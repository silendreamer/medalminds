### Introduction to Evolutionary Evidence  
The theory of evolution explains how species change over time through processes such as natural selection and genetic drift. This theory is supported by various lines of evidence that demonstrate the relationships between different organisms and their common ancestry. Understanding these evidences is crucial for grasping the mechanisms of evolution and the history of life on Earth.

### Fossil Record  
The fossil record is a collection of preserved remains or traces of organisms from the past. Fossils provide a chronological history of life, showing how species have changed over millions of years. Transitional fossils, such as those of Archaeopteryx, illustrate the gradual changes between major groups, like reptiles and birds, supporting the idea of common descent.

### Comparative Anatomy  
Comparative anatomy involves studying the similarities and differences in the structures of different organisms. Homologous structures, which are similar due to shared ancestry, provide strong evidence for evolution; for example, the forelimbs of humans, whales, and bats have different functions but share a similar bone structure. Conversely, analogous structures, like the wings of insects and birds, evolved independently and serve similar functions, illustrating how different evolutionary paths can lead to similar adaptations.

### Molecular Biology  
Molecular biology examines the genetic material of organisms, revealing insights into their evolutionary relationships. DNA and protein sequences can be compared across species to determine how closely related they are; the more similar the sequences, the more recent their common ancestor. This molecular evidence complements findings from the fossil record and comparative anatomy, providing a deeper understanding of evolutionary processes.

### Biogeography  
Biogeography studies the distribution of species across different geographical areas. The patterns of where species are found can often be explained by their evolutionary history and the movement of continents. For example, unique species on isolated islands, such as the Galápagos finches, demonstrate how geographical barriers can lead to speciation and diversification.

### Natural Selection  
Natural selection is a key mechanism of evolution, where individuals with advantageous traits are more likely to survive and reproduce. This process leads to the gradual adaptation of populations to their environments over time. Evidence for natural selection can be observed in the peppered moth, which changed color in response to pollution during the Industrial Revolution, demonstrating how environmental changes can drive evolutionary change.

### Review Questions

2. What are homologous structures, and how do they support the theory of evolution?  
3. How does molecular biology contribute to our understanding of evolutionary relationships among species?
