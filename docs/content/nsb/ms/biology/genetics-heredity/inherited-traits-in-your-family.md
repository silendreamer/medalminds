#### Pedigree Charts
A pedigree is a family tree that tracks the inheritance of a trait across generations. Circles represent females; squares represent males. Filled shapes indicate individuals expressing the trait; half-filled indicates carriers. By analyzing a pedigree, you can determine if a trait is dominant or recessive, and whether it is autosomal (on non-sex chromosomes) or sex-linked.

#### Autosomal Traits
Most genetic traits are autosomal — carried on chromosomes 1–22 (autosomes), not the sex chromosomes. Autosomal dominant traits (like Huntington's disease) appear in every generation. Autosomal recessive traits (like cystic fibrosis, PKU) can skip generations because carriers don't show the trait.

#### Sex-Linked Traits
Some traits are carried on the X chromosome. Because males have only one X (XY) while females have two (XX), males are more often affected by X-linked recessive conditions like color blindness and hemophilia. A female carrier (one affected X) typically shows no symptoms but can pass the condition to sons.

#### Common Inherited Traits
Simple autosomal dominant traits include: widow's peak hairline, hitchhiker's thumb, tongue rolling ability (though evidence is mixed). Autosomal recessive: attached earlobes, blue eyes, albinism. Sex-linked recessive: red-green color blindness (~8% of males, ~0.5% of females).

#### Why This Matters for Medicine
Understanding inheritance patterns helps doctors counsel families about genetic disease risk. If both parents are carriers for cystic fibrosis (Cc × Cc), there is a 25% chance each child will have cystic fibrosis (cc) and a 50% chance of being a carrier.

#### Review Questions
1. How do you determine if a trait is dominant or recessive from a pedigree?
2. Why do X-linked recessive conditions affect males more often than females?
3. If both parents are heterozygous for an autosomal recessive trait, what fraction of their children will be affected?
