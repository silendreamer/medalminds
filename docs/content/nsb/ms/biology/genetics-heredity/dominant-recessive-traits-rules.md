#### Gregor Mendel's Discovery
Gregor Mendel (1822–1884), an Austrian monk, discovered the basic rules of inheritance by experimenting with pea plants. He crossed plants with different traits (tall vs. short, purple vs. white flowers) and tracked the results across thousands of plants over years. His results revealed patterns that we now understand as dominant and recessive inheritance.

#### Dominant and Recessive Alleles
Each organism inherits two alleles for most genes — one from each parent. A dominant allele (written as a capital letter, e.g., T) is expressed whenever it is present, even if only one copy is inherited. A recessive allele (lowercase, e.g., t) is only expressed when both copies are recessive (tt). If an organism has one dominant and one recessive allele (Tt), the dominant trait appears.

#### Homozygous vs. Heterozygous
An organism is homozygous dominant (TT) if both alleles are the same dominant form; homozygous recessive (tt) if both are recessive; and heterozygous (Tt) if the two alleles differ. Heterozygous individuals show the dominant phenotype but carry a hidden recessive allele — they are called carriers.

#### Mendel's Law of Dominance
When two organisms that are homozygous for different alleles are crossed, all offspring in the first generation (F1) show the dominant phenotype. The recessive trait "disappears." In the second generation (F2), the recessive trait reappears in about 1/4 of offspring — the classic 3:1 ratio.

#### Why Dominance Happens
At the molecular level, dominant alleles usually produce a functional protein; recessive alleles often produce a non-functional protein or no protein. One working copy is enough — so one dominant allele produces enough functional protein to give the organism the dominant phenotype.
