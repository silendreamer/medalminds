#### The Rarity of Beneficial Mutations
Most mutations are neutral; some are harmful; very few are beneficial. But "rare" doesn't mean "unimportant" — over millions of years and billions of individuals, even rare beneficial mutations accumulate. Natural selection amplifies them: individuals with beneficial mutations have more offspring, spreading the mutation through the population.

#### Sickle Cell Trait: A Double-Edged Mutation
Homozygous sickle cell (ss) is harmful. But heterozygous sickle cell trait (Ss) provides significant protection against malaria. In malaria-endemic regions of sub-Saharan Africa, the sickle allele has been maintained at high frequencies because carriers survive malaria better. This is heterozygote advantage — the heterozygote is fitter than either homozygote.

#### Lactase Persistence
Most humans lose the ability to digest lactose (milk sugar) after infancy because the lactase gene is turned off. However, a mutation upstream of the lactase gene in some human populations prevents this shutdown. This mutation arose approximately 7,000–9,000 years ago in Europe, coinciding with the rise of cattle herding. People with this mutation could digest milk throughout life, providing a significant nutritional advantage.

#### Antibiotic Resistance: Rapid Evolution
Bacteria reproduce so fast (every 20 minutes) that beneficial mutations appear and spread in hours to days. When antibiotics kill most bacteria in an infection, any bacterium with a mutation that protects it survives and reproduces — within days, the resistant strain dominates. This is natural selection in action, visible in real time.

#### Why Evolution Is Not "Goal-Directed"
Mutations are random — they don't arise because the organism "needs" them. Antibiotic resistance bacteria didn't develop resistance because they were exposed to antibiotics; they happened to HAVE the mutation when the antibiotics arrived. Natural selection only selects what exists.

#### Review Questions
1. Why is the sickle cell allele maintained at high frequency in malaria-endemic regions?
2. How does antibiotic resistance in bacteria demonstrate natural selection?
3. What does it mean to say that mutations are "random" — do organisms mutate in response to their environment?
