#### The Predator-Prey Cycle
The classic predator-prey relationship produces oscillating populations. When prey (e.g., snowshoe hares) are abundant, predators (e.g., lynx) have plenty of food and reproduce more. As predator populations increase, they eat more prey. Prey populations decline. With less food, predators decline. With fewer predators, prey recover. The cycle repeats, typically every 8–11 years for the hare-lynx system in Canada's boreal forests.

#### The Lotka-Volterra Model
The *Lotka-Volterra model* mathematically describes predator-prey cycles. It predicts that predator and prey populations will oscillate with the prey peak leading the predator peak by about a quarter cycle. This is exactly what 90 years of Hudson's Bay Company fur trading records showed for hare and lynx populations — a natural, data-rich demonstration of the model.

#### Complications in Real Systems
The hare-lynx cycle is also influenced by hare food sources (hares eat spruce twigs — when spruce is heavily grazed, hare populations decline even without lynx). Real predator-prey systems are embedded in complex webs. Multiple prey species, multiple predators, and fluctuating environments make simple Lotka-Volterra dynamics messier in nature.

#### Top-Down vs. Bottom-Up Control
*Top-down control* occurs when predators regulate prey populations (predator abundance determines prey abundance). *Bottom-up control* occurs when food supply regulates herbivore populations (plant productivity determines herbivore abundance). Most ecosystems show both: predators control prey numbers, but ultimately food supply limits how many prey (and thus how many predators) an ecosystem can support.
