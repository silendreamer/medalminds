#### Producers: Life's Foundation
*Producers* (autotrophs) make their own food using energy from sunlight or chemical reactions. On land, plants are the primary producers. In the ocean, phytoplankton (microscopic algae and cyanobacteria) produce most of Earth's oxygen and support nearly all marine food webs. In deep-sea hydrothermal vents, *chemoautotrophs* (bacteria that use chemical energy from hydrogen sulfide) are the producers — there is no sunlight. Producers form the base of every food chain.

#### Consumers: Energy Movers
*Consumers* (heterotrophs) cannot make their own food and must eat other organisms. *Primary consumers* (herbivores) eat producers: deer eat grass, caterpillars eat leaves, zooplankton eat phytoplankton. *Secondary consumers* eat primary consumers: frogs eat insects, small fish eat zooplankton. *Tertiary consumers* eat secondary consumers: hawks eat frogs, large tuna eat small fish. *Omnivores* eat both producers and consumers (bears, humans, raccoons).

#### Decomposers: Nature's Recyclers
*Decomposers* (primarily bacteria and fungi) break down dead organisms and waste, releasing nutrients back into the soil and water. Without decomposers, nutrients would be locked in dead matter and unavailable to producers. Decomposers are critical to biogeochemical cycles (carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus). Earthworms, millipedes, and some beetles are *detritivores* — they eat dead matter and fragment it, making it easier for true decomposers to break down.

#### Trophic Levels
A *trophic level* is a position in a food chain. Producers = Level 1; primary consumers = Level 2; secondary consumers = Level 3; tertiary consumers = Level 4. Most food chains have 3–5 trophic levels. Each level is less productive than the one below it — energy is lost at each transfer, which limits how many trophic levels are possible.

2. Why would an ecosystem collapse without decomposers?
