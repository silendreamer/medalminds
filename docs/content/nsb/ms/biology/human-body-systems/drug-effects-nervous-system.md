#### How Drugs Work at the Synapse
Most drugs act by interfering with neurotransmitter activity at synapses. They can: (1) Mimic neurotransmitters — bind to receptors and activate them (agonists). (2) Block receptors — prevent the real neurotransmitter from binding (antagonists). (3) Prevent reuptake — keep neurotransmitters in the cleft longer (e.g., SSRIs for depression, cocaine). (4) Increase release — cause excessive neurotransmitter release.

#### Stimulants
Stimulants increase nervous system activity: higher heart rate, alertness, energy. Examples: caffeine (blocks adenosine receptors), cocaine (blocks dopamine/norepinephrine reuptake), amphetamines (increase monoamine release). These drugs cause dopamine to flood reward pathways, producing the sensation of pleasure and driving addiction.

#### Depressants
Depressants slow nervous system activity: reduced heart rate, relaxation, impaired coordination. Examples: alcohol (enhances GABA; inhibitory neurotransmitter), benzodiazepines (anxiety drugs), opioids (mimic endorphins; block pain signals). Overdose of depressants can suppress breathing enough to cause death.

#### Neurotransmitter-Specific Effects
Understanding which neurotransmitter is affected predicts the drug's effect: dopamine → reward/pleasure (addiction pathway); serotonin → mood (antidepressants target this); acetylcholine → muscle activation and memory; GABA → inhibition/relaxation; glutamate → excitation/learning.

#### Addiction
Addiction occurs when drug-induced dopamine surges rewire the brain's reward circuit. The brain reduces its natural reward response (tolerance), requiring more drug for the same effect. Without the drug, dopamine levels crash (withdrawal). Addiction is a disease of brain chemistry, not simply a failure of willpower.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between a drug agonist and an antagonist?
2. How do stimulants and depressants have opposite effects on the nervous system?
3. Why do drugs that flood the brain with dopamine lead to addiction?
