## Subtopic: Circulatory & Respiratory Systems

### The Heart & Blood Vessels: Your Body's Transportation Network
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** heart-blood-vessels-transportation
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** heart chambers · blood vessel types · pulmonary vs. systemic circulation
**Summary:** Learn how the heart pumps blood through two loops of circulation to deliver oxygen and remove waste from every cell in the body.

#### The Heart's Structure
The human heart is a muscular pump divided into four chambers: two atria (upper, receiving chambers) and two ventricles (lower, pumping chambers). The right side handles deoxygenated blood; the left side handles oxygenated blood. A thick muscular wall called the septum separates the left and right sides. Valves between chambers and at the exits of the ventricles prevent backflow — they open and close with each heartbeat, creating the "lub-dub" sound.

#### Two Loops of Circulation
The circulatory system has two circuits: (1) Pulmonary circulation: right ventricle → pulmonary arteries → lungs (where blood picks up O₂ and drops CO₂) → pulmonary veins → left atrium. (2) Systemic circulation: left ventricle → aorta → body tissues (where O₂ is delivered and CO₂ picked up) → vena cava → right atrium. The left ventricle has thicker walls because it pumps to the entire body.

#### Blood Vessel Types
Arteries carry blood away from the heart (usually oxygenated, except pulmonary arteries). They have thick, elastic walls to handle high pressure. Veins carry blood toward the heart (usually deoxygenated, except pulmonary veins). They have thinner walls and one-way valves to prevent backflow. Capillaries are tiny, one-cell-thick vessels where gas and nutrient exchange actually occurs between blood and tissues.

#### Blood Pressure
Blood pressure is the force blood exerts on artery walls. It's measured as systolic (pressure when heart contracts) over diastolic (pressure when heart relaxes), in mmHg — normal is about 120/80. High blood pressure (hypertension) stresses blood vessel walls and increases heart attack and stroke risk.

#### Science Bowl Clue
Arteries vs. veins is a classic bowl confusion point: arteries carry blood AWAY from heart (mnemonic: Artery = Away); veins carry blood toward heart. The exception — pulmonary vessels go the "opposite" direction for oxygenation. Pulmonary ARTERY carries deoxygenated blood; pulmonary VEIN carries oxygenated blood.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the function of each of the four heart chambers?
2. What is the difference between pulmonary and systemic circulation?
3. What are the three types of blood vessels, and what does each do?

### Blood: The Living Fluid
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** blood-living-fluid
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** blood components · red blood cells · white blood cells · platelets
**Summary:** Understand the four components of blood — and how each performs a specific function essential to life.

#### What Blood Is Made Of
Blood is a connective tissue composed of: (1) Plasma — the liquid matrix; a yellow, protein-rich fluid (~55% of blood volume). (2) Red blood cells (erythrocytes) — the most numerous cells; carry oxygen. (3) White blood cells (leukocytes) — much fewer; fight infection. (4) Platelets (thrombocytes) — cell fragments; clot blood to stop bleeding.

#### Red Blood Cells and Hemoglobin
Red blood cells are small, biconcave discs — this shape maximizes surface area for gas exchange. They contain hemoglobin, an iron-containing protein that binds O₂ in the lungs (where O₂ is abundant) and releases it in tissues (where O₂ is scarce). CO₂ is mostly transported dissolved in plasma or as bicarbonate ions. RBCs have no nucleus (in mammals), which maximizes space for hemoglobin. They live about 120 days.

#### White Blood Cells and Immunity
WBCs are the soldiers of the immune system. There are several types: neutrophils engulf and destroy bacteria; lymphocytes include B cells (make antibodies) and T cells (attack infected cells); monocytes become macrophages that engulf debris. WBCs are larger than RBCs and have nuclei.

#### Platelets and Clotting
When a blood vessel is damaged, platelets cluster at the injury site and release clotting factors. These factors trigger a cascade that converts fibrinogen (a plasma protein) to fibrin, forming a meshwork that traps RBCs and creates a clot. This stops blood loss. Disorders of clotting (like hemophilia) can cause uncontrolled bleeding.

#### Review Questions
1. What are the four components of blood, and what does each do?
2. Why are red blood cells biconcave, and what protein do they contain?
3. How do platelets help stop bleeding?

### Gas Exchange: How Your Lungs Work
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** gas-exchange-how-lungs-work
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** alveoli · gas exchange by diffusion · breathing mechanics
**Summary:** Trace the path of oxygen from air into your bloodstream and carbon dioxide from blood into exhaled air.

#### The Respiratory System's Structure
Air enters through the nose/mouth, passes through the pharynx, larynx, and trachea, then branches into bronchi and smaller bronchioles, ending in clusters of tiny air sacs called alveoli. Adults have about 700 million alveoli, giving the lungs a surface area of ~70 m² (about the size of a tennis court). This enormous surface area is critical for efficient gas exchange.

#### Gas Exchange by Diffusion
Gas exchange happens at the alveoli-capillary interface. Oxygen concentration is high in inhaled air and low in deoxygenated blood → O₂ diffuses into the blood. CO₂ concentration is high in blood (from cellular respiration) and low in the alveoli → CO₂ diffuses into the alveoli to be exhaled. This diffusion is passive — no energy is needed; it follows concentration gradients.

#### Breathing Mechanics
Breathing is driven by pressure changes. Inhalation: the diaphragm (dome-shaped muscle below the lungs) contracts and flattens; the rib cage expands; lung volume increases; pressure inside drops; air rushes in. Exhalation: diaphragm relaxes; lung volume decreases; pressure increases; air is pushed out. Breathing rate is controlled by the brainstem based on CO₂ levels in blood.

#### The Role of Surfactant
The alveoli are coated with a thin film called surfactant, which reduces surface tension and prevents the alveoli from collapsing after each exhale. Premature babies often lack surfactant, causing respiratory distress syndrome — one of the reasons premature birth is dangerous.

#### Science Bowl Clue
Questions often ask: "Where does gas exchange occur?" → alveoli. "What drives gas exchange?" → diffusion (concentration gradient). "What muscle controls breathing?" → diaphragm. Know these three answers cold.

#### Review Questions
1. Why do alveoli have such a large surface area?
2. How does oxygen move from the air sacs into the blood — what drives this movement?
3. What role does the diaphragm play in breathing?

### Heart Health: Applying Circulatory Science
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** heart-health-circulatory-science
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** cardiovascular disease · atherosclerosis · lifestyle factors
**Summary:** Apply your knowledge of the circulatory system to understand how cardiovascular disease develops and how lifestyle choices affect heart health.

#### Cardiovascular Disease
Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death worldwide. It includes coronary artery disease (blockage of arteries supplying the heart), heart attack (myocardial infarction — heart muscle dies when its blood supply is cut off), and stroke (blockage or rupture of brain blood vessels). CVD often develops over decades before causing symptoms.

#### Atherosclerosis
Atherosclerosis is the buildup of plaques — fatty deposits, cholesterol, inflammatory cells — inside artery walls. Over time, plaques narrow the artery (reducing blood flow) and make artery walls less elastic. A rupture of a plaque can trigger a clot that completely blocks the artery, causing a heart attack or stroke.

#### Risk Factors
Modifiable risk factors include: high blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, smoking, diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, and unhealthy diet. Non-modifiable factors include: age, sex (males at higher risk earlier), family history, and genetics. Reducing modifiable factors substantially reduces CVD risk.

#### Fitness and the Heart
Regular aerobic exercise strengthens the heart muscle — trained athletes' hearts pump more blood per beat (higher stroke volume), so the heart doesn't need to beat as fast at rest. Exercise also lowers blood pressure, reduces LDL cholesterol, and increases HDL cholesterol (which carries cholesterol away from arteries). A resting heart rate of 40–50 beats per minute in trained athletes (vs. ~70 in sedentary adults) is evidence of cardiac efficiency.

#### Medical Interventions
Treatments include: statins (drugs that lower LDL cholesterol), beta-blockers (slow heart rate), angioplasty (balloon that opens a narrowed artery), and stent placement (wire mesh that keeps the artery open). Coronary bypass surgery routes blood around a blocked artery using a vessel from elsewhere in the body.

#### Review Questions
1. What is atherosclerosis, and how does it lead to heart attacks?
2. List three modifiable risk factors for cardiovascular disease.
3. How does regular aerobic exercise benefit the heart?

### Respiratory Health: From Asthma to Altitude
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** respiratory-health-asthma-altitude
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** asthma · smoking effects · altitude physiology
**Summary:** Apply respiratory biology to understand how diseases and environmental conditions affect lung function and gas exchange.

#### Asthma
Asthma is a chronic condition in which the airways become inflamed and constrict, reducing airflow. During an asthma attack, muscles around the bronchi tighten (bronchoconstriction) and mucus production increases. This makes exhaling especially difficult — air gets trapped. Triggers include allergens (pollen, dust mites), exercise, cold air, and smoke. Inhalers deliver bronchodilators (open airways) or corticosteroids (reduce inflammation) directly to the lungs.

#### Smoking and Lung Damage
Tobacco smoke contains over 7,000 chemicals, including carcinogens and irritants. Chronic smoking: (1) Destroys alveoli, reducing surface area for gas exchange (emphysema). (2) Causes chronic bronchitis — perpetual airway inflammation and mucus overproduction. (3) Paralyzes cilia (hair-like structures that sweep debris out of airways), so mucus and particles accumulate. (4) Dramatically increases risk of lung cancer. Together, emphysema and chronic bronchitis are called COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease).

#### The Role of Cilia
The respiratory tract is lined with cilia and mucus-secreting cells. Cilia beat rhythmically to sweep foreign particles, bacteria, and mucus up toward the throat (the "mucociliary escalator"). Smoking paralyzes and destroys cilia — smokers often cough heavily in the morning as the only way to clear accumulated mucus after cilia recover slightly during sleep.

#### High Altitude Physiology
At high altitude, air pressure is lower, so each breath delivers fewer oxygen molecules. The body compensates by: increasing breathing rate, increasing red blood cell production (via erythropoietin hormone from the kidneys), and increasing capillary density in muscles. Athletes train at altitude to gain these advantages. Severe altitude sickness occurs when these adaptations are overwhelmed: headache, nausea, pulmonary edema (fluid in lungs).

#### Review Questions
1. What happens in the airways during an asthma attack?
2. How does smoking damage the mucociliary escalator?
3. How does the body adapt to living at high altitude?

### Circulatory & Respiratory Systems: Key Review
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** circulatory-respiratory-key-review
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** circulation path · gas exchange · system interactions
**Summary:** A structured review connecting circulatory and respiratory functions — the two systems that work together to deliver oxygen to every cell.

#### The Oxygen Delivery Chain
Inhaled air → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli → O₂ diffuses into capillaries → blood → heart (left side) → aorta → systemic circulation → tissue capillaries → O₂ delivered to cells → CO₂ picked up → veins → heart (right side) → pulmonary arteries → lungs → CO₂ diffuses into alveoli → exhaled. This is the complete loop.

#### Must-Know Numbers
- Heart chambers: 4 (2 atria, 2 ventricles)
- Normal resting heart rate: 60–100 bpm
- Normal blood pressure: ~120/80 mmHg
- Number of alveoli: ~700 million
- Lung surface area: ~70 m²
- RBC lifespan: ~120 days
- Hemoglobin iron: Fe²⁺ binds O₂

#### System Connections
- Circulatory transports the gases that respiratory exchanges
- Respiratory failure leads to circulatory failure (cells die without O₂)
- Exercise increases both heart rate and breathing rate simultaneously
- Anemia (low RBC/hemoglobin) reduces O₂ delivery even if lungs work fine

#### Common Confusions
- Pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood (despite being arteries)
- Veins carry blood toward heart — NOT always deoxygenated (pulmonary veins carry O₂-rich blood)
- Breathing is driven by pressure changes, not suction

#### Review Questions
1. Trace the path of a red blood cell from the right ventricle to a tissue cell in the big toe and back to the heart.
2. What is the structural adaptation that makes alveoli so effective for gas exchange?
3. How do the circulatory and respiratory systems work together during exercise?

## Subtopic: Digestive & Excretory Systems

### How Digestion Works: Breaking Down Food
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** how-digestion-works
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** mechanical vs. chemical digestion · digestive organ sequence · enzymes
**Summary:** Follow a bite of food through the entire digestive system, understanding both mechanical and chemical digestion at each step.

#### Mechanical vs. Chemical Digestion
Digestion is the process of breaking down food into molecules small enough to enter the bloodstream. Mechanical digestion is physical — chewing, churning — that breaks food into smaller pieces without changing its chemical nature. Chemical digestion uses enzymes to break chemical bonds in food molecules (carbohydrates → sugars, proteins → amino acids, fats → fatty acids + glycerol).

#### The Digestive Pathway
Mouth: teeth perform mechanical digestion; salivary amylase begins chemical digestion of starches. Esophagus: muscular tube that moves food by peristalsis (wave-like muscle contractions). Stomach: churning provides mechanical digestion; hydrochloric acid (pH ~2) kills bacteria; pepsin begins protein digestion. Small intestine: primary site of digestion and absorption; pancreatic enzymes digest carbohydrates, proteins, and fats; bile from the gallbladder emulsifies fats; nutrients absorbed into bloodstream through villi. Large intestine: water and electrolytes reabsorbed; remaining waste becomes feces. Rectum/anus: feces eliminated.

#### The Liver and Pancreas
Both are accessory digestive organs (not part of the GI tract directly). The liver produces bile (stored in gallbladder) to emulsify fats — breaking large fat droplets into tiny ones so lipase enzymes can reach them. The pancreas produces a suite of digestive enzymes (amylase, protease, lipase) and secretes sodium bicarbonate to neutralize stomach acid entering the small intestine.

#### Villi: The Absorption Specialists
The small intestine's inner surface is covered with finger-like projections called villi, and each villus is further covered with microvilli (brush border). This arrangement increases surface area enormously — from a smooth tube of ~6 m² to ~300 m² — maximizing nutrient absorption. Each villus contains blood capillaries (for amino acids and sugars) and lacteals (lymph vessels for fatty acids).

#### Science Bowl Clue
"Where does most digestion and absorption occur?" → small intestine. "What emulsifies fats?" → bile (produced by liver, stored in gallbladder). "What enzyme digests starch in the mouth?" → salivary amylase.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between mechanical and chemical digestion? Give an example of each.
2. What is the role of villi in the small intestine?
3. What are the functions of the liver and pancreas in digestion?

### The Excretory System: Filtering Waste
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** excretory-system-filtering-waste
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** kidneys · nephrons · urine formation · homeostasis
**Summary:** Learn how the kidneys filter blood, regulate water balance, and produce urine — maintaining the chemical stability of your body's fluids.

#### What Excretion Is
Excretion is the removal of metabolic waste products — not just feces (which is mostly undigested food, not metabolic waste). True metabolic wastes include urea (from protein metabolism), CO₂ (from cellular respiration), water, and salts. The main excretory organs are: kidneys (urea, water, salts), lungs (CO₂, water), skin (water, salts in sweat).

#### The Kidneys
The two kidneys are bean-shaped organs in the lower back. Each day they filter about 180 liters of blood, producing about 1–2 liters of urine. Kidneys maintain homeostasis by regulating blood pH, electrolyte concentrations, blood pressure, and fluid balance. They also produce erythropoietin (EPO), which stimulates red blood cell production.

#### The Nephron: Functional Unit of the Kidney
Each kidney contains about 1 million nephrons — the filtration units. Process: (1) Filtration — blood pressure pushes water, salts, glucose, and urea from capillaries into the Bowman's capsule. (2) Reabsorption — the tubule reclaims needed substances (glucose, water, useful ions) back into the blood. (3) Secretion — additional wastes are moved from blood into the tubule. (4) Excretion — the remaining concentrated solution (urine) flows into the collecting duct and exits.

#### Urine Composition
Urine is mostly water (~95%), plus urea (~2.5%), creatinine, salts, and some other metabolic waste products. Healthy urine does NOT contain glucose (all glucose is reabsorbed) or protein (too large to filter out). The presence of glucose in urine (glucosuria) is a sign of uncontrolled diabetes.

#### Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH)
ADH is released by the pituitary gland when blood is too concentrated (dehydrated). It signals the kidneys to reabsorb more water, producing concentrated, dark urine. If blood is too dilute (over-hydrated), ADH levels drop and more water is excreted (dilute, light urine). This is a key homeostatic feedback loop.

#### Review Questions
1. What are the three functional steps of urine formation in the nephron?
2. Why is the presence of glucose in urine abnormal?
3. How does ADH regulate water balance?

### Nutrition: Connecting Food to Body Function
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** nutrition-connecting-food-to-function
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** macronutrients · micronutrients · balanced diet
**Summary:** Understand the six classes of nutrients and why each is essential for the body to function properly.

#### The Six Classes of Nutrients
Nutrients are substances from food that the body uses for energy, growth, and repair: (1) Carbohydrates — primary energy source; broken down to glucose. (2) Proteins — building blocks of cells, enzymes, hormones; broken down to amino acids. (3) Fats — long-term energy storage, insulation, and cell membrane structure. (4) Vitamins — organic compounds needed in small amounts for specific functions. (5) Minerals — inorganic ions needed for many processes (calcium for bones, iron in hemoglobin). (6) Water — most essential; needed for virtually every biochemical reaction.

#### Macronutrients vs. Micronutrients
Macronutrients are needed in large amounts and provide calories: carbohydrates (4 cal/g), proteins (4 cal/g), and fats (9 cal/g). Micronutrients (vitamins and minerals) are needed in small amounts and provide no calories, but are essential for enzyme function, bone health, blood clotting, immune function, and more.

#### Essential Nutrients
Essential nutrients are those the body cannot synthesize in adequate amounts — they must come from food. Essential amino acids (9 of the 20) must be obtained from diet; the body makes the other 11. Essential fatty acids (like omega-3s) cannot be made by the body. Most vitamins must come from food (exception: vitamin D is made with sunlight exposure).

#### Vitamin and Mineral Examples
Vitamin C: collagen synthesis, antioxidant (deficiency = scurvy). Vitamin D: calcium absorption, bone development (deficiency = rickets). Iron: hemoglobin and myoglobin production (deficiency = anemia). Calcium: bone and tooth structure, muscle contraction, nerve function. Potassium: nerve transmission and heart rhythm.

#### Science Bowl Clue
Bowl questions often give a deficiency symptom and ask for the nutrient. Scurvy = vitamin C deficiency. Rickets = vitamin D deficiency. Night blindness = vitamin A deficiency. Anemia (if dietary) = iron deficiency. These are high-yield, predictable connections.

#### Review Questions
1. What are the six classes of nutrients and which provide calories?
2. What disease results from vitamin C deficiency, and what does vitamin C do in the body?
3. Why are some amino acids called "essential"?

### Digestive Disorders: Applying GI Science
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** digestive-disorders-applying-gi-science
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** ulcers · Crohn's disease · liver function · diabetes
**Summary:** Connect digestive biology to real medical conditions, understanding how disruptions in the GI system lead to disease.

#### Peptic Ulcers
Peptic ulcers are sores in the stomach lining or upper small intestine caused by damage from stomach acid. The main cause (discovered in 1983 by Barry Marshall and Robin Warren, who won the Nobel Prize for it) is the bacterium H. pylori, which disrupts the protective mucus layer. NSAID overuse is another cause. Treatment includes antibiotics (to kill H. pylori) and proton pump inhibitors (to reduce acid production). Marshall famously drank a flask of H. pylori to prove the bacterial cause.

#### Inflammatory Bowel Diseases
Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis (IBD) are chronic inflammatory conditions of the GI tract caused by an overactive immune response. Crohn's can affect any part of the GI tract (patchy lesions, through all layers of bowel wall); ulcerative colitis affects the colon (continuous lesions, inner lining only). Both cause chronic pain, diarrhea, and inflammation.

#### Liver Disease
The liver is essential for detoxification, protein synthesis (including clotting factors), bile production, and metabolism. Cirrhosis is scarring of the liver from chronic damage (alcoholism, hepatitis B/C). In cirrhosis, healthy liver tissue is replaced by scar tissue, impairing all liver functions. Jaundice (yellow skin/eyes) results when the damaged liver cannot process bilirubin (a breakdown product of hemoglobin).

#### Type 1 vs. Type 2 Diabetes
Both types affect blood glucose regulation. In Type 1 diabetes, the immune system destroys insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas — the body makes no insulin. In Type 2, cells become resistant to insulin's signals. Without effective insulin signaling, glucose cannot enter cells. Blood glucose rises (hyperglycemia). Excess glucose spills into urine (glucosuria), which is detectable.

#### Review Questions
1. What bacterium causes most peptic ulcers, and how was this discovered?
2. What is jaundice, and what does it indicate about liver function?
3. How does diabetes affect the digestive/excretory connection (hint: glucosuria)?

### Kidney Disease and Dialysis: Excretory Medicine
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** kidney-disease-and-dialysis
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** kidney failure · dialysis · homeostasis disruption
**Summary:** Apply nephron physiology to understand kidney failure, how dialysis artificially replaces kidney function, and the consequences of losing excretory homeostasis.

#### Chronic Kidney Disease
The kidneys can be damaged by diabetes, high blood pressure, infection, or genetic conditions. As nephrons are destroyed, fewer remain to filter blood. Waste products (urea, creatinine) accumulate in blood — a condition called uremia. Without treatment, uremia is fatal.

#### Dialysis: Artificial Filtration
Hemodialysis uses a machine to filter blood outside the body. Blood is drawn from a vein, filtered through a dialysis membrane (which allows small molecules like urea to pass but not large proteins or blood cells), and returned to the body. Patients typically undergo dialysis 3× per week for 3–5 hours per session. Peritoneal dialysis uses the body's own peritoneum (abdominal lining) as the filtering membrane.

#### Kidney Transplant
A kidney transplant replaces one failed kidney with a healthy donor kidney. The recipient keeps their original (non-functional) kidneys and the donor kidney is placed in the lower abdomen. Anti-rejection medications must be taken for life to prevent the immune system from attacking the donor kidney. One healthy kidney is sufficient — humans can live with just one.

#### Urinalysis as Diagnostic Tool
Urinalysis — analyzing urine composition — can reveal many medical conditions: glucose = diabetes, protein = kidney damage, blood = infection or kidney stones, bacteria = urinary tract infection, and bilirubin = liver disease. This is why a urine sample is collected at medical appointments.

#### Review Questions
1. What is uremia, and what causes it?
2. How does hemodialysis mimic kidney function?
3. Why can humans survive with only one kidney?

## Subtopic: Nervous & Endocrine Systems

### How the Nervous System Works
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** nervous-system-how-it-works
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** neurons · action potential · CNS vs. PNS
**Summary:** Understand how neurons transmit electrical signals at lightning speed, allowing you to react, sense, think, and control every body movement.

#### Organization of the Nervous System
The nervous system has two main divisions: the central nervous system (CNS) — brain and spinal cord — processes information and commands. The peripheral nervous system (PNS) — all nerves outside the CNS — carries information to and from the CNS. The PNS is further divided into the somatic nervous system (voluntary control of skeletal muscles) and autonomic nervous system (involuntary control of heart, digestion, glands).

#### The Neuron
Neurons are the functional cells of the nervous system. Parts: (1) Cell body (soma) — contains nucleus and metabolic machinery. (2) Dendrites — branching extensions that receive signals from other neurons or sensory cells. (3) Axon — long projection that carries the signal away from the cell body. (4) Myelin sheath — fatty insulation that speeds signal conduction. (5) Axon terminals (synaptic knobs) — where signals pass to the next neuron.

#### The Action Potential
Neurons communicate using electrical signals called action potentials. At rest, the inside of a neuron is negatively charged (~-70 mV) relative to the outside — the resting membrane potential. When stimulated above threshold, ion channels open: Na⁺ rushes in (depolarization), then K⁺ rushes out (repolarization). This wave of charge reversal travels down the axon at speeds up to 120 m/s. The signal is all-or-nothing: either it fires fully or not at all.

#### The Synapse
At the synapse, the action potential reaches the axon terminal and triggers release of neurotransmitters — chemical messengers — into the synaptic cleft. Neurotransmitters bind to receptors on the dendrite of the next neuron, triggering a new signal if the combined input exceeds threshold. Common neurotransmitters: acetylcholine (muscle contraction, learning), dopamine (reward, movement), serotonin (mood), GABA (inhibitory).

#### Reflex Arcs
A reflex is an automatic, rapid response that bypasses the brain. Sensory neuron → interneuron (in spinal cord) → motor neuron → muscle. The knee-jerk reflex and pulling your hand from a flame are examples. The brain is notified after the reflex already happened.

### The Endocrine System: Chemical Messengers
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** endocrine-system-chemical-messengers
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** hormones · glands · feedback loops · comparison to nervous system
**Summary:** Learn how hormones control long-term body functions — from growth and metabolism to reproduction and stress response.

#### What the Endocrine System Does
The endocrine system uses hormones — chemical signals released into the bloodstream — to regulate body functions. Unlike the nervous system (which sends fast, targeted electrical signals), the endocrine system sends slower, body-wide chemical messages with longer-lasting effects. Hormones travel through blood and act on specific target cells that have receptors for that hormone.

#### Major Glands and Their Hormones
- Hypothalamus: master control; links nervous and endocrine systems; controls pituitary
- Pituitary gland: "master gland"; produces growth hormone (GH), antidiuretic hormone (ADH), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and others
- Thyroid: thyroxine controls metabolic rate and growth
- Pancreas: insulin (lowers blood glucose) and glucagon (raises blood glucose)
- Adrenal glands: epinephrine (adrenaline) — fight-or-flight response; cortisol — stress response
- Gonads (ovaries/testes): estrogen, progesterone, testosterone — reproductive development
- Parathyroid: parathyroid hormone (PTH) — regulates blood calcium

#### Negative Feedback
Most hormone regulation uses negative feedback loops: when hormone levels rise, signals are sent to reduce production; when levels fall, production increases. Example: low thyroid hormone → hypothalamus releases TRH → pituitary releases TSH → thyroid produces more thyroxine → high thyroxine inhibits hypothalamus and pituitary. This is analogous to a thermostat.

#### Nervous vs. Endocrine
Nervous: fast, electrical, short-lived, targeted (specific cells). Endocrine: slow, chemical, long-lasting, widespread (all cells with receptors). Many processes use both — stress triggers both the nervous system (adrenaline rush) and endocrine system (cortisol release).

### Brain Regions and Their Functions
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** brain-regions-and-functions
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** cerebrum · cerebellum · brainstem · limbic system
**Summary:** Map the major brain regions to their specific functions — understanding how a 3-pound organ controls personality, movement, memory, and breathing simultaneously.

#### The Cerebrum
The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain, divided into two hemispheres (left and right) connected by the corpus callosum. It's further divided into four lobes: Frontal lobe: personality, decision-making, voluntary movement, speech production (Broca's area). Parietal lobe: sensory processing (touch, pain, temperature, taste). Temporal lobe: hearing, language comprehension (Wernicke's area), memory. Occipital lobe: vision processing. The outer layer (cortex) is highly folded (gyri and sulci) to maximize surface area.

#### The Cerebellum
Located at the rear of the brain, the cerebellum coordinates voluntary movement, balance, and fine motor control. It receives information from the motor cortex and sensory systems and adjusts movements to be smooth and accurate. Damage causes ataxia — loss of coordination and balance.

#### The Brainstem
The brainstem (medulla oblongata, pons, midbrain) controls automatic vital functions: breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, swallowing, and wake/sleep cycles. The medulla contains the respiratory and cardiac control centers. Brain death is defined by complete, irreversible loss of brainstem function.

#### The Limbic System
The limbic system includes the hippocampus (memory formation), amygdala (emotion, fear response), and hypothalamus (homeostasis, link between nervous and endocrine systems). The "emotional brain" — it processes emotional responses and stores emotional memories.

### Drug Effects on the Nervous System
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** drug-effects-nervous-system
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** stimulants · depressants · neurotransmitter disruption · addiction
**Summary:** Apply your knowledge of synapses and neurotransmitters to understand how different drugs alter nervous system function.

#### How Drugs Work at the Synapse
Most drugs act by interfering with neurotransmitter activity at synapses. They can: (1) Mimic neurotransmitters — bind to receptors and activate them (agonists). (2) Block receptors — prevent the real neurotransmitter from binding (antagonists). (3) Prevent reuptake — keep neurotransmitters in the cleft longer (e.g., SSRIs for depression, cocaine). (4) Increase release — cause excessive neurotransmitter release.

#### Stimulants
Stimulants increase nervous system activity: higher heart rate, alertness, energy. Examples: caffeine (blocks adenosine receptors), cocaine (blocks dopamine/norepinephrine reuptake), amphetamines (increase monoamine release). These drugs cause dopamine to flood reward pathways, producing the sensation of pleasure and driving addiction.

#### Depressants
Depressants slow nervous system activity: reduced heart rate, relaxation, impaired coordination. Examples: alcohol (enhances GABA; inhibitory neurotransmitter), benzodiazepines (anxiety drugs), opioids (mimic endorphins; block pain signals). Overdose of depressants can suppress breathing enough to cause death.

#### Neurotransmitter-Specific Effects
Understanding which neurotransmitter is affected predicts the drug's effect: dopamine → reward/pleasure (addiction pathway); serotonin → mood (antidepressants target this); acetylcholine → muscle activation and memory; GABA → inhibition/relaxation; glutamate → excitation/learning.

#### Addiction
Addiction occurs when drug-induced dopamine surges rewire the brain's reward circuit. The brain reduces its natural reward response (tolerance), requiring more drug for the same effect. Without the drug, dopamine levels crash (withdrawal). Addiction is a disease of brain chemistry, not simply a failure of willpower.

1. What is the difference between a drug agonist and an antagonist?
2. How do stimulants and depressants have opposite effects on the nervous system?
3. Why do drugs that flood the brain with dopamine lead to addiction?

### Hormones in Action: Stress, Growth, and Reproduction
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** hormones-stress-growth-reproduction
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** epinephrine · growth hormone · puberty hormones · feedback
**Summary:** Apply endocrine physiology to understand how your body responds to stress, grows during childhood, and undergoes puberty.

#### The Stress Response
When you perceive danger, the hypothalamus activates two parallel systems: (1) Nervous: activates adrenal medulla → epinephrine (adrenaline) released → heart rate up, blood pressure up, blood redirected to muscles, glucose released into blood, digestion paused. This is the fight-or-flight response, acting in seconds. (2) Endocrine: hypothalamus → ACTH → adrenal cortex → cortisol released (stress hormone). Cortisol maintains elevated blood glucose and suppresses immune response. Chronic stress keeps cortisol high, which is harmful long-term.

#### Growth Hormone
Growth hormone (GH) is released by the pituitary gland, primarily during sleep. It stimulates growth of bones and muscles, protein synthesis, and cell division. Too much GH in childhood = gigantism; too little = growth hormone deficiency (treated with GH injections). Too much GH in adulthood (acromegaly) causes bones of the face, hands, and feet to enlarge (skull sutures already fused, so height doesn't increase).

#### Puberty Hormones
At puberty, the hypothalamus increases release of GnRH → pituitary releases FSH and LH → gonads increase production of sex hormones. In females: estrogen causes breast development, fat redistribution, and triggers the menstrual cycle. In males: testosterone causes muscle growth, voice deepening, facial hair, and sperm production. Both sexes experience growth spurts from increased GH and sex hormone interaction.

#### Insulin and Blood Sugar
After a carbohydrate-rich meal, blood glucose rises → pancreas releases insulin → cells take up glucose (muscle, liver, fat) → blood glucose returns to normal. Between meals, blood glucose falls → pancreas releases glucagon → liver breaks down glycogen and releases glucose. This tight regulation keeps blood glucose between ~70–100 mg/dL. Diabetes disrupts this system.

## Subtopic: Musculoskeletal System

### Bones: Structure, Function, and Growth
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** bones-structure-function-growth
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** bone composition · bone functions · growth plates
**Summary:** Understand what bones are actually made of, why they're strong yet lightweight, and how they grow throughout childhood.

#### What Bones Are Made Of
Bone is a living tissue, not dead rock. Bone matrix is ~70% mineral (mainly calcium phosphate crystals — hydroxyapatite) and ~30% organic material (mainly collagen fibers). The mineral gives hardness and compression strength; the collagen gives flexibility and resistance to fracture. This combination makes bone stronger than concrete per unit weight. If you removed all the mineral, the bone would be rubbery (bend without breaking). Remove the collagen, and it would be brittle (shatter easily).

#### Bone Structure
The outer layer, compact bone, is dense and strong — composed of Haversian systems (osteons), concentric rings of mineralized matrix with blood vessels through the center. The inner layer, spongy (cancellous) bone, has a lattice structure that reduces weight while maintaining strength. The center of long bones contains the medullary cavity, filled with yellow bone marrow (fat storage). Red bone marrow (in spongy bone of flat bones — skull, sternum, vertebrae, pelvis) produces blood cells.

#### Functions of the Skeleton
The skeleton: (1) Supports the body against gravity. (2) Protects organs (skull → brain, ribcage → heart/lungs). (3) Enables movement via joints and muscle attachment. (4) Produces blood cells (red and white blood cells and platelets in red marrow). (5) Stores minerals (calcium and phosphorus) that can be released into blood.

#### Bone Growth
Long bones grow at epiphyseal (growth) plates — cartilage-rich zones near the ends of bones. Hormones (growth hormone, thyroid hormone, sex hormones) stimulate chondrocytes to divide; new cartilage is then replaced by bone (ossification). Growth plates close when sex hormones surge at puberty — after closure, height cannot increase. Bone density continues to increase until the mid-30s.

### Muscles: How Contraction Works
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** muscles-how-contraction-works
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** muscle fiber types · sarcomere · sliding filament theory
**Summary:** Learn how muscle cells convert ATP into mechanical force using the sliding filament theory — the molecular mechanism behind every movement you make.

#### Three Types of Muscle
Skeletal muscle: attached to bones by tendons; voluntary control; striated (striped); can fatigue. Cardiac muscle: forms the heart wall; involuntary; striated; does not fatigue under normal conditions. Smooth muscle: found in hollow organs (stomach, blood vessels, intestines); involuntary; not striated; sustained contractions.

#### The Sarcomere
Skeletal muscle cells (fibers) contain thousands of myofibrils. Each myofibril consists of repeating units called sarcomeres — the basic contractile units. Each sarcomere contains two types of protein filaments: thick filaments (myosin) and thin filaments (actin). The interdigitation of actin and myosin creates the striated (banded) appearance.

#### The Sliding Filament Theory
Contraction occurs when actin and myosin slide past each other, shortening the sarcomere. The mechanism: (1) A nerve signal reaches the muscle via a motor neuron. (2) Acetylcholine is released at the neuromuscular junction. (3) Ca²⁺ is released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum inside the muscle fiber. (4) Ca²⁺ causes troponin/tropomyosin to move, exposing binding sites on actin. (5) Myosin heads attach to actin (cross-bridges) and pull (power stroke), using ATP. (6) ATP breaks the cross-bridge and resets the myosin head. This cycle repeats rapidly.

#### ATP and Muscle Energy
Muscle contraction requires ATP. ATP is produced by: (1) Phosphocreatine (for ~10 seconds of maximum effort). (2) Anaerobic glycolysis (lactic acid pathway — ~2 minutes). (3) Aerobic cellular respiration (long-duration activity). During intense exercise without sufficient oxygen, lactic acid accumulates, causing the burning sensation in muscles.

### Joints and the Skeleton in Motion
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** joints-and-skeleton-in-motion
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** joint types · ligaments vs. tendons · joint injury
**Summary:** Understand how different joint types allow movement, and what connective tissues hold joints together.

#### Types of Joints
Joints are where two or more bones meet. Types by mobility: (1) Immovable (fibrous) joints: skull sutures — bone ends connected by fibrous tissue, no movement. (2) Slightly movable (cartilaginous) joints: vertebrae connected by intervertebral discs of fibrocartilage — limited cushioned movement. (3) Freely movable (synovial) joints: most joints; surrounded by a joint capsule filled with synovial fluid (lubricant). Subtypes: ball-and-socket (hip, shoulder — full rotation), hinge (knee, elbow — one plane), pivot (atlas-axis — rotation), saddle (thumb), and gliding (wrist).

#### Ligaments and Tendons
Ligaments connect bone to bone at joints — provide stability and limit excess movement. Tendons connect muscle to bone — transmit the force of muscle contraction to the skeleton, causing movement. Both are made primarily of collagen fibers. A sprain is a ligament injury; a strain is a muscle or tendon injury.

#### Cartilage
Cartilage is smooth, rubbery connective tissue that covers bone ends in joints (articular cartilage), cushioning and reducing friction. It lacks blood vessels (avascular), making it slow to heal when damaged. Osteoarthritis occurs when cartilage wears away, leading to bone-on-bone contact, pain, and inflammation.

#### Synovial Fluid
Synovial fluid fills the joint cavity, lubricates movement, and nourishes cartilage (which has no blood supply). Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks synovial membranes, causing chronic inflammation and joint damage.

### Exercise Science: Applying Musculoskeletal Biology
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** exercise-science-musculoskeletal
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** strength training · endurance training · injury recovery
**Summary:** Apply musculoskeletal biology to understand how exercise makes you stronger, what happens during injury and recovery, and how different types of training have different physiological effects.

#### How Strength Training Works
When you lift heavy weights, you create microscopic tears in muscle fibers. In the recovery period (24–48 hours), satellite cells (muscle stem cells) repair and reinforce these tears, adding contractile proteins (actin and myosin). Over time, muscle fibers grow larger (hypertrophy) and stronger. This is why rest days are essential — muscle growth happens during recovery, not during exercise.

#### Fast-Twitch vs. Slow-Twitch Muscle Fibers
Type I (slow-twitch) fibers: high mitochondria density, fatigue-resistant, use aerobic metabolism, smaller force output. Good for endurance (marathon running). Type II (fast-twitch) fibers: fewer mitochondria, fatigue quickly, anaerobic metabolism, large force output. Good for sprinting and power lifting. Most people have roughly 50/50 mix; elite endurance athletes have ~70% Type I; elite sprinters have ~70% Type II (partly genetic).

#### Aerobic vs. Anaerobic Exercise
Aerobic exercise (running, cycling, swimming) uses oxygen to produce ATP via cellular respiration. It improves cardiovascular efficiency, increases mitochondrial density in muscle, and enhances oxygen delivery. Anaerobic exercise (sprinting, heavy lifting) exceeds oxygen supply; muscles use glycolysis, producing lactic acid. It builds strength and muscle mass but cannot be sustained long.

#### Bone Density and Exercise
Weight-bearing exercise (running, weightlifting, even walking) stimulates bone remodeling — osteoblasts add new bone matrix, increasing density. Astronauts who float in microgravity lose significant bone density because bones aren't bearing weight. Calcium intake and vitamin D are essential for bone mineralization to keep pace with bone breakdown.

### Sports Injuries: Understanding Musculoskeletal Damage
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** sports-injuries-musculoskeletal
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** fractures · sprains vs. strains · RICE protocol · overuse injuries
**Summary:** Apply anatomy and physiology to understand the most common sports injuries and how the body heals.

#### Fractures
A fracture is a break or crack in bone. Types: simple (bone breaks but doesn't pierce skin), compound/open (bone breaks through skin — infection risk), stress fracture (small crack from repeated impact — overuse), comminuted (shattered into multiple pieces). Bone heals through: hematoma formation → callus (soft then hard cartilage) → ossification (mineral deposition) → remodeling (reshaping to final form). Process takes weeks to months.

#### Sprains and Strains
A sprain is a ligament tear (occurs at joints — ankle, knee). A strain is a muscle or tendon tear. Both involve micro-tears of collagen fibers. Grade I: mild stretch/micro-tear. Grade II: partial tear. Grade III: complete rupture (may require surgery). The RICE protocol: Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation — reduces swelling and pain in the first 48–72 hours.

#### Overuse Injuries
Overuse injuries result from repetitive stress without adequate recovery. Tendinitis = inflammation of tendons (tennis elbow, patellar tendinitis). Shin splints = pain in the tibia from overloading. Stress fractures in long-distance runners. These respond to rest, cross-training, and gradual return to activity.

#### ACL and Meniscus Injuries
The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is a major stabilizing ligament of the knee. ACL tears are common in cutting sports (soccer, basketball). The meniscus is cartilage padding inside the knee joint. Both injuries often require surgery and months of rehabilitation. The knee is the most commonly injured joint in sports.
