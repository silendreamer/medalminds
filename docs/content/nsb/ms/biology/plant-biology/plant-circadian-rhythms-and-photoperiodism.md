#### Circadian Rhythms: The Internal Clock
Circadian rhythms are roughly 24-hour cycles of behavior/physiology. Leaf movements, stomatal opening, and hormone levels follow circadian rhythms. Even in constant light or darkness, these rhythms persist, driven by an internal "clock." This clock is synchronized (entrained) to environmental light-dark cycles.

#### Photoperiod: Day Length as a Signal
Photoperiod is day length (duration of daylight). Many plants measure photoperiod to time development and flowering. Short-day plants flower when days are short (e.g., fall). Long-day plants flower when days are long (e.g., spring). Day-neutral plants flower regardless of photoperiod. This classification is by behavior, not actual day length.

#### Phytochrome: The Red/Far-Red Light Sensor
Phytochrome is a blue-green pigment that exists in two interconvertible forms: PR (red-absorbing) and PFR (far-red absorbing). When PR absorbs red light (dominant at sunrise), it converts to PFR. At night, PFR slowly reverts to PR (or is rapidly converted by far-red light). The PR/PFR ratio signals the time of day and duration of night.

#### How Plants Measure Night Length
The red/far-red light ratio during twilight doesn't change quickly, but the duration of night (time as PR) changes seasonally. Plants may measure the duration of darkness by the stability/accumulation of PR. Some models suggest plants literally "count" the hours of darkness. However, the exact mechanism remains debated.

#### Flowering and Photoperiodic Responses
Short-day plants need a minimum night length to flower; they "count" long nights (winter/fall). Long-day plants need a minimum day length to flower; they require short nights (spring/summer). This photoperiodic control ensures flowering at ecologically appropriate times. Farmers manipulate light to force flowering year-round (e.g., mums).
