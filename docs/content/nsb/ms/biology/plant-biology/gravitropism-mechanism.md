#### Statocytes and Amyloplasts
The root cap contains statocytes — specialized cells with large amyloplasts (starch-storing plastids). These amyloplasts are denser than cytoplasm and sediment downward in response to gravity. This sedimentation creates a pressure on the cell's lower surface that's detected by mechanoreceptors.

#### The Starch-Statolith Hypothesis
The starch-statolith hypothesis proposes that amyloplasts settling downward simulate gravity sensing. When a root is horizontal, amyloplasts settle to the lower side, stimulating mechanoreceptors and initiating signal transduction. This signal triggers auxin redistribution, causing the root to bend downward (positive gravitropism).

#### Auxin Redistribution in Roots
When gravity is sensed, auxin redistributes to the lower side of the root. Unlike in shoots (where auxin promotes elongation), high auxin concentrations inhibit root cell elongation. The lower side grows less than the upper side, causing the root to bend downward. This is the opposite of shoot responses — roots are "sensitive" to auxin in the opposite direction.

#### Experimental Evidence
If amyloplasts are prevented from settling (in a rotating clinostat that tumbles the root), gravitropism is lost. Mutations affecting amyloplast formation eliminate gravitropism. Artificial starch grains implanted into root cap cells restore gravitropism in amyloplast-deficient mutants. This evidence strongly supports the starch-statolith hypothesis.

#### Wisdom of the Roots
Positive gravitropism in roots is adaptive: roots must grow downward to reach water and minerals in deeper soil. Downward growth also anchors the plant against wind. This adaptive response is so reliable that it's built into root development — even in the dark, roots grow downward.
