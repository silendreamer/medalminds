#### The Classification System

All living things are organized into a system of **classification** that groups organisms by shared traits. The broadest groups are called **domains**: Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya. Within each domain are smaller groups called **kingdoms**. This hierarchical organization helps scientists understand the relationships between all life and how organisms evolved over time. Using this system, we can compare organisms and predict their traits based on which kingdom they belong to.

#### The Kingdom Level

There are typically six recognized kingdoms of life. **Bacteria** (domain Bacteria) are single-celled organisms with no nucleus. **Archaea** (domain Archaea) are also single-celled but thrive in extreme environments and differ genetically from bacteria. **Protists** are mostly single-celled eukaryotes—some are plant-like (like algae), some are animal-like (like amoebas), and some are fungus-like. **Fungi** are eukaryotes that absorb nutrients by breaking down dead organisms; they include mushrooms and molds. **Plants** are multicellular eukaryotes that make their own food using sunlight. **Animals** are multicellular eukaryotes that must eat to survive.

#### Distinguishing Features of Kingdoms

Each kingdom has key **distinguishing features** that set it apart. **Cell structure** is crucial: bacteria and archaea lack a nucleus and are prokaryotes, while protists, fungi, plants, and animals have a nucleus and are eukaryotes. **How organisms get energy** matters too—plants photosynthesize using sunlight, fungi absorb nutrients from their environment, and animals consume other organisms. **Multicellularity** separates kingdoms as well; most bacteria and archaea are single-celled, while most plants and animals are multicellular.

#### Comparing Within Kingdoms

Within kingdoms, there is still great diversity. Some fungi are helpful (like yeast used in bread), while others cause disease. Some animals are tiny worms, others are massive whales. Some plants are tiny moss, others are giant redwoods. Despite these differences, organisms in the same kingdom share core traits that make them more similar to each other than to organisms in other kingdoms. This is why the classification system is so useful.
