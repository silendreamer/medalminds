## Subtopic: Forms of Energy (kinetic, potential, thermal, etc.)

### What Is Energy? The Big Picture
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** what-is-energy-big-picture
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** energy definition · forms of energy · SI unit joule
**Summary:** Understand what energy actually is, why physicists define it as "the ability to do work," and how to recognize its major forms in everyday situations.

#### Defining Energy
Energy is defined as the ability to do work — to exert a force over a distance and cause change. It is measured in joules (J), named after physicist James Prescott Joule. One joule equals one newton of force applied over one meter of distance (1 J = 1 N·m). Energy cannot be created or destroyed — only converted from one form to another. This principle, called conservation of energy, is one of the most powerful laws in all of science.

#### Kinetic Energy: Energy of Motion
Kinetic energy is the energy an object has because it is moving. The formula is KE = ½mv², where m is mass in kilograms and v is speed in meters per second. Notice the v² term: doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy. A 2 kg ball moving at 4 m/s has KE = ½(2)(16) = 16 J. Moving objects — cars, wind, flowing water, vibrating atoms — all carry kinetic energy.

#### Potential Energy: Stored Energy
Potential energy is stored energy due to position or condition. Gravitational potential energy (GPE = mgh) depends on mass, gravity (9.8 m/s²), and height. A book on a shelf has GPE; it can fall and convert that energy to kinetic energy. Elastic potential energy is stored in compressed or stretched springs and rubber bands. Chemical potential energy is stored in molecular bonds — food, fuel, and batteries all store chemical PE.

#### Thermal, Radiant, and Other Forms
Thermal energy is the total kinetic energy of all the atoms or molecules in a substance — the faster they move, the higher the temperature. Radiant (light) energy travels as electromagnetic waves and requires no medium. Sound energy is a mechanical wave — it needs matter to travel through. Electrical energy involves moving charges (electrons). Nuclear energy is stored in atomic nuclei and released in fission or fusion reactions.

#### Why Forms Matter for Science Bowl
Science Bowl questions often ask you to identify a form of energy from a description, or to recognize an energy conversion. Key pairs to memorize: chemical → thermal (burning fuel), electrical → radiant (light bulb), kinetic → thermal (friction), nuclear → thermal → electrical (power plant). The word "joule" always signals energy; "watt" signals power (energy per unit time).

#### Review Questions
1. What is the SI unit of energy, and what does it equal in base units?
2. A 5 kg cart moves at 6 m/s. What is its kinetic energy?
3. Name three forms of potential energy and give one example of each.

### Kinetic and Potential Energy: Going Deeper
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** kinetic-potential-energy-deeper
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** KE = ½mv² · GPE = mgh · energy conversion between KE and PE
**Summary:** Master the formulas for kinetic and gravitational potential energy and trace how they convert back and forth in real systems.

#### The KE Formula Up Close
Kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²) has two variables: mass and speed. Mass has a linear effect — doubling mass doubles KE. Speed has a squared effect — doubling speed quadruples KE. This is why car crashes at highway speeds are so much more destructive than low-speed fender-benders. Units check: kg × (m/s)² = kg·m²/s² = joule ✓. Always confirm your units when solving energy problems.

#### The GPE Formula Up Close
Gravitational potential energy (GPE = mgh) depends on mass (kg), gravitational acceleration g = 9.8 m/s² (or 10 m/s² for estimation), and height above a reference point (m). The reference point matters: choose the lowest point in the problem as h = 0. A 3 kg object lifted 5 m has GPE = 3 × 9.8 × 5 = 147 J. When it falls, that 147 J converts to kinetic energy (minus any lost to friction).

#### The Roller Coaster: A Perfect KE↔PE Exchange
A frictionless roller coaster is the classic KE↔PE exchange. At the top of a hill, PE is maximum and KE is minimum (v = 0 at the peak). As the coaster descends, PE converts to KE — speed increases. At the bottom of the valley, all PE has become KE (maximum speed). Total mechanical energy (KE + PE) stays constant throughout if we ignore friction. This is conservation of mechanical energy.

#### Elastic Potential Energy
Elastic PE is stored in deformed elastic objects — springs, rubber bands, bows. By Hooke's Law, spring force F = kx, where k is the spring constant (stiffness) and x is the stretch or compression distance. The elastic PE stored equals ½kx². When a spring releases, elastic PE converts to kinetic energy. Archery, pole vaulting, and trampolining all demonstrate elastic PE converting to kinetic energy.

#### Science Bowl Application
Bowl toss-ups often describe a scenario and ask which form of energy is greatest at a given point. "At the highest point of a pendulum's swing" → maximum PE, zero KE. "At the lowest point" → maximum KE, zero PE. Memorize: height = PE; speed = KE. When they ask for total mechanical energy, it stays constant (if frictionless) and equals the initial PE if started from rest.

#### Review Questions
1. A 4 kg object falls from a height of 10 m. What is its speed just before hitting the ground? (Use g = 10 m/s²)
2. At what point in a pendulum's swing is kinetic energy at its maximum?
3. How does doubling the speed of an object affect its kinetic energy?

### Thermal Energy, Heat, and Temperature
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** thermal-energy-heat-temperature
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** thermal energy vs. temperature · heat transfer · specific heat
**Summary:** Distinguish between thermal energy, heat, and temperature — three related but different concepts that confuse many students.

#### Three Different Things
Thermal energy, heat, and temperature are related but distinct. **Thermal energy** is the total kinetic energy of all particles in an object — a bathtub of warm water has more thermal energy than a cup of boiling water because it contains more molecules. **Temperature** measures the average kinetic energy per particle — the cup of boiling water has higher temperature. **Heat** is thermal energy in transit — energy flowing from a warmer object to a cooler one. Heat is a process, not a thing an object "has."

#### Heat Always Flows From Hot to Cold
The second law of thermodynamics states that heat spontaneously flows from regions of higher temperature to regions of lower temperature — never the reverse without work being done. Two objects in contact will exchange heat until they reach the same temperature (thermal equilibrium). This is why ice melts in your hand (heat flows from your hand to the ice) and why coffee cools down (heat flows from coffee to the cooler air).

#### Three Modes of Heat Transfer
Heat transfers in three ways. **Conduction** is direct contact — atoms bump into neighbors, passing kinetic energy along (metals conduct well; wood and air conduct poorly). **Convection** is fluid movement — warmer, less-dense fluid rises while cooler, denser fluid sinks, creating circulation currents (explains weather patterns and boiling water). **Radiation** is electromagnetic waves (mainly infrared) that need no medium — this is how the Sun warms Earth across 93 million miles of vacuum.

#### Specific Heat Capacity
Different materials require different amounts of energy to change temperature. Specific heat capacity (c) measures joules needed to raise 1 kg by 1°C. Water has an exceptionally high specific heat (4,186 J/kg·°C), which is why oceans moderate coastal climates. The formula Q = mcΔT calculates heat transferred, where Q is joules, m is mass in kg, and ΔT is the temperature change in °C.

#### Science Bowl Clue Patterns
Bowl questions on thermal energy often test conduction vs. convection vs. radiation distinctions, or ask which material has higher thermal energy (hint: consider both temperature AND mass). Common trap: "Which has more thermal energy — a large block of ice at 0°C or a small cup of water at 20°C?" The answer depends on total mass and particle count, not just temperature.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain the difference between thermal energy and temperature using a specific example.
2. Which mode of heat transfer can work through a vacuum? Give one real-world example.
3. Water has a specific heat of 4,186 J/kg·°C. How much energy is needed to heat 2 kg of water by 5°C?

### Energy Conversions in Real Life
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** energy-conversions-real-life
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** energy conversion chains · efficiency · real-world energy systems
**Summary:** Trace energy conversion chains in common devices and understand why no conversion is ever 100% efficient.

#### Every Device Is an Energy Converter
No device creates energy — every device converts energy from one form to another. A light bulb converts electrical energy → radiant (light) + thermal energy. A car engine converts chemical (fuel) → thermal → kinetic energy. A hydroelectric dam converts gravitational PE (water height) → kinetic (falling water) → electrical. A microphone converts sound (mechanical wave) → electrical. Understanding energy conversion chains helps you answer "where does the energy come from?" for any system.

#### The Conversion Chain for Electricity
Electricity in your home typically starts as chemical PE in coal or natural gas. Burning releases thermal energy, which heats water to steam. Steam drives turbines (thermal → kinetic). Turbines spin generators (kinetic → electrical). Electricity travels through wires to your home. At each step, some energy is "lost" as heat — this is why overall efficiency from fuel to useful electricity is only about 30–40% for a typical coal plant.

#### Efficiency: How Much Energy Is Useful?
Efficiency = (useful energy output / total energy input) × 100%. A 20% efficient car engine turns 20% of gasoline's chemical energy into kinetic energy; the other 80% becomes waste heat. LED bulbs are about 90% efficient (90% light, 10% heat); incandescent bulbs are only ~5% efficient (5% light, 95% heat). Higher efficiency means less waste. The "lost" energy isn't destroyed — it's converted to thermal energy that spreads into the environment.

#### Solar Cells: Light to Electricity
Solar photovoltaic (PV) cells convert radiant energy (sunlight) directly into electrical energy using the photovoltaic effect. When photons strike silicon semiconductor material, they knock electrons loose, creating a current. Commercial solar panels are typically 15–22% efficient. Solar thermal systems instead concentrate sunlight to heat a fluid that drives a steam turbine — a different conversion pathway (radiant → thermal → kinetic → electrical).

#### Real-World Application: The Human Body
Your body is an energy converter. Chemical potential energy in food (measured in Calories, where 1 food Calorie = 4,184 J) converts to ATP, which powers muscle contractions (chemical → kinetic) and maintains body temperature (chemical → thermal). Even thinking requires energy — neurons use ATP to pump ions across membranes. The human body is roughly 25% efficient at converting food energy to useful mechanical work.

#### Review Questions
1. Write the complete energy conversion chain for a coal-fired power plant producing electricity for a home.
2. A motor receives 500 J of electrical energy and does 350 J of mechanical work. What is its efficiency?
3. Why is it impossible for any real energy conversion to be 100% efficient?

### Energy at Home: Tracking Kilowatt-Hours
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** energy-at-home-kilowatt-hours
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** power vs. energy · kilowatt-hour · reading an energy bill
**Summary:** Connect energy physics to electricity bills — understand watts, kilowatts, and kilowatt-hours in the context of home energy use.

#### Power vs. Energy
Power is the rate of energy use: Power (watts) = Energy (joules) / Time (seconds). A 100-watt light bulb uses 100 J every second. Power tells you HOW FAST energy is being used; energy tells you the total amount used. For home electricity, power is measured in kilowatts (kW) and energy in kilowatt-hours (kWh): 1 kWh = 1 kW used for 1 hour = 3,600,000 J = 3.6 megajoules.

#### Reading an Electricity Bill
Your electricity bill charges you per kilowatt-hour used. If your home uses 900 kWh per month at $0.12/kWh, your bill is $108. To find how much a device costs to run: multiply its power rating (in kW) by hours of use by the rate. A 0.1 kW (100 W) TV watched 4 hours per day for 30 days uses 0.1 × 4 × 30 = 12 kWh, costing about $1.44. This arithmetic shows up in Science Bowl Energy problems.

#### High vs. Low Energy Appliances
The biggest electricity users in a typical U.S. home are: heating/cooling (HVAC, ~45% of energy use), water heating (~18%), large appliances like washers/dryers/refrigerators (~15%), lighting (~9%). A central air conditioner runs at 3,000–5,000 W; a laptop at 20–60 W; an LED bulb at 8–10 W. Knowing these magnitudes helps you spot unreasonable answers in multiple-choice Energy questions.

#### Energy Star and Efficiency Standards
The U.S. Department of Energy's Energy Star program certifies appliances that meet efficiency standards — typically 10–50% more efficient than minimum federal requirements. Upgrading from an old refrigerator to an Energy Star model can save 100+ kWh/year. The DOE sets appliance efficiency standards through the National Appliance Energy Conservation Act; these standards have saved consumers hundreds of billions of dollars.

#### Science Bowl Connection
Energy questions frequently reference the DOE and real policy numbers. Know these: U.S. electricity generation is roughly 40% natural gas, 20% coal, 20% nuclear, 20% renewables (approximate 2024 figures). The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) tracks all this data and is often cited in bowl question sources. Efficiency, units (kWh, BTU, quad), and conversion factors appear regularly.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between a watt and a watt-hour?
2. A 2,000 W hair dryer is used for 15 minutes. How many watt-hours of energy does it use?
3. Name the three largest categories of home energy use in the U.S.

### Conservation of Energy: Forms in Review
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** conservation-of-energy-review
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** law of conservation of energy · energy conversion review · efficiency
**Summary:** A structured review of all energy forms and the conservation law, reinforcing the key facts and formulas before moving to applied topics.

#### The Law of Conservation of Energy
The Law of Conservation of Energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed — only converted from one form to another. The total energy of a closed system remains constant. This is one of the most fundamental laws in physics. When energy appears to "disappear" (a ball rolling to a stop), it has actually converted to thermal energy through friction. The total always balances. This law makes Science Bowl Energy questions predictable: trace where the energy goes.

#### 20 Must-Know Energy Facts
1. Energy unit = joule (J); power unit = watt (W = J/s)
2. KE = ½mv²; GPE = mgh; Q = mcΔT
3. KE quadruples when speed doubles
4. Thermal energy = total KE of all particles; temperature = average KE per particle
5. Heat flows from hot → cold spontaneously
6. Conduction (contact), convection (fluid flow), radiation (waves) — only radiation crosses a vacuum
7. Efficiency = useful output / total input × 100%
8. 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J
9. Water has the highest specific heat of common substances (4,186 J/kg·°C)
10. Chemical PE stored in bonds; released by combustion or cellular respiration
11. Nuclear energy comes from strong nuclear force in atomic nuclei
12. Sound requires a medium; light does not
13. Elastic PE = ½kx² (spring); F = kx (Hooke's Law)
14. Gravitational PE = 0 at the chosen reference height
15. Total mechanical energy = KE + PE (constant if frictionless)
16. Photosynthesis converts radiant → chemical energy
17. Battery: chemical → electrical energy
18. Generator: kinetic → electrical energy
19. Motor: electrical → kinetic energy
20. The "lost" energy in any real conversion becomes thermal energy

#### Conversion Chain Drill
Match each device to its primary energy conversion: (a) electric motor, (b) campfire, (c) green plant, (d) dam/generator, (e) ear.
Answers: (a) electrical → kinetic, (b) chemical → thermal + radiant, (c) radiant → chemical, (d) gravitational PE → kinetic → electrical, (e) sound (mechanical) → electrical (nerve signal).

#### Common Traps on the Exam
Trap 1: "Energy is lost in an inefficient process." Energy is NEVER lost — it converts to thermal energy. Trap 2: Confusing power (watts) with energy (joules or kWh). Trap 3: Assuming heat and temperature are the same thing. Trap 4: Forgetting that GPE depends on the chosen reference point (h = 0).

#### Review Questions
1. State the Law of Conservation of Energy in your own words.
2. An object's KE is 200 J at the bottom of a hill. It reaches the top where all KE has converted to PE (no friction). What is its PE at the top?
3. A machine is 60% efficient and receives 300 J. How much useful work does it do, and how many joules become waste heat?

## Subtopic: Energy Transfer & Transformation

### How Energy Moves: The Three Transfer Modes
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** energy-transfer-three-modes
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** conduction · convection · radiation · thermal equilibrium
**Summary:** Master the three ways energy moves between objects — and learn to identify which mode is at work from a brief description.

#### Why Energy Transfers
Energy transfers when there is a temperature difference. Warmer systems have higher-energy particles; when they contact or interact with cooler systems, energy flows from hot to cold until thermal equilibrium is reached. All three modes (conduction, convection, radiation) accomplish this transfer but through fundamentally different mechanisms. Recognizing which mode is operating from a short description is a classic Science Bowl skill.

#### Conduction: Energy Through Contact
Conduction transfers thermal energy through direct contact by particle collisions. In solids, vibrating atoms pass energy to neighbors. Metals (especially copper and silver) conduct heat efficiently because their electrons can move freely and carry energy rapidly. Wood, plastic, air, and wool are poor conductors (good insulators). The rate of conduction depends on the material's thermal conductivity, cross-sectional area, temperature difference, and thickness. Touching a metal doorknob in winter feels cold because the metal rapidly conducts heat away from your hand.

#### Convection: Energy Through Fluid Movement
Convection transfers heat by the movement of a fluid (liquid or gas). Warmer fluid is less dense and rises; cooler fluid is denser and sinks, creating a convection current. Examples: boiling water (hot water rises, cool water sinks), sea breezes (warm land air rises, cool ocean air moves in to replace it), Earth's mantle convection driving plate tectonics, and the convection zone inside the Sun. Forced convection uses a fan or pump to move the fluid — like a car's cooling system.

#### Radiation: Energy Through Waves
Radiation transfers energy as electromagnetic waves — primarily infrared radiation — and requires no medium. All objects above absolute zero emit radiation; hotter objects emit more. The Sun heats Earth through radiation across 93 million miles of vacuum. Dark, matte surfaces absorb and emit radiation better than shiny, light surfaces. A thermos uses a vacuum layer and reflective walls to minimize all three heat transfer modes simultaneously.

#### Identifying Transfer Mode: Quick Decision Tree
Contact between solids → Conduction. Fluid movement (liquid/gas circulating) → Convection. No medium required, or energy traveling through space → Radiation. Many real systems use all three: a boiling pot gets heat from the stove by conduction (pot bottom), distributes it by convection (water circulation), and loses some to the room by radiation.

1. Explain why metals feel colder than wood at the same room temperature.
2. Describe the convection current in a pot of water being heated on a stove.
3. Which heat transfer mode works through outer space? Why can the other two not work there?

### Energy Transformation Chains: From Source to Use
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** energy-transformation-chains
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** transformation chain · primary energy source · useful vs. waste energy
**Summary:** Follow energy from its origin through multiple transformations to understand how every useful energy service ultimately traces back to a primary source.

#### Primary Energy Sources
Primary energy sources are the starting points of energy transformation chains. Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) store chemical PE from ancient organisms. Nuclear fuels (uranium-235, plutonium-239) store nuclear PE in atomic nuclei. Renewable sources — solar radiation, wind (caused by solar heating), water (elevated by solar-powered evaporation), geothermal (from Earth's interior heat and radioactive decay) — provide continuous energy flows. Tracing energy back to its primary source often reveals "solar" as the ultimate origin (fossil fuels = ancient solar energy stored in biomass).

#### The Coal-to-Light-Bulb Chain
Start: chemical PE in coal. Combustion: chemical → thermal (1). Steam boiler: thermal heats water (thermal transfer). Steam turbine: thermal → kinetic (2). Generator: kinetic → electrical (3). Power lines: electrical transported (some lost to resistance as thermal). LED bulb: electrical → radiant (light) + a small amount of thermal (4). At each numbered step, some energy becomes waste heat. Total efficiency of this chain: roughly 30-35% of the coal's chemical energy reaches you as light.

#### The Solar-to-Food Chain
The Sun radiates energy. Plants capture it through photosynthesis: radiant → chemical (stored in glucose). Animals eat plants: chemical PE transfers up the food chain, losing about 90% at each trophic level. You eat food: chemical → chemical (ATP in cells) → kinetic (movement) + thermal (body heat). Every hamburger you eat traces its energy to sunlight that hit grass weeks or months ago.

#### Energy Degradation: Why Efficiency Matters
Every transformation loses some energy as low-quality thermal energy (waste heat). This is the Second Law of Thermodynamics: the quality of energy degrades with each conversion. High-quality energy (electricity, concentrated fuel) can do more work than low-quality energy (dispersed warmth). Society's energy challenge is to minimize these losses — hence the importance of efficiency standards for appliances, vehicles, and power plants.

#### Science Bowl Pattern
Bowl problems give you a device or scenario and ask you to identify: (1) the primary energy source, (2) the form used, or (3) what the "waste" energy becomes. The waste energy answer is almost always thermal energy. Remember that electrical energy from the grid is already 3–4 conversions removed from the primary fuel.

#### Review Questions
1. Trace the energy transformation chain from a natural gas well to a spinning electric fan motor.
2. Why is solar energy considered the ultimate source of most energy on Earth?
3. In a typical energy transformation chain, what form does "wasted" energy usually take?

### Real-World Energy Transfer: A Problem Set
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** real-world-energy-transfer-problems
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** heat transfer identification · efficiency calculation · real systems
**Summary:** Work through six real-world scenarios that require identifying transfer modes and calculating efficiency — the type of applied thinking Science Bowl tests.

#### Scenario 1: Cooking Methods
A chef cooks food three ways: (a) directly on a metal grill, (b) in boiling water, (c) in a microwave. Identify the primary heat transfer mode for each. Answers: (a) conduction — metal grill contact; (b) convection — hot water circulates around food; (c) radiation — microwave electromagnetic waves penetrate and heat water molecules inside the food. Bonus: a conventional oven uses all three — conduction from the rack, convection from hot air circulating, and radiation from hot oven walls.

#### Scenario 2: Insulating a House
A homeowner wants to reduce heat loss in winter. Where should they focus: windows, walls, or the roof? Heat rises, so the roof accounts for roughly 25–35% of heat loss; walls account for 20–30%; windows and doors together about 25–30%. The best insulation strategy: attic first (cheap, big impact), then walls. Double-pane windows trap air (poor conductor) between glass layers. Weatherstripping eliminates convection drafts. Reflective roof material reduces radiation absorption in summer.

#### Scenario 3: Car Engine Efficiency
A car engine converts gasoline's chemical energy to kinetic energy with about 20% efficiency. If a car uses 1 gallon of gasoline (≈ 132,000,000 J of chemical energy), how many joules actually move the car? 0.20 × 132,000,000 = 26,400,000 J of useful kinetic energy. The remaining 105,600,000 J become waste heat — in the exhaust, engine block, and coolant system. This is why engines need coolant and why cars have exhaust pipes.

#### Scenario 4: Thermos Bottle Design
A thermos minimizes all three heat transfer modes: the vacuum layer between walls eliminates conduction and convection (no material to conduct, no fluid to convect). The reflective (silvered) inner walls minimize radiation (shiny surfaces emit and absorb less infrared radiation). As a result, a good thermos keeps liquids hot or cold for hours. Identify which design feature targets which transfer mode.

#### Scenario 5: The Greenhouse Effect
Sunlight (short-wave radiation) passes through Earth's atmosphere and warms the surface. The warm surface radiates infrared (long-wave) radiation back upward. Greenhouse gases (CO₂, water vapor, methane) absorb this infrared and re-emit it in all directions, including back toward Earth. This is radiation-based heat transfer — the atmosphere acts like a selective radiation filter, transparent to incoming solar but partially opaque to outgoing infrared.

#### Review Questions
1. In Scenario 3, how many joules become waste heat per gallon of gasoline used?
2. Why does a thermos use a vacuum layer rather than just filling that space with a good insulating material?
3. Using the greenhouse effect scenario, explain why Earth's temperature would plummet overnight without an atmosphere.

### Energy Transfer: Complete Review
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** energy-transfer-complete-review
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** conduction vs. convection vs. radiation · efficiency · transfer chains
**Summary:** A rapid-fire review of energy transfer concepts tying together modes, chains, and efficiency for competition readiness.

#### 15 Must-Know Transfer Facts
1. Conduction: contact, works best in solids and metals
2. Convection: fluid movement, creates currents
3. Radiation: electromagnetic waves, no medium needed
4. Vacuum blocks conduction and convection; only radiation crosses
5. Shiny/light surfaces reflect radiation; dark/matte surfaces absorb and emit it
6. Thermal equilibrium: temperatures equalize when heat flow stops
7. Heat always flows hot → cold spontaneously
8. All objects above 0 K emit radiation

## Subtopic: Conservation of Energy

### The Law That Rules Everything
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** conservation-of-energy-law
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** conservation law · closed system · mechanical energy
**Summary:** Understand why the Law of Conservation of Energy is physics' most powerful bookkeeping tool — and how to apply it to any problem.

#### What "Conservation" Means in Physics
In everyday speech, "conservation" means careful use. In physics, "conservation" means a quantity stays constant — it is neither created nor destroyed. The Law of Conservation of Energy states that the total energy of an isolated (closed) system remains constant over time. Energy can change form (chemical → thermal → kinetic → electrical), but the total quantity never changes. This law has never been violated in any experiment in the history of science.

#### Applying Conservation of Energy: The Bookkeeping Approach
To use conservation of energy, track energy before and after an event. Before: list all forms of energy present and their amounts. After: list all forms. Total before = total after. Example: a 2 kg ball drops from 10 m (g = 10 m/s²). Before: GPE = mgh = 2×10×10 = 200 J, KE = 0. After (just before hitting ground): GPE = 0, KE = 200 J. Speed: KE = ½mv² → 200 = ½(2)v² → v² = 200 → v ≈ 14.1 m/s.

#### Conservation in Action: The Pendulum
A pendulum perfectly demonstrates KE↔PE conservation (ignoring air resistance and friction). At the highest point of its swing: maximum PE, zero KE. At the bottom (equilibrium): zero PE, maximum KE. The pendulum rises to the same height on both sides because total mechanical energy is constant. In reality, friction gradually converts mechanical energy to thermal energy and the pendulum slows — but energy is still conserved; it just changes form.

#### Where Does the "Lost" Energy Go?
In real systems, mechanical energy isn't truly conserved because friction, air resistance, and other forces convert KE into thermal energy. A ball rolling to a stop doesn't lose energy — it transfers energy to its surroundings as heat. Rub your hands together: the friction converts kinetic energy to thermal energy (your hands warm up). The total energy is always conserved; it just becomes more dispersed and less useful.

#### Science Bowl Significance
Conservation of energy is the foundation of virtually every Science Bowl Energy question. When a problem describes a system changing state, your first instinct should be: "Where did the energy go? What form did it take?" The total must balance. Look for trick questions where "lost" energy appears to violate conservation — it never does; it's always hiding as thermal energy somewhere.

#### Review Questions
1. A 5 kg object is dropped from 20 m. What is its speed just before hitting the ground? (g = 10 m/s², ignore air resistance)
2. A pendulum is released from height h above its lowest point. At what height does it have half its total energy as KE?
3. Why do real pendulums eventually stop swinging, even though energy is conserved?

### Conservation of Energy: Worked Problems
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** conservation-energy-worked-problems
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** energy conservation calculations · system analysis · friction effects
**Summary:** Six worked problems applying conservation of energy to increasingly complex situations — building the problem-solving fluency needed for Science Bowl.

#### Problem 1: Falling Object (Basic)
A 3 kg rock falls from rest at a height of 45 m. Find its speed just before impact. (g = 10 m/s²)
Initial: PE = 3×10×45 = 1350 J, KE = 0. Final: PE = 0, KE = 1350 J.
1350 = ½(3)v² → v² = 900 → **v = 30 m/s**. Units check: J/(kg) = m²/s² ✓.

#### Problem 2: Spring Launch
A compressed spring (elastic PE = 50 J) launches a 0.5 kg ball. Find the ball's launch speed.
50 J = ½(0.5)v² → v² = 200 → **v ≈ 14.1 m/s**. Note: all spring PE converts to kinetic (if horizontal, no GPE change).

#### Problem 3: Roller Coaster with Friction
A 500 kg roller coaster starts at rest at height 80 m. It reaches a valley and is measured at 35 m/s. How much energy was lost to friction?
Initial PE = 500×10×80 = 400,000 J. Final KE = ½(500)(35²) = ½(500)(1225) = 306,250 J. Energy lost = 400,000 − 306,250 = **93,750 J** (became thermal energy from friction).

#### Problem 4: Two-Stage Conversion
A 1 kg ball is thrown upward at 20 m/s from the ground. What is the maximum height it reaches? (g = 10 m/s²)
At max height, v = 0, so all KE → PE. Initial KE = ½(1)(400) = 200 J. GPE = mgh → 200 = 1×10×h → **h = 20 m**.

#### Problem 5: Power and Time
A motor lifts a 200 kg crate 10 m in 40 seconds. What power does the motor develop?
Work done = GPE gained = 200×10×10 = 20,000 J. Power = Work/time = 20,000/40 = **500 W** (= 0.5 kW).

#### Problem 6: Efficiency with Conservation
A hydroelectric turbine receives water with 2,000,000 J of gravitational PE. It is 85% efficient. How much electrical energy is produced?
Useful output = 0.85 × 2,000,000 = **1,700,000 J**. Waste heat = 300,000 J (heats water and bearings slightly).

#### Review Questions
1. A 10 kg object slides down a frictionless ramp from height 5 m. Find its speed at the bottom. (g = 10 m/s²)
2. A spring stores 80 J. It launches a 2 kg block. What is the block's speed after launch?
3. A system starts with 5000 J of total energy. After friction, only 3500 J is mechanical. Where is the missing 1500 J?

## Subtopic: Efficiency & Energy Loss

### What Is Efficiency? The Physics of Useful Energy
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** what-is-efficiency-physics
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** efficiency formula · useful vs. waste energy · second law
**Summary:** Understand why no real machine is 100% efficient, how to calculate efficiency, and what happens to the "lost" energy.

#### Defining Efficiency
Efficiency measures how much of the input energy becomes useful output energy. The formula is: Efficiency (%) = (Useful Energy Output / Total Energy Input) × 100. Or equivalently: Efficiency = Useful Power Output / Total Power Input. An efficiency of 60% means 60% of input energy does useful work; 40% is wasted (usually as heat). A perfectly efficient machine would have efficiency = 100%, but this is physically impossible for any real device — the Second Law of Thermodynamics guarantees some energy is always lost to thermal entropy.

#### Why 100% Efficiency Is Impossible
The Second Law of Thermodynamics states that in any spontaneous energy conversion, some energy becomes dispersed thermal energy (entropy increases). Even a "perfect" machine with no friction or air resistance generates some waste as electrons resist current flow (in electrical devices) or molecules collide (in thermal devices). This fundamental limit means engineers focus on minimizing losses rather than eliminating them. Real-world efficiencies: diesel engine ~45%, electric motor ~90%, LED bulb ~90%, incandescent bulb ~5%, gasoline engine ~20-25%, coal power plant ~35%.

#### What Happens to "Lost" Energy?
"Lost" energy doesn't disappear — it converts to thermal energy that spreads into the environment. A spinning motor warms the air around it. A light bulb filament glows AND heats the room. Friction between brake pads and rotors converts kinetic energy to heat (you can smell this). In power electronics, waste heat is so significant that chips require cooling fans. The energy is "lost" only in the sense that it's no longer in a useful, concentrated form.

#### Types of Energy Losses
**Friction losses:** Moving parts rubbing against each other convert kinetic energy to thermal. Minimized by lubrication, smooth surfaces, and bearings. **Electrical resistance losses:** Current through wires generates heat (P = I²R). High-voltage transmission reduces current and thus resistance losses. **Heat exhaust losses:** Heat engines cannot convert all thermal energy to work — they must dump some heat to a cold reservoir (Carnot's theorem). **Radiation losses:** Hot objects radiate infrared energy to surroundings.

#### Improving Real-World Efficiency
Engineers reduce losses through: insulation (reduce heat loss from buildings and pipes), lubrication (reduce friction), better materials (superconductors for zero electrical resistance), waste heat recovery (use exhaust heat for another purpose — "cogeneration"), and improved design (aerodynamic shapes reduce air resistance). The DOE's Advanced Manufacturing Office supports research on energy efficiency in industrial processes.

### Efficiency in Practice: Comparing Technologies
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** efficiency-in-practice-comparing-technologies
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** comparative efficiency · real device percentages · technology choices
**Summary:** Compare the efficiency of real technologies — vehicles, bulbs, power plants — and understand how efficiency improvements translate to energy and cost savings.

#### Lighting Efficiency Comparison
Incandescent bulbs: ~5% efficient (5% light, 95% heat). Compact fluorescent (CFL): ~25% efficient. LED: ~90% efficient. Switching a 60 W incandescent to a 9 W LED that produces equal light saves 51 W of power. At 4 hours/day for 365 days: 51W × 4h × 365d = 74,460 Wh ≈ 74.5 kWh saved per bulb per year. At $0.12/kWh, that's $8.94/year per bulb. The DOE estimates widespread LED adoption in the U.S. saves 75 terawatt-hours of electricity annually.

#### Vehicle Efficiency Comparison
Gasoline car: ~20-25% efficient (80% of fuel energy → heat). Hybrid vehicle: ~35-40% (recovers kinetic energy via regenerative braking — converts KE back to electrical energy when slowing). Electric vehicle: ~85-90% efficient motor, but the electricity generation may only be 35-60% efficient depending on grid mix. Diesel engine: ~40-45% efficient (higher compression ratio = more efficient combustion). Hydrogen fuel cell: ~60% efficient (converts H₂ + O₂ → electricity + water directly).

#### Power Plant Efficiency Comparison
Coal: ~33-40%. Natural gas (combined cycle): ~55-60% (uses exhaust heat from gas turbine to run a steam turbine — double conversion). Nuclear: ~33% (limited by steam cycle thermodynamics). Solar PV: ~15-22% (but "fuel" is free). Hydroelectric: ~85-95% (most efficient large-scale electricity source). Wind: ~35-45% of wind energy captured (Betz limit = theoretical max 59.3%).

#### Cogeneration: Using Waste Heat
Cogeneration (combined heat and power, CHP) captures "waste" thermal energy from electricity generation and uses it for space heating or industrial processes. A conventional power plant is ~35% efficient; a cogeneration plant can reach 80-90% overall efficiency by using the exhaust heat. Many hospitals, universities, and factories use cogeneration systems. The DOE's Combined Heat and Power Partnership promotes cogeneration adoption.

#### The Efficiency-Cost Trade-off
More efficient technologies often cost more upfront but save money over time. Life Cycle Cost Analysis (LCCA) compares: (initial cost) + (operating costs over lifetime). An LED bulb costs ~$5 vs. $1 for incandescent but lasts 25× longer and uses 90% less electricity. Simple payback period = additional upfront cost / annual energy savings. Understanding this analysis is important for Science Bowl Energy policy questions.

#### Review Questions
1. A combined-cycle natural gas plant is 58% efficient. If it generates 580 MW of electricity, what is its total fuel input power?
2. Why is hydroelectric power much more efficient than coal power, even though both drive generators?
3. What is regenerative braking in a hybrid vehicle, and which energy conversion does it perform?

#### Efficiency at a Glance
Formula: η (%) = (P_out / P_in) × 100 = (E_out / E_in) × 100. Rearrangements: E_out = η × E_in; E_waste = E_in − E_out; E_waste = (1 − η) × E_in.

Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards, set by the DOE and National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), require automakers to meet average fuel economy targets for their fleets (currently ~49 mpg for 2026 model year cars). Higher mpg = higher efficiency = less fuel burned per mile. These standards have been a major driver of vehicle efficiency improvements since 1975.
