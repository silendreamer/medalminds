#### Side-by-Side Comparison
A 1,000-megawatt nuclear plant produces the same electricity as a similarly sized coal plant but uses about 27 tons of enriched uranium fuel per year, while the coal plant burns approximately 3 million tons of coal and emits roughly 7 million tons of CO₂. The nuclear plant's waste — about 27 tons of spent fuel — fits in a space the size of a shipping container. Coal's waste includes millions of tons of CO₂ released into the atmosphere, hundreds of thousands of tons of ash (containing naturally radioactive thorium and uranium), and significant mercury and sulfur dioxide emissions. Measured per kilowatt-hour of electricity produced, nuclear energy has among the lowest lifecycle carbon emissions of any electricity source — similar to wind and solar.

#### Reliability and Land Use
Nuclear's 93% capacity factor far exceeds coal (55%), natural gas combined cycle (56%), solar PV (25%), and onshore wind (35%). A nuclear plant occupies a relatively small footprint — about 1.5 square miles for a 1,000 MW plant — compared to wind farms requiring hundreds of square miles for equivalent output. However, nuclear plants take 10–20 years and billions of dollars to build, while solar and wind farms can be deployed in months. This trade-off between reliability, cost, construction time, and environmental impact shapes the energy policy debate.

#### Review Questions
1. How much CO₂ does a nuclear plant emit compared to a coal plant of equivalent size?
2. Why is the capacity factor an important measure when comparing energy sources?
3. What is the land use trade-off between nuclear and wind energy?
