#### The Critical Difference: Rate of Reaction
A nuclear reactor maintains exactly one new fission per fission event — a "criticality" balance. The reaction is stable: constant power output. If control rods are withdrawn too much, the reactor becomes slightly supercritical and power rises — but slowly enough that operators (or passive feedback mechanisms) can respond. A nuclear weapon, by contrast, assembles a highly enriched (>90% U-235 or Pu-239) supercritical mass instantaneously — using a gun-type or implosion design — so the chain reaction multiplies billions of times in microseconds before the assembly flies apart. A reactor cannot explode like a nuclear bomb: the fuel is too dilute (3–5% enriched), the geometry wrong, and the chain reaction too slow.

What Chernobyl experienced was a steam explosion and graphite fire from extreme overheating — catastrophic, but not a nuclear detonation. The actual nuclear fission stopped almost immediately as the core dispersed. The radiation hazard came from the fission products and fuel blown into the environment, not from a bomb-type explosion.
