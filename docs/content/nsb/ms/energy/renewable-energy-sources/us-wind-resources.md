#### Where the Wind Blows
The richest wind resources in the continental United States are in the Great Plains — a continuous corridor from North Dakota and Montana south through Kansas, Oklahoma, and Texas. The flat terrain, lack of tree cover, and persistent pressure gradients from the Gulf of Mexico create sustained winds averaging 8–10 m/s. Texas leads all states in wind capacity (over 40 GW installed), followed by Iowa, which generates more than 60% of its electricity from wind. The U.S. wind resource is vast enough to supply more than 10 times current U.S. electricity demand if fully tapped — though transmission limitations and local land use conflicts constrain where turbines can be built.

#### Wind Capacity Factor
The capacity factor of U.S. onshore wind averages about 35% nationally — meaning a 2 MW turbine produces, on average, about 700 kW of power over a full year. This has been rising as turbines get taller (reaching stronger, steadier winds), blades get longer (sweeping more area), and better siting data is used. Some Great Plains sites exceed 45% capacity factors. Offshore wind averages 40–50%. NREL maintains detailed wind resource maps and the DOE's Wind Energy Technologies Office (at Oak Ridge and Sandia national laboratories) conducts research on turbine design, materials, and grid integration.
