#### Head-to-Head Comparison Table

| Source | Capacity Factor | LCOE (2023) | Key Advantage | Key Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Utility Solar PV | ~25% | $0.03–0.05/kWh | Cheapest new electricity | Variable; needs storage |
| Onshore Wind | ~35% | $0.03–0.05/kWh | Low cost; large resource | Variable; siting conflicts |
| Offshore Wind | ~45% | $0.08–0.12/kWh | Stronger, steadier wind | High installation cost |
| Hydropower | ~40% | $0.05–0.10/kWh | Dispatchable; pumped storage | Geography-limited; ecological impacts |
| Geothermal | ~90% | $0.06–0.10/kWh | Baseload; tiny footprint | Limited to volcanic regions |
| Biomass | ~85% | $0.08–0.15/kWh | Dispatchable; uses waste | Land use; lifecycle emissions debated |

#### Key Takeaways for the Grid
No single renewable perfectly solves all grid needs. Solar and wind provide the cheapest electricity but are variable. Hydropower and geothermal provide reliable baseload power but are geographically constrained. Biomass is dispatchable but raises land-use and emissions concerns. A decarbonized grid will likely need a portfolio approach — mixing multiple renewable sources, storage, transmission, and possibly nuclear — tailored to each region's specific resource mix and demand patterns.

#### Review Questions
1. Which renewable source has the highest capacity factor of those listed?
2. Why might a grid planner choose geothermal over solar even if solar is cheaper per kilowatt-hour?
3. What is the main advantage of hydropower over wind and solar?
