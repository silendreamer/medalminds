#### How Utility Solar Works
A utility-scale solar farm operates on the same photovoltaic principles as a rooftop system, just enormously scaled up. The Bhadla Solar Park in India — the world's largest — spans 56 km² and generates 2,245 megawatts at peak. In the United States, the Solar Star project in California (579 MW) and the Topaz Solar Farm (550 MW) were among the first large-scale installations. Solar farms use tracking systems — motorized mounts that rotate panels to follow the sun through the day — improving output by 20–40% compared to fixed panels. DC electricity from panels is converted to AC by inverters for transmission on the grid.

#### Intermittency and Grid Integration
Solar's major challenge is intermittency: it generates power only during daylight hours and is reduced by clouds, smoke, and snow. A utility solar farm has a capacity factor of roughly 25% — meaning it generates, on average, 25% of its theoretical maximum output over a full year. This is lower than nuclear (93%), coal (55%), or gas (56%). To compensate, grid operators pair solar farms with battery storage systems, natural gas peakers (plants that run only when demand is high), or long-distance transmission lines connecting sunny and cloudy regions. NREL research shows that combining solar, wind, and storage from diverse geographic locations can provide reliable power without fossil fuel backup.
