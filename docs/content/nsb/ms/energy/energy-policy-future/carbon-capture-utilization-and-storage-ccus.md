#### Point Source Carbon Capture
Capturing CO₂ from power plants, industrial facilities, or direct air requires removing CO₂ from the exhaust/air. Chemical solvents (amines, hydroxides) absorb CO₂. After saturation, solvents are heated, releasing concentrated CO₂. Point source capture is technically mature — several commercial facilities operate. Cost: $50-100/ton of CO₂ captured. Capture from industrial exhaust is cheaper than from ambient air.

#### Direct Air Capture (DAC)
Capturing CO₂ from ambient air (~420 ppm) is far harder than from point sources (4-8% at power plants). DAC typically uses solid sorbents or liquid solvents that bind CO₂ from air. The process is energy-intensive — requires 200-600 kWh per ton of CO₂ captured, depending on technology and air humidity. Cost: $250-600/ton (currently expensive but projected to drop to $100-200/ton by 2050).

#### Utilization: Making Use of Captured CO₂
Captured CO₂ can be used in: beverage carbonation, chemicals/plastics, building materials (concrete with embedded CO₂), enhanced oil recovery (injecting CO₂ into oil fields increases extraction — controversial because it recovers oil that would be burned). Using CO₂ in chemicals/fuels (electrofuels: CO₂ + H₂O + electricity → hydrocarbons) is emerging. Most utilization is temporary — CO₂ is eventually re-released (soft drinks, chemicals degraded). Some utilization is permanent (concrete, building materials).

#### Geological Storage
Permanent storage involves injecting CO₂ into deep geological formations (1,000+ meters underground). Suitable formations: depleted oil/gas fields (proven storage), deep saline formations, coal seams. Once injected, CO₂ becomes immobile due to pressure, temperature, and rock properties. Estimated capacity for permanent storage is huge (thousands of Gt), but verification and monitoring are challenging. Risk of leakage is low but non-zero.

#### CCUS Economics and Timelines
Current costs ($50-600/ton CO₂ depending on method) are often higher than emission prices ($5-80/ton in carbon markets). This creates an economic barrier — why would companies pay to capture if emission prices are lower? But carbon prices are rising, and CCUS costs are falling. Some models see CCUS as essential for 1.5-2°C climate targets, particularly for hard-to-abate sectors (aviation, shipping, cement). Large-scale deployment is likely 10-20 years away.
