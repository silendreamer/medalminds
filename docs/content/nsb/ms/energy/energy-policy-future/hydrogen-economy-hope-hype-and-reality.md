#### Types of Hydrogen: Color Coding
Gray hydrogen is produced from natural gas (emits 10-12 kg CO₂ per kg hydrogen). Blue hydrogen is gray hydrogen plus carbon capture (5-10 kg CO₂ per kg, depending on capture efficiency). Green hydrogen is produced by electrolysis using renewable electricity (<1 kg CO₂ per kg). Currently, ~95% of hydrogen is gray (cheap, $1-2 per kg). Green hydrogen costs $5-10/kg due to electricity costs and electrolysis inefficiency.

#### Electrolysis and Production Efficiency
Electrolysis splits water into hydrogen and oxygen. Alkaline electrolyzers are mature (80-85% efficiency). Polymer electrolyte membrane (PEM) electrolyzers are newer (70-80% efficiency but cheaper). Solid-oxide electrolyzers operate at high temperature (80°C), improving efficiency to 85-90% but requiring more durable materials. Improving electrolysis efficiency and cost is a key priority to make green hydrogen competitive.

#### Current Hydrogen Uses
~98% of hydrogen is used in industry: ammonia production (fertilizer), petroleum refining (hydrocracking), methanol synthesis, steel production (direct reduction). These are essential uses that currently rely on gray hydrogen. Switching to green hydrogen would decarbonize these industries but requires vast production increases and infrastructure.

#### Future Hydrogen Applications
Proposed uses: fuel cells for vehicles (buses, trucks, forklifts, trains), heating buildings, power generation, aviation fuel. Hydrogen fuel cells are proven but limited — high cost, low efficiency (55-65% tank-to-wheels), lack of refueling infrastructure. In vehicles, battery-electric is more efficient (85% tank-to-wheels) and cheaper. Hydrogen shines in applications where batteries are impractical: aviation (hydrogen is lighter than batteries), long-haul shipping.

#### Hype vs. Reality
Hydrogen is often oversold as a universal solution ("hydrogen economy"). In reality, hydrogen is one tool for specific uses. For light-duty vehicles, batteries are superior (cheaper, more efficient). For industry and long-haul transport, hydrogen is promising but not yet economic. A realistic path involves green hydrogen for hard-to-electrify sectors, NOT a complete shift to hydrogen economy.
