#### Coal's Peak in U.S. Energy
Coal once generated over 50% of U.S. electricity (as recently as 2008). It was cheap, abundant, and the technology for burning it was well-established. The U.S. consumed about 1 billion tons of coal per year at peak. Coal mining employed hundreds of thousands of workers in Appalachian, Western, and Midwestern states. The National Coal Association tracked production; the Energy Information Administration (EIA) published detailed statistics on coal's dominance.

#### The Rapid Decline
Since 2008, U.S. coal use has fallen dramatically — to less than 17% of electricity generation by 2023. Two main drivers: (1) Natural gas prices collapsed after the shale gas revolution (hydraulic fracturing / fracking unlocked vast domestic gas supplies), making gas-fired power plants economically superior. (2) Wind and solar costs plummeted — utility-scale solar fell ~90% in cost per kilowatt-hour between 2010 and 2023. Environmental regulations (air quality, water quality, coal ash disposal) also increased compliance costs.

#### Economic Impacts on Coal Communities
Coal-producing regions — particularly Appalachian states like West Virginia, Kentucky, and eastern Ohio — face significant economic challenges as mines close. Coal mining jobs peaked at ~90,000 nationally (down from ~250,000 in the 1980s). These are often high-wage jobs in regions with few alternatives. The federal government's Abandoned Mine Land (AML) program funds cleanup of land damaged by pre-1977 mining. The POWER (Partnerships for Opportunity and Workforce and Economic Revitalization) Initiative supports economic diversification in coal communities.

#### Coal and Science Bowl Energy Questions
Science Bowl toss-ups and bonus questions about coal often reference: coal's formation during the Carboniferous period, the four coal grades (peat/lignite/bituminous/anthracite), sulfur dioxide as coal's primary air pollutant, fly ash as a combustion byproduct, and the DOE's Office of Fossil Energy and Carbon Management. Know that "coal rank" refers to the level of carbon concentration.
