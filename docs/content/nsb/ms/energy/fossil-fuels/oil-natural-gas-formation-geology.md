#### From Marine Life to Petroleum
Oil and natural gas form from the remains of marine microorganisms — primarily plankton, algae, and zooplankton — that died and settled to the seafloor hundreds of millions of years ago. In oxygen-poor bottom environments, these organisms were preserved rather than decomposed and were gradually buried under sediment. Over millions of years, heat and pressure transformed this organic matter (called kerogen) into liquid oil and gaseous methane. This process is called catagenesis, and it typically occurs at temperatures of 60–120°C (140–250°F), at burial depths of 1–4 km.

#### Source Rocks, Migration, and Traps
The rock where petroleum forms is called the **source rock** (usually organic-rich shale). Once formed, oil and gas are less dense than the surrounding rock and water, so they migrate upward through porous rock layers. They accumulate in **reservoir rocks** (typically porous sandstone or limestone) beneath an impermeable **cap rock** (like salt or tight shale) that prevents further upward migration — this is called a **trap**. Common trap types: anticlines (upward rock folds), fault traps, and salt domes. Geologists use seismic surveys to locate these traps before drilling.

#### Conventional vs. Unconventional Resources
**Conventional oil and gas** are found in permeable reservoir rocks and flow easily to the well. **Unconventional resources** are trapped in source rocks (shale oil and shale gas) or tight formations that don't allow free flow — they require hydraulic fracturing (fracking) or other techniques to release. The U.S. shale gas revolution, driven by combining horizontal drilling with fracking, transformed the country from a gas importer to the world's largest natural gas producer by 2009.

#### Natural Gas Composition
Natural gas is primarily methane (CH₄, typically 70–90%), with smaller amounts of ethane (C₂H₆), propane (C₃H₈), butane (C₄H₁₀), and non-hydrocarbon gases (CO₂, N₂, H₂S). Natural gas liquids (NGLs) — ethane, propane, butane — are separated and sold separately. Methane released directly to the atmosphere (without burning) is a powerful greenhouse gas — about 80× more potent than CO₂ over 20 years. This is why methane leakage from gas infrastructure ("fugitive emissions") is an important environmental issue.
