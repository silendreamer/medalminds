## Subtopic: Solar Energy (Passive & Active)

### How Solar Energy Works: The Photovoltaic Effect
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** solar-photovoltaic-effect
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** photovoltaic effect · solar cell · semiconductor · silicon · electron
**Summary:** Solar panels generate electricity by using photons of sunlight to knock electrons loose in a semiconductor material — a process called the photovoltaic effect.

#### Light as Energy Packets
Sunlight isn't just a glow — it's a stream of energy packets called photons. Each photon carries a specific amount of energy determined by its wavelength (color). When a photon strikes certain materials, it can knock an electron loose from its atom, allowing electric current to flow. This is the photovoltaic (PV) effect, discovered by Edmond Becquerel in 1839. Silicon — the second most abundant element in Earth's crust — is by far the most widely used photovoltaic material because of its semiconductor properties: it conducts electricity under some conditions but not others, allowing engineers to control the flow of electrons.

#### Inside a Solar Cell
A solar cell is a silicon sandwich. Two layers of silicon are treated with different impurities: one layer (n-type) has extra electrons; the other (p-type) has "holes" — places where electrons are missing. At the junction between these layers, a built-in electric field forms. When sunlight knocks electrons loose in the silicon, this electric field pushes them in one direction, creating a directional flow of electrons — electricity. A single silicon solar cell produces about 0.5 volts. Cells are wired together into modules (panels), and modules are wired together into arrays. A typical residential rooftop system has 20–30 panels and produces 5–10 kilowatts.

#### Efficiency and Types of Solar Panels
Solar panel efficiency — the fraction of sunlight converted to electricity — ranges from about 15% for most standard residential panels to 22–23% for premium monocrystalline panels. Multi-junction concentrator cells used in spacecraft can exceed 40% but are extremely expensive. A standard 400-watt panel is about 2 m² — meaning it converts roughly 20% of the 1,000 watts/m² arriving on a sunny day. The remaining 80% is reflected or converted to heat. Panel efficiency has improved dramatically over the past 30 years while prices have fallen over 90%, making solar the fastest-growing source of new electricity worldwide.

#### The Solar Resource
The amount of solar energy available depends on location, time of year, cloud cover, and atmospheric conditions. The "solar constant" — the solar irradiance at Earth's outer atmosphere — is about 1,361 watts per square meter. After passing through the atmosphere, roughly 1,000 W/m² reaches a horizontal surface on a clear day at noon. Average annual sunlight (insolation) varies from about 4.5–6.5 peak sun hours per day in the southwestern United States (ideal for solar) to 2.5–3.5 hours in the Pacific Northwest. The DOE's National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) in Golden, Colorado is the leading U.S. research center for solar and other renewable energy technologies.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the photovoltaic effect, and who first observed it?
2. Why is silicon such a widely used material for solar cells?
3. What does "solar panel efficiency" mean, and what is a typical value for residential panels?

### Passive Solar Design: Heating Without Panels
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** passive-solar-design
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** passive solar · thermal mass · south-facing windows · daylighting · heat gain
**Summary:** Passive solar design uses building orientation, window placement, and thermal mass to naturally heat, cool, and light buildings without active mechanical systems.

#### What Is Passive Solar?
Passive solar energy uses building design — not machinery — to capture, store, and distribute solar heat. The term "passive" distinguishes these techniques from "active" solar systems (which use pumps, fans, or photovoltaic panels). A well-designed passive solar building in the northern hemisphere orients its longest wall to face south, placing large windows there to admit winter sunlight. In summer, roof overhangs are sized to block the higher summer sun while admitting the lower winter sun. This simple geometry can dramatically reduce heating and cooling energy needs without any moving parts.

#### Thermal Mass and Heat Storage
The key to passive solar heating is thermal mass: materials that absorb, store, and slowly release heat. Concrete floors, stone walls, brick, and tiles all have high thermal mass. When sunlight streams through south-facing windows, it warms these surfaces. As the sun sets and temperatures drop, the stored heat slowly radiates into the living space, maintaining warmth through the night. Water is also an excellent thermal mass (used in containers on south walls in some designs). The classic "Trombe wall" — a dark-colored masonry wall behind a south-facing glass panel — absorbs solar heat and releases it indoors 6–8 hours later.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between passive and active solar systems?
2. Why do passive solar homes in the northern hemisphere face south?
3. What is thermal mass, and name two materials with high thermal mass.

### Solar Energy at Scale: Utility Solar Farms
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** utility-solar-farms
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** utility-scale solar · capacity factor · solar farm · grid integration · inverter
**Summary:** Large solar farms produce electricity for thousands of homes but face challenges of intermittency, land use, and grid integration.

#### How Utility Solar Works
A utility-scale solar farm operates on the same photovoltaic principles as a rooftop system, just enormously scaled up. The Bhadla Solar Park in India — the world's largest — spans 56 km² and generates 2,245 megawatts at peak. In the United States, the Solar Star project in California (579 MW) and the Topaz Solar Farm (550 MW) were among the first large-scale installations. Solar farms use tracking systems — motorized mounts that rotate panels to follow the sun through the day — improving output by 20–40% compared to fixed panels. DC electricity from panels is converted to AC by inverters for transmission on the grid.

#### Intermittency and Grid Integration
Solar's major challenge is intermittency: it generates power only during daylight hours and is reduced by clouds, smoke, and snow. A utility solar farm has a capacity factor of roughly 25% — meaning it generates, on average, 25% of its theoretical maximum output over a full year. This is lower than nuclear (93%), coal (55%), or gas (56%). To compensate, grid operators pair solar farms with battery storage systems, natural gas peakers (plants that run only when demand is high), or long-distance transmission lines connecting sunny and cloudy regions. NREL research shows that combining solar, wind, and storage from diverse geographic locations can provide reliable power without fossil fuel backup.

#### Review Questions
1. What is a solar tracking system, and why is it used?
2. What is the capacity factor of solar, and how does it compare to nuclear?
3. How do grid operators compensate for solar's intermittency?

### Comparing Solar Technologies: PV vs. Concentrated Solar Power
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** pv-vs-csp
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** CSP · parabolic trough · power tower · molten salt storage · PV
**Summary:** Photovoltaic panels and concentrated solar power (CSP) both harness the sun but convert its energy in fundamentally different ways, with CSP having the advantage of built-in thermal storage.

#### How CSP Works
Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) uses mirrors or lenses to focus sunlight onto a receiver, generating intense heat that drives a conventional steam turbine — no photovoltaic effect involved. The most common design is the parabolic trough: curved reflective troughs focus sunlight onto a fluid-filled tube running along their focal line, heating synthetic oil to ~400°C. The hot oil transfers heat to water, generating steam. Power towers (central receiver systems) use hundreds of computer-controlled mirrors (heliostats) focused on a tower-top receiver; the Ivanpah Solar Electric Generating System in California's Mojave Desert uses this design and generates 392 MW. Some CSP plants store excess heat in molten salt tanks, enabling electricity generation hours after sunset — a significant advantage over PV.

#### Trade-offs Between PV and CSP
PV: simpler, cheaper, works in diffuse light, easy to scale (from rooftops to multi-GW farms), no moving parts in the panels. CSP: requires direct, intense sunlight (works poorly in cloudy regions), more complex, higher capital cost, but can store energy thermally for hours without expensive batteries. As battery costs have fallen dramatically since 2010, PV + battery storage is increasingly competitive with CSP for storage capability, and utility-scale PV costs have dropped below CSP in most markets. New CSP projects are rare outside of sunny desert regions; PV dominates new solar installations worldwide.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference in how PV and CSP convert solar energy to electricity?
2. What advantage does CSP with molten salt storage have over PV alone?
3. Why has PV become more economically competitive than CSP in recent years?

### Solar's Past, Present, and Future
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** solar-history-future
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** cost reduction · perovskite · bifacial panels · agrivoltaics · price learning curve
**Summary:** Solar PV costs have fallen 90% in the past decade, driven by manufacturing scale-up, and emerging technologies promise further improvements.

#### The Solar Learning Curve
Solar PV follows a "learning rate" of about 20% cost reduction for every doubling of installed capacity. When the first commercial solar panels shipped in the 1970s, they cost $76 per watt. By 2010, the price was ~$2/watt. By 2023, utility-scale solar costs had fallen to $0.20–$0.30 per watt — a 99.7% price reduction over 50 years, making solar the cheapest source of new electricity in history in most of the world. This rapid cost decline — faster than most energy analysts predicted — has made solar the fastest-growing energy source on Earth, with over 1 terawatt of total installed capacity as of 2023.

#### Emerging Solar Technologies
Perovskite solar cells use a class of crystals (calcium titanium oxide structure) that can be applied as thin films and are cheaper to manufacture than silicon. Lab efficiencies exceed 26%; tandem perovskite-silicon cells have reached 33%. Commercial durability (stability over 25 years) is still being improved. Bifacial panels capture sunlight from both front and back surfaces, using light reflected from the ground beneath them — improving output by 5–15%. Agrivoltaics combines solar panels with agricultural land, placing panels high enough for crops or sheep to coexist beneath them, sharing land between food and energy production.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the solar learning rate, and what has it meant for prices?
2. What advantage do perovskite solar cells have over silicon?
3. What is agrivoltaics, and why might it be valuable?

### Solar in Daily Life: From Space Satellites to Calculators
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** solar-daily-life
**Estimated time:** 8 min
**Key concepts:** space solar · off-grid solar · net metering · solar water heater
**Summary:** Solar energy powers everything from the International Space Station to remote village water pumps, and net metering policies help rooftop solar owners get credit for excess electricity.

#### Solar Applications Beyond Grid Power
Space: virtually all satellites and the International Space Station are powered by solar panels — there's no atmosphere to block sunlight, maximizing efficiency. Off-grid solar electrifies remote villages in Africa, Asia, and Latin America where running transmission lines would be prohibitively expensive. Solar water heaters (distinct from PV) use rooftop collectors to directly heat water using solar thermal energy, common in Israel, China, and Germany. Solar-powered water pumps provide irrigation and drinking water in rural areas. Calculators have used tiny solar cells since the 1970s — their low power requirements make even inefficient small cells practical.

#### Net Metering
Most U.S. states have net metering policies: rooftop solar owners who generate more electricity than they use send the excess to the grid and receive a credit on their electricity bill, effectively spinning their meter backward. This policy makes rooftop solar economically attractive for homeowners, who can size their system to roughly match their annual electricity use and see bills near zero. Utilities and some states have debated the fairness of net metering — whether solar customers appropriately pay for grid infrastructure they still rely on — leading to ongoing policy revisions in California and elsewhere.

1. Why is solar ideal for powering spacecraft?
2. What is net metering, and how does it benefit rooftop solar owners?

## Subtopic: Wind Energy

### How Wind Turbines Generate Electricity
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** wind-turbines-basics
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** wind turbine · rotor blade · generator · kinetic energy · capacity factor
**Summary:** Wind turbines convert the kinetic energy of moving air into electricity through aerodynamic lift on rotor blades that spin a generator.

#### Wind as Kinetic Energy
Wind is simply moving air — air that gained kinetic energy from uneven solar heating of Earth's surface. Warm air over deserts rises, cool air from the ocean rushes in to replace it, creating persistent wind patterns. The power available in wind is proportional to the cube of wind speed: double the wind speed and you get eight times the power. This cubic relationship makes wind resource assessment critical — a site with average winds of 7 m/s yields about twice the energy of a site averaging 5.6 m/s. A turbine that is well-sited at a consistently windy location produces dramatically more electricity than one in marginal wind conditions.

#### How a Turbine Works: Aerodynamic Lift
A wind turbine doesn't work like a sail (pushing the blade) — it works like an airplane wing. The curved shape of each rotor blade creates different air pressures on its two sides as wind passes, generating lift perpendicular to the wind direction. This lift force rotates the rotor. Three blades are the universal standard because fewer blades spin faster and with less torque (hard on machinery); more blades capture diminishing additional energy while adding cost and weight. Modern utility-scale turbines have blades 50–80 meters long — longer than a 747's wingspan. The hub height (where blades attach) is typically 80–120 meters, where winds are stronger and steadier than at ground level.

#### From Rotor to Grid
The rotor spins at 5–15 revolutions per minute — too slow for a generator, which needs 1,000–1,800 rpm. Most turbines use a gearbox to step up the rotation speed. The generator inside the nacelle (the housing on top of the tower) converts mechanical rotation to electricity. A turbine also needs to face the wind: an active yaw system uses motors to rotate the nacelle toward the wind direction, controlled by a wind vane. Modern direct-drive turbines eliminate the gearbox using permanent magnet generators that work at slow rotor speeds — reducing maintenance needs.

#### Offshore vs. Onshore Wind
Offshore wind turbines are built in the ocean, where winds are stronger, steadier, and less turbulent than on land, and where large turbines don't disturb neighbors. The trade-off is much higher construction and maintenance costs. Offshore wind turbines are typically 10–15 MW capacity — double or triple the size of land-based machines. The United States has been slow to develop offshore wind compared to Europe; the first commercial U.S. offshore wind farm (Block Island, RI, 5 turbines, 30 MW) opened in 2016. DOE's National Offshore Wind Strategy targets 30 GW of offshore capacity by 2030.

#### Review Questions
1. Why does wind power scale with the cube of wind speed?
2. How does a wind turbine blade generate force? (Hint: it's not like a sail.)
3. What are the trade-offs between offshore and onshore wind energy?

### Wind Resources in the United States
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** us-wind-resources
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** wind resource map · Great Plains · wind corridor · capacity factor · DOE Wind Energy Technologies Office
**Summary:** The United States has vast wind resources concentrated in the Great Plains and offshore coasts, enough to supply many times the nation's current electricity demand.

#### Where the Wind Blows
The richest wind resources in the continental United States are in the Great Plains — a continuous corridor from North Dakota and Montana south through Kansas, Oklahoma, and Texas. The flat terrain, lack of tree cover, and persistent pressure gradients from the Gulf of Mexico create sustained winds averaging 8–10 m/s. Texas leads all states in wind capacity (over 40 GW installed), followed by Iowa, which generates more than 60% of its electricity from wind. The U.S. wind resource is vast enough to supply more than 10 times current U.S. electricity demand if fully tapped — though transmission limitations and local land use conflicts constrain where turbines can be built.

#### Wind Capacity Factor
The capacity factor of U.S. onshore wind averages about 35% nationally — meaning a 2 MW turbine produces, on average, about 700 kW of power over a full year. This has been rising as turbines get taller (reaching stronger, steadier winds), blades get longer (sweeping more area), and better siting data is used. Some Great Plains sites exceed 45% capacity factors. Offshore wind averages 40–50%. NREL maintains detailed wind resource maps and the DOE's Wind Energy Technologies Office (at Oak Ridge and Sandia national laboratories) conducts research on turbine design, materials, and grid integration.

1. Why does the Great Plains have such excellent wind resources?
2. What is the wind capacity factor, and what factors have been increasing it?
3. Which U.S. state leads in installed wind power capacity?

### Wind Power's Real-World Impact and Challenges
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** wind-power-challenges
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** wind variability · grid integration · bird mortality · noise · visual impact
**Summary:** Wind energy's rapid growth has brought real benefits and real trade-offs, including grid integration challenges, wildlife impacts, and land use conflicts.

#### Wind and the Grid
Wind power output varies with wind speed — a turbine at half its rated wind speed produces only one-eighth the power. Grid operators must constantly balance supply and demand, predicting wind output hours in advance and scheduling backup generation (often fast-responding natural gas peakers) to cover calm periods. As wind's share of the grid increases, the value of energy storage, demand response, and transmission connections to distant regions grows. Texas's ERCOT grid, with over 40% wind generation capacity, has developed sophisticated forecasting and balancing tools. The 2021 Texas winter storm froze both wind turbines and natural gas infrastructure — a reminder that no single energy source is immune to extreme weather.

#### Environmental and Community Impacts
Modern wind turbines are large and visible — a concern in scenic landscapes. Noise (primarily a "whooshing" from blade aerodynamics) limits turbines to distances of at least 300–500 meters from homes in most jurisdictions. Turbines do kill birds and bats; the American Bird Conservancy estimates 140,000–500,000 bird deaths per year from turbines in the United States — far less than the estimated 600 million killed annually by cats, or 200 million from building collisions, but a concern for local raptor populations near major wind farms. Radar-activated lighting systems and species-specific shutdown protocols during migration periods are increasingly required.

#### Review Questions
1. Why must grid operators maintain backup power sources even when wind is abundant?
2. What happened in Texas in 2021 regarding wind energy and extreme weather?
3. Name two environmental or community concerns associated with wind turbines.

### Innovation in Wind: Bigger, Taller, Smarter
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** wind-turbine-innovation
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** turbine size scaling · floating offshore · airborne wind · machine learning
**Summary:** Wind turbines are growing larger and smarter while engineers explore floating offshore platforms and airborne systems to access stronger winds.

#### The Trend Toward Larger Turbines
Since the 1980s, turbine rotor diameter has grown from 20 meters to 220+ meters, and rated capacity has grown from 50 kW to 15–20 MW for offshore machines. Larger turbines sweep more area, reach higher altitude winds, and produce electricity at lower cost per kilowatt-hour. GE Vernova's Haliade-X turbine (220 m rotor, 13 MW rated) can generate enough electricity for 12,000 European homes per turbine. Siemens Gamesa is testing 21 MW offshore machines. There are physical limits to how large turbines can grow — blade transport on roads and self-weight become constraints — but lightweight composite materials and segmented blades are pushing limits further.

#### Floating Offshore Wind
Most offshore wind turbines sit on fixed foundations in water up to ~60 meters deep. But the best offshore wind resources are often in deeper water — off California, Oregon, Hawaii, and the U.S. East Coast beyond the continental shelf. Floating offshore wind platforms — anchored by mooring lines rather than bottom-fixed foundations — enable turbines in water hundreds of meters deep. Norway's Hywind Tampen floating wind farm (88 MW, operational 2022) powers North Sea oil platforms. DOE has invested heavily in floating offshore wind demonstration projects to enable development along the U.S. Pacific Coast.

#### Review Questions
1. Why have wind turbines been getting larger over time?
2. What is floating offshore wind, and why is it important for the United States?

### Wind and Jobs: The Wind Energy Workforce
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** wind-energy-workforce
**Estimated time:** 8 min
**Key concepts:** wind turbine technician · manufacturing jobs · supply chain · rural economic development
**Summary:** Wind energy is one of the fastest-growing job categories in the United States, providing manufacturing, installation, and maintenance careers primarily in rural communities.

#### Wind Turbine Service Technicians
Wind turbine service technician is the fastest-growing occupation in the United States according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, projected to grow 45% by 2032. Technicians climb towers, inspect and repair blades, maintain gearboxes and generators, and operate computerized diagnostic systems — often working in remote locations in all weather conditions. Community colleges in wind-rich states offer 2-year associate's degree programs specifically for this career path. Average pay is around $56,000/year with strong demand in the Great Plains states. The role requires mechanical aptitude, comfort with heights, and willingness to work in teams in challenging conditions.

#### Rural Economic Development
Wind turbines generate significant income for rural landowners who lease their land — typically $5,000–$10,000 per turbine per year — while continuing to farm or graze animals around the turbines (turbines occupy only a small fraction of total land). Wind farms pay property taxes to rural counties, funding schools and services in areas with declining agricultural revenue. A single large wind farm can add millions of dollars per year to a county's tax base. This combination of lease payments and tax revenue has made wind energy politically popular in many rural communities despite occasional opposition from others concerned about visual impact and noise.

### Comparing Wind to Other Renewables
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** wind-vs-other-renewables
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** LCOE · lifecycle emissions · land use · reliability · complementarity
**Summary:** Wind and solar are complementary resources whose strengths and weaknesses offset each other, making them ideal partners on a decarbonized grid.

#### Levelized Cost of Energy Comparison
The levelized cost of energy (LCOE) — the total cost to build, operate, and fuel a power plant divided by all the electricity it produces over its lifetime — is the standard metric for comparing electricity sources. As of 2023: onshore wind ($0.03–0.05/kWh) and utility solar ($0.03–0.05/kWh) are the cheapest sources of new electricity in most of the world. Offshore wind is higher ($0.08–0.12/kWh) due to installation costs but falling rapidly. Natural gas combined cycle ($0.04–0.07/kWh) is competitive but subject to fuel price volatility. Nuclear ($0.09–0.15/kWh for new plants) is reliable but expensive to build. Coal ($0.07–0.14/kWh) is declining due to both economics and environmental regulations.

#### Wind and Solar: Better Together
Wind and solar have opposite daily and seasonal patterns in many regions. Solar peaks in summer afternoons; wind often peaks in winter and at night. In the United States, the southern Great Plains have strong year-round wind with peak solar in summer; the Pacific Coast has moderate wind and solar; the Midwest has excellent wind but less solar. Combining wind and solar on the same grid — especially with transmission connecting different regions — dramatically reduces the storage and backup capacity needed to keep electricity reliable. Studies show that a grid with 60–70% wind and solar can be kept reliable with modest amounts of storage and dispatchable backup, without nuclear or fossil fuels.

2. Why are wind and solar described as "complementary" resources?
3. How does combining wind and solar from different regions improve grid reliability?

## Subtopic: Hydropower & Tidal

### How Hydropower Works
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hydropower-basics
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** potential energy · kinetic energy · turbine · dam · generator
**Summary:** Hydropower converts the gravitational potential energy of elevated water into electricity using turbines and generators — the oldest and largest source of renewable electricity.

#### Water, Height, and Energy
Hydropower converts the energy stored in elevated water into electricity. Water held behind a dam has gravitational potential energy — energy due to its height above the turbine. When released, this energy converts to kinetic energy (the energy of motion) as water falls and accelerates. The turbine's blades are struck by this fast-moving water, spinning the turbine; the turbine spins the generator. The power available is proportional to both the height (head) of water and the flow rate (volume per second): P = ρ × g × Q × h, where ρ is water density (1,000 kg/m³), g is gravitational acceleration (9.8 m/s²), Q is flow rate in cubic meters per second, and h is head height in meters.

#### Types of Hydropower Plants
Conventional hydropower dams store water in reservoirs, providing dispatchable power — operators can increase or decrease output by opening or closing gates. Run-of-river plants have no large reservoir; they divert a portion of a river's flow through turbines and return it downstream. They are less impactful on ecosystems but cannot store energy for peak demand. Pumped-storage hydropower works in reverse: during low electricity demand (often at night), excess power from the grid pumps water uphill into an upper reservoir; during peak demand, water flows back down through turbines. Pumped storage provides ~95% of all U.S. grid-scale energy storage capacity — the largest battery in existence.

#### The Scale of Hydropower
Hydropower is the world's largest source of renewable electricity, providing about 16% of global electricity. In the United States, hydropower provides about 6% of electricity and 38% of renewable electricity. The largest U.S. dam is the Robert Moses Niagara Power Plant (2.7 GW) on the Niagara River in New York; Grand Coulee Dam on the Columbia River in Washington (6.8 GW) is the largest by generating capacity on the mainland. China's Three Gorges Dam (22.5 GW) is by far the world's largest power plant of any type.

#### Review Questions

### Environmental Impacts of Dams
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** dam-environmental-impacts
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** salmon migration · reservoir sedimentation · methane emissions · dam removal
**Summary:** Large dams provide clean electricity and water storage but fragment rivers, block fish migration, trap sediment, and emit greenhouse gases from decomposing organic matter.

#### Ecosystem Disruption
A dam fundamentally transforms a river from a flowing system to a reservoir, with profound ecological consequences. Coldwater fish that require flowing water for spawning — especially salmon — cannot reach their upstream spawning grounds when a dam blocks their path. Fish ladders (a series of stepped pools that fish can jump through) partially mitigate this but are imperfect solutions for large dams. The Snake River dams in the Pacific Northwest have contributed to the near-extinction of wild salmon runs despite federal salmon recovery programs costing billions of dollars. Below the dam, reduced sediment flow erodes beaches and river deltas; the Nile Delta has retreated significantly since the Aswan High Dam was completed in 1970.

#### Reservoir Emissions and Dam Removal
Reservoirs in tropical regions produce significant methane — a potent greenhouse gas — from decomposition of flooded vegetation and organic material under low-oxygen conditions. Some tropical dams have worse lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions per kilowatt-hour than natural gas plants. In temperate regions, reservoir emissions are much lower. Dam removal has become an increasingly common environmental restoration tool: as aging dams reach the end of their economic life, removing them can restore salmon runs and river ecosystems dramatically. The 2023 removal of four dams on the Klamath River in Oregon and California was the largest dam removal project in U.S. history.

### Tidal and Wave Energy
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** tidal-wave-energy
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** tidal energy · wave energy · tidal barrage · tidal stream · MHK
**Summary:** Tides and ocean waves contain enormous energy that can be captured by underwater turbines and wave devices, though commercial scale deployment remains limited.

#### How Tidal Energy Works
Tides are caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon (and to a lesser extent the Sun) on Earth's oceans, creating predictable rises and falls in sea level every 12.4 hours. Unlike wind and solar, tidal patterns are entirely predictable decades in advance — a major advantage for grid planning. Tidal energy can be captured two ways: tidal barrages (dam-like structures across tidal estuaries that harness the height difference between high and low tide), or tidal stream turbines (underwater turbines placed in fast-moving tidal currents, working like underwater wind turbines). The La Rance Tidal Barrage in Brittany, France (240 MW), operational since 1966, is the world's largest tidal barrage. Tidal stream systems are more environmentally benign than barrages, which block estuaries.

#### Wave Energy: Still Experimental
Waves store energy from wind blowing over the ocean surface. A rough sea represents enormous kinetic and potential energy — the power in ocean waves at the world's coasts is estimated at about 2 terawatts, roughly equivalent to global electricity demand. Despite decades of research, no wave energy technology has achieved commercial success at scale; the marine environment is extraordinarily harsh on mechanical devices. Point absorbers (buoys that bob with waves and drive generators) and oscillating water columns (air chambers compressed by waves to drive air turbines) are two leading designs. DOE's Water Power Technologies Office funds marine and hydrokinetic (MHK) energy research, and several demonstration projects operate off the U.S. Pacific Coast.

1. What makes tidal energy more predictable than solar or wind?
2. What is the difference between a tidal barrage and a tidal stream turbine?
3. Why has wave energy not achieved commercial success despite large resources?

### Hydropower in a Changing Climate
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** hydropower-climate-change
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** drought vulnerability · snowpack · reservoir management · grid flexibility
**Summary:** Climate change is reducing snowpack and shifting precipitation patterns in ways that threaten hydropower reliability while simultaneously increasing its value for balancing variable renewables.

#### The Snowpack Connection
Most western U.S. hydropower depends on mountain snowpack — accumulated snow that melts slowly throughout spring and summer, feeding rivers and refilling reservoirs. Climate change is causing warmer temperatures and more precipitation falling as rain rather than snow, reducing the snowpack that acts as a natural reservoir. The Colorado River basin, which supplies water and hydropower to seven states and Mexico, has experienced a 20-year "mega-drought" linked to climate change. Lake Mead — the reservoir behind Hoover Dam — fell to its lowest level ever recorded in 2022, forcing reductions in power output from the 2.1 GW Hoover Dam facility. Southwestern U.S. hydropower is increasingly unreliable as droughts intensify.

#### Flexibility: Hydropower's Hidden Value
Even as climate change threatens hydropower reliability in drought-prone regions, its flexibility makes it increasingly valuable in a grid dominated by variable wind and solar. A dam with a reservoir can hold back water when wind and solar are abundant (and electricity is cheap) and release it through turbines when the sun sets or the wind drops (and electricity is valuable). This flexibility — controllable within seconds — provides grid stability services that renewable generators alone cannot. The Pacific Northwest's Columbia River system (22 major dams, total ~21 GW capacity) provides essential balancing services for the entire western U.S. grid, integrating large amounts of wind and solar from Montana to California.

1. How does declining snowpack threaten western U.S. hydropower?
2. What happened to Lake Mead's water level, and what caused it?
3. Why is hydropower's flexibility especially valuable as more wind and solar are added to the grid?

## Subtopic: Geothermal & Biomass

### Geothermal Energy: Heat from the Earth
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** geothermal-energy-basics
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** geothermal gradient · hydrothermal · hot dry rock · enhanced geothermal · baseload
**Summary:** Geothermal energy taps heat from Earth's interior — continuously replenished by radioactive decay and primordial heat — to provide clean, reliable baseload electricity and direct heating.

#### The Earth's Internal Heat
Earth's interior is intensely hot: the inner core reaches about 5,400°C, similar to the Sun's surface. This heat comes from two sources: primordial heat left over from Earth's formation 4.5 billion years ago (when gravitational compression heated the planetary material) and ongoing radioactive decay of isotopes like uranium-238, thorium-232, and potassium-40 in Earth's mantle and crust. Heat flows outward from this hot interior toward the cooler surface — the geothermal gradient averages about 25–30°C per kilometer of depth. Where tectonic plates interact (mid-ocean ridges, subduction zones, hot spots), the gradient is much steeper and geothermal energy is most accessible.

#### How Geothermal Plants Generate Electricity
The most accessible geothermal resources are hydrothermal systems — places where groundwater naturally circulates deep enough to become very hot (150–350°C) and is naturally permeable to fluid flow. Three types of power plants exploit these systems. Dry steam plants (the oldest type) pipe steam directly from underground fractures to turbines — the Geysers in Northern California, the world's largest geothermal facility (1,520 MW), uses this method. Flash steam plants are most common: water at 180°C or above is brought to the surface and depressurized, causing it to "flash" to steam that drives turbines. Binary cycle plants use lower-temperature water (100–180°C) to vaporize a secondary working fluid with a lower boiling point (like isobutane), which drives turbines — expanding geothermal viability to lower-temperature resources.

#### Enhanced Geothermal Systems
Conventional geothermal requires naturally hot, permeable, water-bearing rock — limiting it to geologically active regions. Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) inject water into hot dry rock anywhere, creating artificial permeability through hydraulic fracturing, then circulate water through the heated rock and back to the surface. EGS could make geothermal viable across most of the United States. DOE's Enhanced Geothermal Shot initiative (launched 2022) aims to reduce EGS costs by 90% by 2035. NREL and the Geothermal Rising organization estimate that EGS could provide over 90% of U.S. electricity demand — though significant technical challenges remain.

### Biomass Energy: Burning Biology
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** biomass-energy-basics
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** biomass · bioenergy · biofuels · carbon neutral · lifecycle emissions
**Summary:** Biomass energy uses organic matter — wood, agricultural waste, dedicated energy crops, and organic garbage — as fuel, releasing energy stored by photosynthesis.

#### What Is Biomass?
Biomass refers to any organic material of biological origin used as an energy source: wood chips, crop residues (corn stover, wheat straw), dedicated energy crops (switchgrass, miscanthus, poplar), animal manure, food waste, and municipal solid waste. Biomass contains chemical energy originally captured from sunlight by photosynthesis — burning it releases CO₂, but the growing plants that produced the biomass absorbed CO₂ from the atmosphere. If biomass is regrown at the same rate it is harvested, the net CO₂ emissions are theoretically zero (carbon neutral). In practice, the carbon neutrality claim depends heavily on land use changes, harvesting practices, and energy required for processing.

#### Biopower, Biofuels, and Biogas
Biomass can be converted to energy three ways. Direct combustion: burning wood or waste in a boiler produces steam for electricity or heat — simple but relatively inefficient. Biofuels: fermenting corn starch or sugarcane juice produces ethanol (blended into gasoline); pressing soybeans or algae produces biodiesel. The United States blends 10% ethanol into most gasoline (E10); E85 (85% ethanol) is used in flex-fuel vehicles. Biogas: anaerobic digestion of food waste, manure, or sewage by bacteria produces methane-rich biogas, which can replace natural gas in heating, electricity generation, or vehicle fuel. Landfill gas capture turns decomposing garbage into usable energy.

### Geothermal and Biomass in the Real World
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** geothermal-biomass-real-world
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** Iceland · the Geysers · corn ethanol · landfill gas · district heating
**Summary:** Iceland runs almost entirely on geothermal and hydropower; the U.S. Geysers complex powers a California county; corn ethanol supplies 10% of U.S. gasoline; landfill gas recovery reduces methane emissions.

#### Iceland: A Geothermal Nation
Iceland sits directly on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge — a diverging plate boundary — with abundant volcanic activity and hydrothermal resources. About 66% of Iceland's primary energy (not just electricity) comes from geothermal, and 30% from hydropower. Geothermal directly heats 90% of Icelandic homes through hot water pipes — a system called district heating. The capital, Reykjavik, is one of the world's cleanest cities despite its cold climate because it burns no fossil fuels for heating. Iceland also uses geothermal for greenhouse food production, fish drying, swimming pools, and aluminum smelting (using cheap geothermal electricity). Costa Rica and Kenya also generate large fractions of their electricity from geothermal.

#### U.S. Geothermal and Biomass Examples
The Geysers Complex north of San Francisco is the world's largest geothermal facility, generating enough electricity for 900,000 homes. It draws on steam from natural fractures in ancient volcanic rock. California, Nevada, Utah, and Hawaii have significant geothermal resources. On the biomass side, over 200 U.S. landfills capture methane gas from decomposing waste, either burning it for electricity or upgrading it to renewable natural gas. The EPA's Landfill Methane Outreach Program (LMOP) has helped over 500 projects develop, reducing methane emissions (which is 80× more potent than CO₂ over 20 years) while producing energy.

### Renewable Energy: The Big Picture
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** renewables-big-picture
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** energy transition · decarbonization · grid reliability · energy security · DOE goals
**Summary:** Renewable energy is growing rapidly and now provides the cheapest electricity in history, but a complete transition requires solving storage, transmission, and grid reliability challenges.

#### The Scale of the Transition
In 2023, renewables generated about 21% of U.S. electricity (wind: ~10%, solar: ~6%, hydro: ~6%, biomass + geothermal: ~2%). The DOE's "Solar Futures Study" projects solar alone could provide up to 40% of U.S. electricity by 2035 with appropriate policy and investment. The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) of 2022 provided the largest climate investment in U.S. history — approximately $370 billion in clean energy tax credits and investments — accelerating renewable deployment dramatically. DOE's goal is 100% clean electricity by 2035.

#### Remaining Challenges
The principal remaining challenges for a fully renewable grid are: (1) Long-duration energy storage — batteries store 4–8 hours well, but multi-day and seasonal storage remains expensive; (2) Transmission — the best wind and solar resources are often far from population centers, requiring new long-distance transmission lines; (3) Grid stability — managing frequency and voltage with fewer large spinning generators requires new inverter-based resources and grid-forming controls; (4) Manufacturing and supply chains — scaling up solar, wind, and battery manufacturing fast enough requires vast quantities of silicon, lithium, cobalt, rare earth elements, and steel, raising concerns about mining impacts and supply chain security.
