## Subtopic: Food Webs & Energy Flow

### Producers, Consumers, and Decomposers
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** producers-consumers-decomposers
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** producer · consumer · decomposer · trophic level
**Summary:** All organisms in an ecosystem are classified by how they obtain energy: producers make their own, consumers eat others, and decomposers break down dead matter.

#### Producers: Life's Foundation
*Producers* (autotrophs) make their own food using energy from sunlight or chemical reactions. On land, plants are the primary producers. In the ocean, phytoplankton (microscopic algae and cyanobacteria) produce most of Earth's oxygen and support nearly all marine food webs. In deep-sea hydrothermal vents, *chemoautotrophs* (bacteria that use chemical energy from hydrogen sulfide) are the producers — there is no sunlight. Producers form the base of every food chain.

#### Consumers: Energy Movers
*Consumers* (heterotrophs) cannot make their own food and must eat other organisms. *Primary consumers* (herbivores) eat producers: deer eat grass, caterpillars eat leaves, zooplankton eat phytoplankton. *Secondary consumers* eat primary consumers: frogs eat insects, small fish eat zooplankton. *Tertiary consumers* eat secondary consumers: hawks eat frogs, large tuna eat small fish. *Omnivores* eat both producers and consumers (bears, humans, raccoons).

#### Decomposers: Nature's Recyclers
*Decomposers* (primarily bacteria and fungi) break down dead organisms and waste, releasing nutrients back into the soil and water. Without decomposers, nutrients would be locked in dead matter and unavailable to producers. Decomposers are critical to biogeochemical cycles (carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus). Earthworms, millipedes, and some beetles are *detritivores* — they eat dead matter and fragment it, making it easier for true decomposers to break down.

#### Trophic Levels
A *trophic level* is a position in a food chain. Producers = Level 1; primary consumers = Level 2; secondary consumers = Level 3; tertiary consumers = Level 4. Most food chains have 3–5 trophic levels. Each level is less productive than the one below it — energy is lost at each transfer, which limits how many trophic levels are possible.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between a producer, a primary consumer, and a secondary consumer?
2. Why would an ecosystem collapse without decomposers?
3. Why do most food chains have only 3–5 trophic levels?

### Energy Flow: The 10% Rule
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** energy-flow-ten-percent-rule
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** 10% rule · energy pyramid · biomass · trophic efficiency
**Summary:** Only about 10% of energy at one trophic level is available to the next level, which explains why food chains are short and why carnivores are rarer than herbivores.

#### Where Does Energy Go?
When a deer eats grass, it doesn't capture all the grass's energy. About 90% is lost: some grass is indigestible (cellulose), some energy is used in the deer's own metabolism (respiration), and some is lost as heat. Only ~10% is incorporated into new deer tissue (biomass) and available to a predator that eats the deer. This is the *10% rule* or *rule of trophic efficiency*.

#### The Energy Pyramid
An *energy pyramid* (or ecological pyramid) shows the amount of energy (usually measured in kilocalories or kilojoules) at each trophic level. The base (producers) is always widest. Each level above has ~10% of the energy of the level below. Example: if producers have 10,000 kcal, primary consumers have ~1,000 kcal, secondary consumers ~100 kcal, tertiary consumers ~10 kcal.

#### Why Carnivores Are Rare
The 10% rule means that a given area of land can support far more herbivores than carnivores, and far more carnivores than apex predators. A 10,000 kg area of grass can support ~1,000 kg of deer, which can support ~100 kg of wolves. Apex predators (great white sharks, Bengal tigers, polar bears) require enormous territories to find enough prey. This is why they are naturally rare and extremely vulnerable to habitat loss.

#### Biomass Pyramids
A *biomass pyramid* shows the total mass of living organisms at each trophic level. Like energy pyramids, they are usually widest at the base. However, some aquatic ecosystems have *inverted biomass pyramids* because phytoplankton reproduce so quickly that a small biomass supports a much larger biomass of zooplankton — the biomass measured at one moment doesn't capture the rapid turnover.

#### Review Questions
1. What happens to the ~90% of energy that is NOT passed to the next trophic level?
2. If producers in a field have 50,000 kcal of energy, how much energy is available to tertiary consumers?
3. Why do apex predators require large territories?

### Food Chains and Food Webs
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** food-chains-food-webs
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** food chain · food web · keystone species · trophic cascade
**Summary:** Food webs represent the complex reality of feeding relationships in ecosystems, and the removal of key species can trigger cascading effects throughout the web.

#### Food Chain vs. Food Web
A *food chain* is a simple, linear sequence: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk. Real ecosystems are far more complex: a grasshopper might be eaten by a frog, a spider, a bird, or a lizard. A food *web* shows all feeding relationships in an ecosystem — interconnected food chains. Webs are more realistic and more stable: if one prey species declines, a predator with multiple prey options can switch.

#### Keystone Species
A *keystone species* has a disproportionately large effect on its ecosystem relative to its abundance. Remove a keystone species and the ecosystem dramatically changes. Sea otters are a classic example: they eat sea urchins, which eat kelp. Without sea otters, urchin populations explode, kelp forests are destroyed, and all the species that depend on kelp (fish, seabirds, seals) lose habitat. Sea otters are not the most abundant species, but they hold the community together.

#### Trophic Cascades
A *trophic cascade* occurs when a change at one trophic level ripples up or down the food web. When wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone in 1995, elk behavior changed — elk avoided grazing in open areas where wolves could easily catch them. This allowed vegetation to recover along riverbanks, which stabilized stream banks, changed water flow, and even affected fish populations. The wolf's reintroduction changed the physical geography of the park — a dramatic cascade.

#### Stability Through Diversity
Food webs with more species and more feeding connections are more stable. If a spider web has only one prey and it disappears, the spider starves. If it has many prey options, losing one is manageable. Biodiversity increases ecosystem resilience — the ability to recover from disturbance.

#### Review Questions
1. Why is a food web more realistic than a food chain?
2. What is a keystone species? Give one example.
3. How did wolf reintroduction at Yellowstone trigger a trophic cascade?

### Energy Flow in an Aquatic Ecosystem
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** energy-flow-aquatic-ecosystem
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** phytoplankton · zooplankton · marine food web · 10% rule application
**Summary:** Apply food web and energy flow concepts to a marine ecosystem, tracing energy from phytoplankton to apex predators.

#### The Ocean Food Web
Marine food webs begin with *phytoplankton* (microscopic algae and cyanobacteria) that photosynthesize near the surface. *Zooplankton* (tiny animals and animal-like protists, like copepods and krill) are primary consumers. Small fish (anchovies, herring, sardines) eat zooplankton. Larger fish (tuna, cod) eat small fish. Apex predators (sharks, orcas, polar bears) are at the top.

#### Applying the 10% Rule
If phytoplankton in a section of ocean have 1,000,000 kcal: → zooplankton have ~100,000 kcal → small fish have ~10,000 kcal → tuna have ~1,000 kcal → great white shark has ~100 kcal. This is why overfishing tuna has enormous consequences: removing one trophic level disrupts all others. It's also why it's ecologically "expensive" to eat tuna (4 trophic levels from producers) compared to eating anchovies (2 levels).

#### Why Oceans Are Productive but Fragile
Oceans cover 71% of Earth's surface and produce ~50% of global oxygen. Despite their vast size, ocean food webs can collapse rapidly — overfishing of a keystone species, ocean acidification reducing phytoplankton, or plastic pollution blocking sunlight can ripple through the entire web. The 1990s collapse of Atlantic cod in the Grand Banks (from millions of tons of fish to near-zero in decades) demonstrates how quickly overfishing can destroy a food web.

#### Review Questions
1. In a marine food web, what organism plays the role of producer? What plays the role of primary consumer?
2. If phytoplankton have 500,000 kcal, how much energy is available to tuna (assuming tuna are tertiary consumers)?
3. Why did the collapse of Atlantic cod have broader ecosystem consequences?

### Tracing Energy in a Prairie Ecosystem
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** energy-flow-prairie-ecosystem
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** prairie food web · energy calculation · ecosystem services
**Summary:** Trace energy through a prairie food web and calculate the consequences of removing or adding a species.

#### The Prairie Food Web
A North American tallgrass prairie supports: producers (grasses, wildflowers, legumes) → primary consumers (grasshoppers, prairie dogs, bison) → secondary consumers (badgers, coyotes, ferruginous hawks) → tertiary consumers (great horned owls, golden eagles, historically: wolves). Decomposers (bacteria, fungi, dung beetles) recycle nutrients and are essential to soil health.

#### Calculation Practice
If prairie grasses have 500,000 kcal in a 1-hectare area: (a) How much energy is available to prairie dogs (primary consumers)? (b) How much to badgers (secondary)? (c) How much to golden eagles (tertiary)? Answers: (a) 50,000 kcal, (b) 5,000 kcal, (c) 500 kcal. This is why a single hectare can support thousands of grasshoppers, hundreds of prairie dogs, dozens of badgers, but only a fraction of a golden eagle's territory.

#### What Happens When Bison Are Removed?
Historically, bison grazed prairie grasses, keeping them short and stimulating root growth. Their wallowing created muddy depressions that became ponds. Their dung fertilized soil. When bison were eliminated from most of the Great Plains in the 1800s, grass species composition changed, ponds dried up, and many species dependent on bison-disturbed habitat declined. Bison were a keystone species of the prairie ecosystem.

#### Review Questions
1. Identify two producers, two primary consumers, and two secondary consumers in the prairie food web.
2. Calculate the energy available to a golden eagle if prairie grasses have 1,000,000 kcal.
3. In what ways was the bison a keystone species of the North American prairie?

## Subtopic: Biomes & Habitats

### What Is a Biome?
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** what-is-a-biome
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** biome · climate · vegetation zones · terrestrial biomes
**Summary:** A biome is a large geographic area characterized by specific climate conditions that support characteristic communities of plants and animals.

#### Biomes Are Defined by Climate
A *biome* is a large-scale community of organisms adapted to a particular climate. The two key climate factors that determine biomes are *temperature* and *precipitation* (rainfall). Temperature is largely determined by latitude (distance from the equator) and altitude (elevation). Tropical regions near the equator are warm year-round; polar regions are cold year-round. Precipitation determines whether an area is forest, grassland, or desert.

#### The Major Terrestrial Biomes
Tropical rainforest: near equator, high rainfall (>200 cm/year), warm, highest biodiversity of any biome (~50% of all species in ~6% of Earth's land). Temperate deciduous forest: moderate rainfall, four distinct seasons, trees lose leaves in fall (oak, maple, beech). Taiga (boreal forest): long cold winters, cone-bearing trees (conifers — spruce, fir, pine), largest terrestrial biome by area. Tundra: extreme cold, permafrost, very low precipitation, no trees, short growing season. Tropical savanna: seasonal rainfall, dominated by grasses with scattered trees (acacia), large herbivore populations (Africa). Temperate grassland: moderate climate, few trees, deep fertile soils, high winds (North American prairie). Desert: <25 cm precipitation/year, extreme temperature swings, adaptations for water conservation. Chaparral (Mediterranean shrubland): hot dry summers, mild wet winters, fire-adapted shrubs.

#### Aquatic Biomes
Aquatic biomes cover ~75% of Earth's surface. Freshwater biomes include rivers, lakes, wetlands, and ponds. Marine biomes include oceans (open ocean, coral reefs, intertidal zones, deep sea). Estuaries — where freshwater rivers meet saltwater ocean — are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth (nursery grounds for many marine species). Wetlands (swamps, marshes, bogs) have some of the highest biodiversity and provide flood control, water filtration, and carbon storage.

#### Why Biome Patterns Are Predictable
If you travel from the equator to the poles, you pass through the same sequence of biomes you'd pass through climbing a tall mountain from base to summit. Both involve decreasing temperature: rainforest → grassland/forest → taiga-like zone → tundra/snowpack. This predictable pattern reflects the central role of climate in determining biome type.

#### Review Questions
1. What two climate factors primarily determine which biome occupies a region?
2. Name four terrestrial biomes and describe the characteristic climate of each.
3. Why do biome zones near the equator mirror biome zones at the base of tall mountains?

### Tropical Rainforests and Tundra: Extremes of Life
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** rainforests-tundra-extremes
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** tropical rainforest · tundra · biodiversity · permafrost
**Summary:** Tropical rainforests and tundra represent opposite extremes of Earth's biomes — one is the most biodiverse, the other among the most challenging environments for life.

#### Tropical Rainforests
Tropical rainforests are found in a band around the equator: Amazon Basin (South America), Congo Basin (Africa), Southeast Asian islands. They receive 200–400 cm of rain per year and are warm year-round (~25–30°C). They contain an estimated 50% of all terrestrial species, despite covering only ~6% of Earth's land surface. The Amazon alone has ~40,000 plant species, ~1,300 bird species, and ~3,000 fish species.

#### Rainforest Structure
Rainforests are stratified into vertical layers: the *emergent layer* (tallest trees, >50 m, full sunlight), the *canopy* (interconnecting branches, ~20–40 m, where most life exists), the *understory* (shrubs, small trees, shade-adapted plants), and the *forest floor* (dark, humid, fast decomposition, few plants). Most biodiversity is in the canopy, not the forest floor. The forest floor is NOT the base of the food web; the canopy is where most photosynthesis occurs.

#### The Tundra
The Arctic tundra is found in Alaska, Canada, Siberia, and Scandinavia. Temperatures are extreme (−30 to 10°C), precipitation is low (15–25 cm/year — less than many deserts), and the growing season is only 6–10 weeks. *Permafrost* — permanently frozen soil below the surface — prevents tree growth and limits rooting depth. Vegetation is dominated by mosses, lichens, sedges, and dwarf shrubs.

#### Tundra Species Adaptations
Animals in the tundra show dramatic adaptations: Arctic foxes change fur color seasonally (brown in summer, white in winter) for camouflage; musk oxen have thick underfur (*qiviut*) for insulation; caribou migrate thousands of kilometers to find food; lemmings remain active under snow. Plants are low-growing to avoid wind, and many are cushion-shaped to trap heat.

#### Permafrost and Climate Change
Permafrost stores vast amounts of organic carbon — dead plant material that didn't decompose because of cold. As climate warms, permafrost thaws, releasing this carbon as CO₂ and methane (a potent greenhouse gas). This creates a *positive feedback loop*: warming → permafrost thaws → more greenhouse gases → more warming. The Arctic is warming about 4× faster than the global average.

#### Review Questions
1. What percentage of terrestrial species live in tropical rainforests?
2. What is permafrost, and why does its thawing matter for climate change?
3. Name the four vertical layers of a tropical rainforest from top to bottom.

### Grasslands, Deserts, and Temperate Forests
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** grasslands-deserts-temperate-forests
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** savanna · temperate grassland · desert · temperate deciduous forest
**Summary:** Grasslands, deserts, and temperate forests each have distinctive climate patterns and characteristic species adapted to their conditions.

#### Savannas
The African savanna receives 50–130 cm of rain per year, but with a pronounced dry season. The mix of wet and dry seasons supports both grasses and scattered trees (acacia, baobab). Savannas support the largest land animal migrations on Earth — over 1 million wildebeest, zebra, and gazelle follow seasonal rains across East Africa. Cheetahs, lions, leopards, elephants, giraffes, and hippos all depend on the savanna ecosystem.

#### Temperate Grasslands
North American prairies, South American pampas, African veldts, and Eurasian steppes are temperate grasslands. They receive 25–75 cm of rain per year — enough for grasses but not trees (trees can grow along river corridors). Deep, fertile soils (mollisols) developed under prairie grass are among Earth's most productive agricultural soils. The American Midwest (the "Corn Belt") was originally tallgrass prairie. Less than 1% of original North American tallgrass prairie remains.

#### Deserts
Deserts cover ~33% of Earth's land surface. Hot deserts (Sahara, Arabian, Sonoran) have daytime temperatures >50°C and near-freezing nights. Cold deserts (Gobi, Atacama, Antarctic interior) are cold year-round. Both receive <25 cm/year precipitation. Desert organisms conserve water through structural (succulent stems, waxy skin), behavioral (nocturnal activity), and physiological (concentrated urine, metabolic water) adaptations.

#### Temperate Deciduous Forests
Found in eastern North America, Europe, and East Asia. Four distinct seasons: warm summers, cold winters. Deciduous trees (oak, maple, hickory, beech) lose leaves in fall to conserve water and energy during winter. The leaf litter decomposes over winter and spring, enriching the soil. These forests were largely cleared for agriculture — much of the eastern U.S., Europe, and China were once covered by temperate deciduous forest.

#### Review Questions
1. What distinguishes a savanna from a temperate grassland?
2. Why do deserts have extreme temperature swings between day and night?
3. Why do deciduous trees lose their leaves in autumn?

### Biome Distribution: Why Here and Not There?
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** biome-distribution-why
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** climate graph · biome prediction · latitude · rain shadow effect
**Summary:** Climate graphs (climatographs) show monthly temperature and precipitation patterns that predict biome type; the rain shadow effect explains why some areas are unexpectedly dry.

#### Reading a Climate Graph
A *climate graph* (climatograph) shows monthly average temperature (line graph) and monthly precipitation (bar graph) for one location. By examining temperature range (seasonal variation) and precipitation amount and timing, you can identify the likely biome: high, uniform temperature + uniform high precipitation → tropical rainforest; large temperature swings + moderate precipitation → temperate deciduous forest; extreme cold year-round + low precipitation → tundra.

#### The Rain Shadow Effect
Mountain ranges create *rain shadows*. As moist air rises over a mountain (windward side), it cools and releases precipitation (often as rain or snow). By the time the air descends on the other side (leeward side), it is dry and warm. The leeward side of the mountain becomes arid. The Cascade Mountains create a dramatic rain shadow: the Olympic Peninsula (windward) receives ~380 cm/year, while eastern Washington (leeward) receives ~20 cm/year — desert conditions. The Great Basin Desert exists in the rain shadow of the Sierra Nevada.

#### Applying Climate Data
If a climate graph shows: average temperature 25–30°C year-round, monthly precipitation 15–30 cm throughout the year → tropical rainforest. If it shows: summer 25°C, winter −5°C, summer maximum precipitation, 75 cm/year total → temperate deciduous forest. If it shows: average temperature −10 to 5°C, precipitation 15–25 cm/year → tundra.

#### Review Questions
1. What two variables are shown on a climate graph (climatograph)?
2. Explain the rain shadow effect. What happens to precipitation on the windward vs. leeward sides of a mountain?
3. If a location has 28°C temperatures year-round and 250 cm of rain annually, what biome is it likely in?

### Comparing Biomes Across Continents
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** comparing-biomes-across-continents
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** convergent evolution in biomes · biome comparison · species comparison
**Summary:** Similar climate conditions on different continents produce similar biomes with ecologically equivalent but evolutionarily distinct species — a demonstration of convergent evolution.

#### Convergent Evolution in Biomes
The Sonoran Desert (North America) and the Namib Desert (Africa) have similar climate conditions, and their plants and animals show striking similarities: both have tall columnar cacti-like plants (though American saguaro cacti and African euphorbs are not related), both have burrowing rodents, both have venomous sit-and-wait predators. This is *convergent evolution* — similar environments select for similar adaptations in unrelated lineages.

#### Chaparral Around the World
Chaparral (California), maquis (Mediterranean), fynbos (South Africa), matorral (Chile), and mallee (Australia) all share the same climate: hot dry summers, mild wet winters. They all have fire-adapted, drought-tolerant shrubs with hard, waxy leaves. Yet each region has evolved its own unique set of plant species. The climate is the same; the evolutionary history is different.

#### Grasslands as Ecological Equivalents
Each continent's grasslands support large herbivores: Africa has wildebeest and zebra; North America historically had bison; South America has guanacos; Australia has kangaroos. These animals play ecologically equivalent roles — grazing, moving, fertilizing — but are not closely related. Their similarities reflect convergent adaptations to similar environments (grassland, seasonal drought, high plant productivity).

#### Review Questions
1. What is an ecological equivalent? Give one example from two different continents.
2. Why do chaparral biomes around the world have similar plant forms, even though their species are different?
3. How does convergent evolution in biomes support the idea that environment shapes adaptation?

## Subtopic: Population Dynamics

### What Is a Population?
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** what-is-a-population
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** population · density · distribution · carrying capacity
**Summary:** A population is a group of individuals of the same species in the same area, and its size is regulated by birth, death, immigration, and emigration.

#### Defining a Population
A *population* is all individuals of the same species living in a defined area at the same time. All the white-tailed deer in Yellowstone National Park are one population. All the oak trees in a forest are one population. Populations can be studied by size (how many individuals), *density* (number per unit area — individuals/km²), *distribution* (how individuals are spread across the habitat), and age structure (proportion of young vs. old individuals).

#### What Changes Population Size?
Population size (N) changes based on four factors: birth rate (B), death rate (D), immigration (I, individuals moving in), and emigration (E, individuals moving out). In a simple model: ΔN = B − D + I − E. If births + immigration exceed deaths + emigration, the population grows. If deaths + emigration exceed births + immigration, it declines.

#### Carrying Capacity
The *carrying capacity* (K) is the maximum population size an environment can sustain given available resources (food, water, shelter, space). At carrying capacity, birth rates equal death rates and the population is stable. When a population exceeds K, resources become limiting — starvation, disease, and competition increase death rates until the population falls back toward K. Carrying capacity is dynamic — it changes if resource availability changes.

#### Population Distribution Patterns
Populations can be distributed *randomly* (no pattern — dandelion seeds blown by wind), *uniformly* (evenly spaced — trees competing for light, territorial animals), or *clumped* (groups — schools of fish, herds of wildebeest, human cities). Clumped distribution is most common and results from resource distribution, social behavior, or reproductive patterns.

### Population Growth Models
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** population-growth-models
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** exponential growth · logistic growth · J-curve · S-curve
**Summary:** Populations grow exponentially when resources are unlimited but follow logistic (S-shaped) growth when limited by carrying capacity.

#### Exponential Growth: The J-Curve
When resources are unlimited and there are no predators or disease, a population grows *exponentially* — each generation is larger than the last by the same percentage. The graph of exponential growth looks like a J-curve (slow at first, then increasingly steep). Bacteria in a new culture medium, deer introduced to an island with abundant food, or humans during the Industrial Revolution are examples of near-exponential growth.

#### Exponential Growth Is Unsustainable
Exponential growth cannot continue forever. A single bacterium dividing every 20 minutes would, in 36 hours, produce enough cells to cover the Earth 2 feet deep. This never happens because resources run out, disease spreads, and waste accumulates. Something always limits growth. That "something" is the *limiting factor*.

#### Logistic Growth: The S-Curve
Real populations follow *logistic growth* — they grow rapidly at first (when far below carrying capacity), slow as they approach K, and stabilize near K. The graph is S-shaped (sigmoid curve). As population density increases, *density-dependent* limiting factors (competition, predation, disease) become more intense, reducing birth rates and increasing death rates. The population levels off at K.

#### Limiting Factors
*Density-dependent* limiting factors become stronger as population density increases: disease (spreads faster in crowded populations), competition (for food, water, mates), predation (predators may focus on abundant prey), and waste accumulation. *Density-independent* factors affect populations regardless of density: natural disasters (hurricanes, fires, drought), extreme temperatures, human activities. A wildfire kills the same fraction of a population whether it's dense or sparse.

### Predator-Prey Dynamics
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** predator-prey-dynamics
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** predator-prey cycle · Lotka-Volterra · boom-bust cycle
**Summary:** Predator and prey populations are linked — as prey increase, predators increase; then predators reduce prey, causing predators to decline, allowing prey to recover.

#### The Predator-Prey Cycle
The classic predator-prey relationship produces oscillating populations. When prey (e.g., snowshoe hares) are abundant, predators (e.g., lynx) have plenty of food and reproduce more. As predator populations increase, they eat more prey. Prey populations decline. With less food, predators decline. With fewer predators, prey recover. The cycle repeats, typically every 8–11 years for the hare-lynx system in Canada's boreal forests.

#### The Lotka-Volterra Model
The *Lotka-Volterra model* mathematically describes predator-prey cycles. It predicts that predator and prey populations will oscillate with the prey peak leading the predator peak by about a quarter cycle. This is exactly what 90 years of Hudson's Bay Company fur trading records showed for hare and lynx populations — a natural, data-rich demonstration of the model.

#### Complications in Real Systems
The hare-lynx cycle is also influenced by hare food sources (hares eat spruce twigs — when spruce is heavily grazed, hare populations decline even without lynx). Real predator-prey systems are embedded in complex webs. Multiple prey species, multiple predators, and fluctuating environments make simple Lotka-Volterra dynamics messier in nature.

#### Top-Down vs. Bottom-Up Control
*Top-down control* occurs when predators regulate prey populations (predator abundance determines prey abundance). *Bottom-up control* occurs when food supply regulates herbivore populations (plant productivity determines herbivore abundance). Most ecosystems show both: predators control prey numbers, but ultimately food supply limits how many prey (and thus how many predators) an ecosystem can support.

3. What is the difference between top-down and bottom-up population control?

### Human Population Growth
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** human-population-growth
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** demographic transition · human carrying capacity · age structure
**Summary:** Human population grew slowly for most of history, then surged exponentially after the Industrial Revolution — with complex implications for Earth's carrying capacity.

#### The Human Population Story
For most of human history, population grew very slowly due to high birth rates AND high death rates (disease, famine, infant mortality). Around 1800, the human population reached 1 billion. Advances in medicine, agriculture, and sanitation dramatically lowered death rates while birth rates remained high → explosive growth. The population hit 2 billion by 1927, 4 billion by 1975, and 8 billion by 2022 — doubling in 47 years.

#### The Demographic Transition Model
The *demographic transition model* describes how countries move through four stages: Stage 1 (high birth rate, high death rate — slow growth); Stage 2 (high birth rate, declining death rate — rapid growth); Stage 3 (declining birth rate, low death rate — slowing growth); Stage 4 (low birth rate, low death rate — near-zero growth). Most high-income countries are in Stage 4; many low-income countries are in Stage 2–3, driving most of current population growth.

#### Age Structure Pyramids
An *age structure pyramid* shows the proportion of a population in different age groups (young, reproductive, old). A wide base = many young = rapidly growing population. A narrow, rectangular shape = stable population. An inverted pyramid = declining population. Japan has a rapidly aging, declining population; Nigeria has a broad-based, rapidly growing pyramid.

#### Earth's Carrying Capacity
Scientists debate Earth's carrying capacity for humans. Estimates range from 4 billion to 16+ billion, depending on assumptions about diet (meat-heavy diets require far more resources than plant-based diets), energy use, and technology. At current consumption rates, humans may already be exceeding sustainable resource use in some dimensions (freshwater, fish stocks, atmospheric carbon capacity).

1. What two changes caused explosive human population growth after 1800?
2. Describe the four stages of the demographic transition model.
3. Why is Earth's carrying capacity for humans difficult to estimate precisely?

### Population Growth in Wildlife Management
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** population-growth-wildlife-management
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** wildlife management · minimum viable population · population viability
**Summary:** Understanding population dynamics is essential for wildlife conservation — managing hunting, invasive species, and habitat to maintain viable populations.

#### Minimum Viable Population
The *minimum viable population* (MVP) is the smallest population size at which a species can survive long-term. Too-small populations suffer from *inbreeding depression* (mating between close relatives reduces genetic diversity and increases harmful traits), *demographic stochasticity* (random variation in birth and death rates becomes significant), and vulnerability to catastrophic events. The Florida panther population fell below 30 individuals in the 1990s and began showing genetic defects — crossbreeding with Texas pumas was used to restore genetic diversity.

#### Overpopulation Without Predators
When predators are removed, prey populations can explode. White-tailed deer in the eastern U.S. number around 30 million — largely because wolves and mountain lions were eliminated. Deer overgrazing prevents forest regeneration, harms bird populations, increases tick-borne disease transmission, and causes millions of vehicle collisions annually. Hunting is used as a population management tool in the absence of natural predators.

#### Invasive Species and Population Explosions
Invasive species arrive in new environments without natural predators or diseases. Asian carp in the Mississippi River have no significant predators; they grow rapidly, outcompete native fish for zooplankton, and have spread through much of the river system. Zebra mussels in the Great Lakes filter enormous quantities of phytoplankton, disrupting the food web. Without natural population controls, invasive species can grow exponentially.

## Subtopic: Human Impact on Ecosystems

### How Humans Change Ecosystems
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** how-humans-change-ecosystems
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** habitat destruction · deforestation · fragmentation · invasive species
**Summary:** Human activities — habitat destruction, pollution, invasive species, and climate change — are the primary drivers of the current biodiversity crisis.

#### Habitat Destruction: The Biggest Threat
Habitat destruction is the leading cause of biodiversity loss worldwide. When forests are cleared for agriculture, wetlands drained for development, or coral reefs dynamited for fish, the organisms that live there lose their homes. The *species-area relationship* describes how species richness decreases with habitat area: halving a habitat typically reduces species richness by about 10–15%. Tropical rainforests — cleared at ~20 million acres per year — house the highest concentration of endangered species.

#### Habitat Fragmentation
Even when habitat isn't completely destroyed, *fragmentation* — dividing continuous habitat into isolated patches — is damaging. Animals that need large territories (wolves, mountain lions, grizzly bears) cannot survive in small fragments. Populations become isolated, reducing genetic diversity. *Wildlife corridors* (strips of habitat connecting fragments, like Yellowstone-to-Yukon) help animals move between patches, maintaining genetic flow.

#### Invasive Species
*Invasive species* are non-native organisms that spread rapidly in new environments where they lack natural predators, diseases, or competitors. Examples: zebra mussels in the Great Lakes (filter phytoplankton, starve native species), kudzu vine in the southeastern U.S. (grows over and kills native plants), Burmese pythons in the Florida Everglades (eat native mammals and birds). The Convention on Biological Diversity lists invasive species as the second-greatest threat to biodiversity (after habitat loss).

#### Overexploitation
Overexploitation — harvesting organisms faster than they can reproduce — has driven many species to extinction or near-extinction: passenger pigeons (extinct 1914 after being killed in the billions), American bison (reduced from 60 million to ~1,000 by 1890), Atlantic cod (near-collapse from overfishing). Sustainable harvesting matches harvest rates to population growth rates; overexploitation exceeds population growth, causing collapse.

1. What is the species-area relationship?
2. How does habitat fragmentation harm species differently than total habitat destruction?
3. Why do invasive species often cause ecological damage in their new environments?

### Pollution and Its Effects on Ecosystems
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** pollution-effects-ecosystems
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** bioaccumulation · biomagnification · eutrophication · acid rain
**Summary:** Pollution enters ecosystems and can concentrate through food webs (biomagnification) or cause dramatic ecosystem disruption through nutrient overloading and acidification.

#### Bioaccumulation and Biomagnification
*Bioaccumulation* is the buildup of a toxic substance in an individual organism over time (an organism absorbs the toxin faster than it can excrete it). *Biomagnification* is the increasing concentration of a toxin at successively higher trophic levels. DDT (an insecticide) is the classic example: phytoplankton had 0.000003 ppm DDT; small fish had 0.5 ppm; large fish had 2 ppm; bald eagles had 25 ppm — nearly 10 million× concentrated relative to the water. DDT thinned eagle eggshells, causing population collapse. DDT was banned in the U.S. in 1972.

#### Eutrophication
*Eutrophication* is nutrient enrichment of a water body (typically from agricultural fertilizer runoff or sewage) that causes algal blooms, depletion of oxygen, and fish kills. The sequence: excess nitrogen and phosphorus → algae bloom rapidly → algae die and decompose → bacteria decompose dead algae, consuming oxygen → hypoxic (low-oxygen) "dead zone" forms → fish and other aerobic organisms die. The Gulf of Mexico "dead zone" — fed by fertilizers from the Mississippi River basin — covers ~20,000 km² in summer.

#### Acid Rain
Burning fossil fuels releases sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ). These react with water vapor to form sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) and nitric acid (HNO₃), falling as *acid rain*. Acid rain: lowers pH of lakes and streams (killing fish and amphibians), leaches nutrients from forest soils, damages limestone structures, and can harm forests directly. The 1970 Clean Air Act and 1990 amendments significantly reduced acid rain in the U.S.

#### Plastic Pollution
Plastics break down into *microplastics* (particles <5 mm) that are ingested by marine animals at all trophic levels. Seabirds feed plastic to chicks; turtles mistake plastic bags for jellyfish; baleen whales filter microplastics with krill. The Great Pacific Garbage Patch (actually dispersed plastic debris covering ~1.6 million km² in the Pacific) is a concentration zone of floating debris. Microplastics have been found in human blood, placenta, and drinking water.

### Climate Change and Ecosystems
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** climate-change-ecosystems
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** greenhouse effect · global warming · coral bleaching · phenological mismatch
**Summary:** Human-caused climate change is shifting ecosystems worldwide, with effects including coral bleaching, species range shifts, and phenological mismatches.

#### The Greenhouse Effect (Natural vs. Enhanced)
The *greenhouse effect* is a natural process: greenhouse gases (water vapor, CO₂, methane, nitrous oxide) in the atmosphere absorb infrared radiation emitted by Earth's surface and re-emit it, warming the planet. Without the natural greenhouse effect, Earth would be about −18°C (rather than the current ~15°C average). The *enhanced greenhouse effect* is the amplification of this warming by human-released greenhouse gases — primarily CO₂ from burning fossil fuels (now ~420 ppm, up from 280 ppm pre-industrial) and methane from agriculture and landfills.

#### Effects on Ecosystems
Warming temperatures cause: (1) *Coral bleaching* — coral reefs expel their symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) when water gets too warm, turning white and often dying (the Great Barrier Reef has lost >50% of its coral since 1995); (2) *Species range shifts* — organisms move poleward or to higher elevations as their habitat warms (mountain species can run out of habitat); (3) *Phenological mismatches* — changes in the timing of biological events (migration, flowering, hatching) can cause species to become out of sync (birds arrive for breeding season before their insect prey hatch).

#### Ocean Acidification
The ocean absorbs ~25% of human CO₂ emissions. CO₂ dissolves in water to form carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), lowering ocean pH. Ocean pH has fallen from ~8.2 to ~8.1 since industrialization (a 26% increase in acidity because pH is logarithmic). This *ocean acidification* makes it harder for corals, oysters, clams, and other organisms to build calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) shells and skeletons — with cascading effects throughout marine food webs.

1. What is the difference between the natural greenhouse effect and the enhanced greenhouse effect?

### Conservation Success Stories
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** conservation-success-stories
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** Endangered Species Act · wildlife recovery · habitat restoration
**Summary:** Conservation efforts have successfully recovered many species from near-extinction, demonstrating that targeted intervention can reverse biodiversity loss.

#### The Bald Eagle
The bald eagle was reduced to just 417 breeding pairs in the lower 48 states by 1963, primarily due to DDT's eggshell-thinning effects, hunting, and habitat loss. After DDT was banned in 1972 and the Endangered Species Act (1973) provided legal protection, bald eagle populations recovered to over 9,000 breeding pairs by 2006, when the species was removed from the Endangered Species List — one of the greatest conservation success stories.

#### The American Bison
American bison were reduced from ~60 million to fewer than 1,000 by 1890 due to commercial hunting. Conservation efforts began in the early 1900s; today there are ~500,000 bison (mostly on ranches, with ~30,000 in public herds). The Prairie Wind Project and American Prairie Reserve are working to restore large wild bison herds across the Northern Great Plains, with associated grassland and wildlife restoration.

#### California Condor
The California condor (*Gymnogyps californianus*) went entirely extinct in the wild in 1987 (only 27 individuals remained, all captured for captive breeding). Through intensive captive breeding and reintroduction, the wild population has grown to over 300 individuals as of 2023. Threats (lead poisoning from spent ammunition, power line collisions) are still managed. The condor is an example of how extreme intervention can prevent extinction.

#### The Endangered Species Act
The U.S. Endangered Species Act (1973) prohibits "take" of listed species (killing, harming, harassing), requires federal agencies to avoid jeopardizing listed species, and protects critical habitat. Of species listed, ~98% have avoided extinction — though many have not yet recovered to healthy population sizes. Debates continue about balancing species protection with economic development.

### Human Impact in Your Watershed
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** human-impact-your-watershed
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** watershed · nonpoint source pollution · riparian buffer · citizen science
**Summary:** Watersheds connect human land use to downstream water quality, and individuals can take concrete actions to reduce their ecological footprint.

#### What Is a Watershed?
A *watershed* (also called a drainage basin) is all the land that drains into a particular water body (river, lake, ocean). Every property is in a watershed — rain that falls on your lawn, street, or farm eventually drains to a creek, river, and ultimately the ocean. Actions on land — applying fertilizers, paving surfaces, changing vegetation — affect downstream water quality.

#### Nonpoint Source Pollution
*Point source pollution* comes from a specific, identifiable source (a pipe, smokestack). *Nonpoint source pollution* (NPS) comes from dispersed sources across a landscape — agricultural fertilizer and pesticide runoff, urban stormwater, pet waste, road salt. NPS is the leading cause of water quality degradation in U.S. waters (EPA). Because it comes from everywhere, it's harder to regulate than point sources.

#### Solutions: What Actually Works
*Riparian buffers* (strips of native vegetation along stream banks) filter runoff before it enters streams. Constructed wetlands can remove >80% of nitrogen from agricultural runoff. Permeable pavement allows stormwater infiltration. Citizen science programs (macroinvertebrate monitoring, water quality testing) help track ecosystem health. At the individual level: reducing fertilizer use, picking up pet waste, washing cars on grass (not pavement), and planting native species reduce NPS pollution.

1. What is a watershed?
2. What is nonpoint source pollution, and why is it harder to regulate than point source pollution?
3. What is a riparian buffer, and how does it reduce water pollution?
