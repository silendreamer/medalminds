#### Defining a Population
A *population* is all individuals of the same species living in a defined area at the same time. All the white-tailed deer in Yellowstone National Park are one population. All the oak trees in a forest are one population. Populations can be studied by size (how many individuals), *density* (number per unit area — individuals/km²), *distribution* (how individuals are spread across the habitat), and age structure (proportion of young vs. old individuals).

#### What Changes Population Size?
Population size (N) changes based on four factors: birth rate (B), death rate (D), immigration (I, individuals moving in), and emigration (E, individuals moving out). In a simple model: ΔN = B − D + I − E. If births + immigration exceed deaths + emigration, the population grows. If deaths + emigration exceed births + immigration, it declines.

#### Carrying Capacity
The *carrying capacity* (K) is the maximum population size an environment can sustain given available resources (food, water, shelter, space). At carrying capacity, birth rates equal death rates and the population is stable. When a population exceeds K, resources become limiting — starvation, disease, and competition increase death rates until the population falls back toward K. Carrying capacity is dynamic — it changes if resource availability changes.

#### Population Distribution Patterns
Populations can be distributed *randomly* (no pattern — dandelion seeds blown by wind), *uniformly* (evenly spaced — trees competing for light, territorial animals), or *clumped* (groups — schools of fish, herds of wildebeest, human cities). Clumped distribution is most common and results from resource distribution, social behavior, or reproductive patterns.
