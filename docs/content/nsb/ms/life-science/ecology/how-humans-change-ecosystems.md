#### Habitat Destruction: The Biggest Threat
Habitat destruction is the leading cause of biodiversity loss worldwide. When forests are cleared for agriculture, wetlands drained for development, or coral reefs dynamited for fish, the organisms that live there lose their homes. The *species-area relationship* describes how species richness decreases with habitat area: halving a habitat typically reduces species richness by about 10–15%. Tropical rainforests — cleared at ~20 million acres per year — house the highest concentration of endangered species.

#### Habitat Fragmentation
Even when habitat isn't completely destroyed, *fragmentation* — dividing continuous habitat into isolated patches — is damaging. Animals that need large territories (wolves, mountain lions, grizzly bears) cannot survive in small fragments. Populations become isolated, reducing genetic diversity. *Wildlife corridors* (strips of habitat connecting fragments, like Yellowstone-to-Yukon) help animals move between patches, maintaining genetic flow.

#### Invasive Species
*Invasive species* are non-native organisms that spread rapidly in new environments where they lack natural predators, diseases, or competitors. Examples: zebra mussels in the Great Lakes (filter phytoplankton, starve native species), kudzu vine in the southeastern U.S. (grows over and kills native plants), Burmese pythons in the Florida Everglades (eat native mammals and birds). The Convention on Biological Diversity lists invasive species as the second-greatest threat to biodiversity (after habitat loss).

#### Overexploitation
Overexploitation — harvesting organisms faster than they can reproduce — has driven many species to extinction or near-extinction: passenger pigeons (extinct 1914 after being killed in the billions), American bison (reduced from 60 million to ~1,000 by 1890), Atlantic cod (near-collapse from overfishing). Sustainable harvesting matches harvest rates to population growth rates; overexploitation exceeds population growth, causing collapse.
