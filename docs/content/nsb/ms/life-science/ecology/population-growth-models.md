#### Exponential Growth: The J-Curve
When resources are unlimited and there are no predators or disease, a population grows *exponentially* — each generation is larger than the last by the same percentage. The graph of exponential growth looks like a J-curve (slow at first, then increasingly steep). Bacteria in a new culture medium, deer introduced to an island with abundant food, or humans during the Industrial Revolution are examples of near-exponential growth.

#### Exponential Growth Is Unsustainable
Exponential growth cannot continue forever. A single bacterium dividing every 20 minutes would, in 36 hours, produce enough cells to cover the Earth 2 feet deep. This never happens because resources run out, disease spreads, and waste accumulates. Something always limits growth. That "something" is the *limiting factor*.

#### Logistic Growth: The S-Curve
Real populations follow *logistic growth* — they grow rapidly at first (when far below carrying capacity), slow as they approach K, and stabilize near K. The graph is S-shaped (sigmoid curve). As population density increases, *density-dependent* limiting factors (competition, predation, disease) become more intense, reducing birth rates and increasing death rates. The population levels off at K.

#### Limiting Factors
*Density-dependent* limiting factors become stronger as population density increases: disease (spreads faster in crowded populations), competition (for food, water, mates), predation (predators may focus on abundant prey), and waste accumulation. *Density-independent* factors affect populations regardless of density: natural disasters (hurricanes, fires, drought), extreme temperatures, human activities. A wildfire kills the same fraction of a population whether it's dense or sparse.

2. What is the difference between density-dependent and density-independent limiting factors?
