#### Invertebrates: The Majority
About 97% of all animal species are invertebrates — animals without a backbone. Major invertebrate phyla include: Arthropoda (insects, spiders, crustaceans — the largest phylum, with over a million species), Mollusca (snails, clams, octopuses), Echinodermata (sea stars, sea urchins — radially symmetrical as adults), Annelida (segmented worms), and Cnidaria (jellyfish, corals, hydra). Sponges (Porifera) are among the simplest animals — no tissues, no organs.

#### Vertebrates: Five Classes
All vertebrates belong to Phylum Chordata and have a backbone (vertebral column). The five traditional vertebrate classes: (1) **Fish** — gills, scales, cold-blooded (includes jawless fish like lampreys, cartilaginous fish like sharks, and bony fish); (2) **Amphibians** — moist skin, begin life in water, cold-blooded (frogs, salamanders); (3) **Reptiles** — dry scaly skin, amniotic eggs, cold-blooded (snakes, lizards, turtles, crocodilians); (4) **Birds** — feathers, warm-blooded, amniotic eggs, endotherms; (5) **Mammals** — hair/fur, warm-blooded, nurse young with milk.

#### Warm-Blooded vs. Cold-Blooded
"Warm-blooded" (endothermic) means the animal generates its own body heat internally. "Cold-blooded" (ectothermic) means the animal's body temperature depends on the environment. Endotherms (birds, mammals) can remain active in cold environments but need more food. Ectotherms (fish, amphibians, reptiles) use less energy but become sluggish in cold. "Cold-blooded" doesn't mean their blood is always cold — a basking lizard may be warmer than a mammal.
