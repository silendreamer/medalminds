#### Darwin's Finches
When Darwin visited the Galápagos Islands in 1835, he collected specimens of finches from different islands. Back in England, ornithologist John Gould identified them as 14 distinct species — all descended from a single ancestral finch species that arrived from South America millions of years ago. Each island and habitat had different food sources, and birds with beaks better suited to those foods survived and reproduced more.

#### Beak as a Tool
On islands with hard seeds, finches with thick, crushing beaks thrived. On islands with flowers, those with long, probing beaks fed on nectar. On islands with insects in bark, finches with narrow, pointed beaks did best. The beak shape is a heritable, structural adaptation. Peter and Rosemary Grant spent 40 years on the Galápagos measuring finch beaks and directly observed beak size shift in a single generation after a drought changed which seeds were available.

#### Connecting to the Big Picture
The Galápagos finches illustrate three key concepts: (1) natural selection acts on existing variation, (2) the environment determines which variant wins, and (3) geographic isolation allows populations to diverge into separate species. They remain one of the most powerful real-world demonstrations of evolution.

#### Review Questions
1. How did geographic isolation contribute to finch diversification on the Galápagos?
2. What specific selection pressure shaped beak diversity among finches?
3. How did the Grant study provide direct evidence of natural selection?
