#### Three Types of Muscle
Skeletal muscle: attached to bones by tendons; voluntary control; striated (striped); can fatigue. Cardiac muscle: forms the heart wall; involuntary; striated; does not fatigue under normal conditions. Smooth muscle: found in hollow organs (stomach, blood vessels, intestines); involuntary; not striated; sustained contractions.

#### The Sarcomere
Skeletal muscle cells (fibers) contain thousands of myofibrils. Each myofibril consists of repeating units called sarcomeres — the basic contractile units. Each sarcomere contains two types of protein filaments: thick filaments (myosin) and thin filaments (actin). The interdigitation of actin and myosin creates the striated (banded) appearance.

#### The Sliding Filament Theory
Contraction occurs when actin and myosin slide past each other, shortening the sarcomere. The mechanism: (1) A nerve signal reaches the muscle via a motor neuron. (2) Acetylcholine is released at the neuromuscular junction. (3) Ca²⁺ is released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum inside the muscle fiber. (4) Ca²⁺ causes troponin/tropomyosin to move, exposing binding sites on actin. (5) Myosin heads attach to actin (cross-bridges) and pull (power stroke), using ATP. (6) ATP breaks the cross-bridge and resets the myosin head. This cycle repeats rapidly.

#### ATP and Muscle Energy
Muscle contraction requires ATP. ATP is produced by: (1) Phosphocreatine (for ~10 seconds of maximum effort). (2) Anaerobic glycolysis (lactic acid pathway — ~2 minutes). (3) Aerobic cellular respiration (long-duration activity). During intense exercise without sufficient oxygen, lactic acid accumulates, causing the burning sensation in muscles.
