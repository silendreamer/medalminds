#### The Six Classes of Nutrients
Nutrients are substances from food that the body uses for energy, growth, and repair: (1) Carbohydrates — primary energy source; broken down to glucose. (2) Proteins — building blocks of cells, enzymes, hormones; broken down to amino acids. (3) Fats — long-term energy storage, insulation, and cell membrane structure. (4) Vitamins — organic compounds needed in small amounts for specific functions. (5) Minerals — inorganic ions needed for many processes (calcium for bones, iron in hemoglobin). (6) Water — most essential; needed for virtually every biochemical reaction.

#### Macronutrients vs. Micronutrients
Macronutrients are needed in large amounts and provide calories: carbohydrates (4 cal/g), proteins (4 cal/g), and fats (9 cal/g). Micronutrients (vitamins and minerals) are needed in small amounts and provide no calories, but are essential for enzyme function, bone health, blood clotting, immune function, and more.

#### Essential Nutrients
Essential nutrients are those the body cannot synthesize in adequate amounts — they must come from food. Essential amino acids (9 of the 20) must be obtained from diet; the body makes the other 11. Essential fatty acids (like omega-3s) cannot be made by the body. Most vitamins must come from food (exception: vitamin D is made with sunlight exposure).

#### Vitamin and Mineral Examples
Vitamin C: collagen synthesis, antioxidant (deficiency = scurvy). Vitamin D: calcium absorption, bone development (deficiency = rickets). Iron: hemoglobin and myoglobin production (deficiency = anemia). Calcium: bone and tooth structure, muscle contraction, nerve function. Potassium: nerve transmission and heart rhythm.

#### Science Bowl Clue
Bowl questions often give a deficiency symptom and ask for the nutrient. Scurvy = vitamin C deficiency. Rickets = vitamin D deficiency. Night blindness = vitamin A deficiency. Anemia (if dietary) = iron deficiency. These are high-yield, predictable connections.
