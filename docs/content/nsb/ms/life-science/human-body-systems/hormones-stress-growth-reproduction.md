#### The Stress Response
When you perceive danger, the hypothalamus activates two parallel systems: (1) Nervous: activates adrenal medulla → epinephrine (adrenaline) released → heart rate up, blood pressure up, blood redirected to muscles, glucose released into blood, digestion paused. This is the fight-or-flight response, acting in seconds. (2) Endocrine: hypothalamus → ACTH → adrenal cortex → cortisol released (stress hormone). Cortisol maintains elevated blood glucose and suppresses immune response. Chronic stress keeps cortisol high, which is harmful long-term.

#### Growth Hormone
Growth hormone (GH) is released by the pituitary gland, primarily during sleep. It stimulates growth of bones and muscles, protein synthesis, and cell division. Too much GH in childhood = gigantism; too little = growth hormone deficiency (treated with GH injections). Too much GH in adulthood (acromegaly) causes bones of the face, hands, and feet to enlarge (skull sutures already fused, so height doesn't increase).

#### Puberty Hormones
At puberty, the hypothalamus increases release of GnRH → pituitary releases FSH and LH → gonads increase production of sex hormones. In females: estrogen causes breast development, fat redistribution, and triggers the menstrual cycle. In males: testosterone causes muscle growth, voice deepening, facial hair, and sperm production. Both sexes experience growth spurts from increased GH and sex hormone interaction.

#### Insulin and Blood Sugar
After a carbohydrate-rich meal, blood glucose rises → pancreas releases insulin → cells take up glucose (muscle, liver, fat) → blood glucose returns to normal. Between meals, blood glucose falls → pancreas releases glucagon → liver breaks down glycogen and releases glucose. This tight regulation keeps blood glucose between ~70–100 mg/dL. Diabetes disrupts this system.
