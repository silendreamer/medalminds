#### The Light Reactions in the Thylakoid
The thylakoid is a flattened sac inside the chloroplast where light reactions happen. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy, exciting electrons. These energized electrons move through an electron transport chain, pumping protons and generating ATP and NADPH. Water is split in this process, releasing oxygen as a byproduct.

#### What ATP and NADPH Do
ATP and NADPH are energy carriers produced by the light reactions. They carry energy to the Calvin cycle, where this energy is used to build glucose. Think of ATP as a coin and NADPH as a gift card — both carry energy in forms the plant can "spend."

#### The Calvin Cycle (Light-Independent Reactions)
The Calvin cycle, occurring in the stroma, uses ATP and NADPH to fix CO₂ into glucose. It occurs in three phases: (1) CO₂ fixation combines CO₂ with RuBP (a 5-carbon sugar) to form unstable 6-carbon molecules; (2) reduction uses ATP and NADPH to reduce these molecules; (3) regeneration uses ATP to regenerate RuBP. The cycle repeats about 3 times to produce one glucose molecule.

#### Why They're Called Light-Dependent and Light-Independent
Light reactions absolutely require light. Calvin cycle is light-independent (occurs in dark too), but it depends on the ATP and NADPH from light reactions. Without light reactions running, the Calvin cycle slows dramatically.

#### Location in the Chloroplast
Thylakoids are stacked into grana. Light reactions happen in thylakoid membranes. The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma, the fluid-filled space around the thylakoids. This compartmentalization keeps the two stages organized and efficient.
