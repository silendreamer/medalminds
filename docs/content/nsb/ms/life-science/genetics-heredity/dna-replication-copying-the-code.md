#### Why Cells Replicate DNA
Before a cell divides, it must copy all of its DNA so each daughter cell gets a complete set of genetic instructions. In humans, that means copying about 3 billion base pairs — accurately. Errors in copying can lead to mutations. The process of copying DNA is called replication and occurs during the S phase of the cell cycle.

#### How Replication Works
Replication is semiconservative: each new DNA molecule contains one original strand and one newly built strand. The process begins at specific sites called origins of replication. An enzyme called helicase unwinds and "unzips" the double helix by breaking the hydrogen bonds between base pairs, creating a replication fork. Each separated strand then serves as a template.

#### The Role of DNA Polymerase
DNA polymerase is the key enzyme that reads the template strand and adds complementary nucleotides to build the new strand. It works in the 5' to 3' direction. Because the two strands run in opposite directions, one new strand (the leading strand) is built continuously, while the other (the lagging strand) is built in short segments called Okazaki fragments. DNA ligase then joins these fragments.

#### Accuracy and Error Rates
DNA polymerase has proofreading ability — it catches and corrects most errors as it goes. The error rate is roughly 1 in a billion base pairs. Any errors that slip through can become mutations. Some mutations are harmless; others can affect protein function.

#### Common Trap
Students sometimes confuse DNA replication (copying DNA in the nucleus before cell division) with transcription (copying DNA into RNA to make a protein). Replication makes a DNA copy; transcription makes an RNA copy. Different enzyme, different product, different purpose.
