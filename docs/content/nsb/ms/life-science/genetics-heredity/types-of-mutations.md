#### Point Mutations
A point mutation changes a single nucleotide. Types: (1) Substitution — one base replaced by another. Can be a missense mutation (changes amino acid), nonsense mutation (creates a stop codon early), or silent mutation (same amino acid, different codon). Example: sickle cell disease is caused by a single A→T substitution in the hemoglobin gene that changes glutamic acid to valine.

#### Insertions and Deletions (Indels)
An insertion adds one or more bases; a deletion removes one or more bases. If the number of bases added or removed is NOT a multiple of 3, this causes a frameshift mutation — the entire reading frame of the mRNA is shifted, garbling all codons from that point on. Frameshifts typically produce a nonfunctional protein.

#### Example: Sickle Cell (Point Mutation)
Normal codon: GAG → glutamic acid (charged, water-loving). Mutant codon: GTG → valine (uncharged, hydrophobic). This single amino acid change causes hemoglobin to clump in low-oxygen conditions, distorting red blood cells into a sickle shape that blocks blood vessels.

#### Chromosomal Mutations
These affect large segments: (1) Deletion — a segment of a chromosome is lost. (2) Duplication — a segment is copied twice. (3) Inversion — a segment is reversed. (4) Translocation — a segment moves to a different chromosome. Down syndrome (trisomy 21) is caused by an extra chromosome 21 (nondisjunction during meiosis, not a point mutation).

#### Frameshift vs. Point Mutation
Point substitutions affect only one codon (usually). Frameshifts affect every codon downstream. This is why insertion/deletion mutations are typically more damaging than substitutions.
