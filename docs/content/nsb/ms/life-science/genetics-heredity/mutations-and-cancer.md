#### What Is Cancer?
Cancer is a group of diseases characterized by uncontrolled cell division. Normal cells divide only when needed, responding to chemical signals. Cancer cells ignore these signals and divide continuously, forming tumors and potentially invading other tissues (metastasis). Cancer is fundamentally a disease of DNA mutations.

#### Two Key Gene Types
Two types of genes normally work together to control cell division: (1) Proto-oncogenes promote cell division. When mutated (becoming oncogenes), they are permanently "on" — like a stuck accelerator. (2) Tumor suppressor genes slow or stop cell division. When mutated, they fail — like brakes that don't work. Cancer usually requires mutations in both types to develop.

#### The "Two-Hit" Model
For tumor suppressor genes, both copies must usually be mutated before cancer develops (the "two-hit hypothesis"). Individuals can inherit one mutated copy, requiring only one more "hit" to lose all tumor suppression. This explains inherited cancer predispositions like BRCA1/2 mutations (breast cancer risk) and Li-Fraumeni syndrome.

#### Carcinogens
Substances that cause cancer are called carcinogens. Examples: UV radiation (skin cancer), cigarette smoke chemicals (lung cancer), asbestos (mesothelioma), aflatoxins from mold (liver cancer). These are mutagens that specifically increase cancer risk by damaging DNA in rapidly-dividing cells.

#### Prevention and Treatment
Avoiding carcinogens reduces cancer risk. Sunscreen blocks UV radiation. Modern cancer treatments target the specific mutations driving the cancer — targeted therapy, immunotherapy — rather than just killing all rapidly-dividing cells (chemotherapy).
