#### States of Matter and Phase Changes

All matter exists in one of three main states: **solid**, **liquid**, or **gas**. A **phase change** is when matter transforms from one state to another, and it happens because of energy—usually in the form of heat. When you add heat to a solid, it can become a liquid. When you add more heat to a liquid, it becomes a gas. Similarly, removing heat reverses the process: a gas becomes a liquid (called **condensation**), and a liquid becomes a solid (called **freezing**). Understanding these changes helps explain everyday phenomena like ice melting or water boiling.

#### Melting and Boiling

**Melting** happens when heat energy breaks apart the rigid structure of a solid, allowing its particles to move more freely as a liquid. For example, ice melts into water at 0°C (32°F). **Boiling** occurs when a liquid gets enough heat energy for its particles to escape as a gas. Water boils at 100°C (212°F) at sea level, forming steam. Each substance has its own **melting point** and **boiling point**—the specific temperatures where these changes happen. These temperatures are important for identifying substances and understanding how materials behave at different conditions.

#### Sublimation: Solid to Gas

**Sublimation** is a special phase change where a solid transforms directly into a gas, skipping the liquid stage entirely. A common example is **dry ice** (solid carbon dioxide), which sublimates into carbon dioxide gas at room temperature without ever becoming liquid. Another everyday example is how wet clothes hang-dry outside even when below freezing—water ice sublimates into water vapor. Sublimation requires energy, just like melting and boiling, and it occurs when solid particles gain enough energy to escape into the air as gas.

#### Energy and Heat in Phase Changes

Every phase change requires **energy**. **Endothermic** phase changes—like melting, boiling, and sublimation—require adding heat energy to the substance. **Exothermic** phase changes—like freezing and condensation—release heat energy. The amount of energy needed depends on the type of substance and how much of it you have. This is why it takes longer to boil a large pot of water than a small cup. Understanding energy flow helps explain why sweating cools you down (evaporation) and why fog forms when warm air meets a cold surface (condensation).
