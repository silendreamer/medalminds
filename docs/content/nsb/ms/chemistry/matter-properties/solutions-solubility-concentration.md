#### Solutes and Solvents

A **solution** is a mixture where a substance called the **solute** dissolves completely into another substance called the **solvent**. The solvent is usually a liquid—often water—and the solute can be a solid, liquid, or gas. When you stir salt into water, salt is the solute and water is the solvent. They mix at the particle level, forming a uniform liquid that looks the same throughout.

#### Solubility

**Solubility** is the maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a certain temperature. Different substances have different solubilities. For example, salt dissolves easily in water, but oil does not. Solubility depends mainly on **temperature**—generally, heating water makes it dissolve more salt or sugar. Some substances, like most gases, become *less* soluble as temperature increases.

#### Concentration

**Concentration** describes how much solute is dissolved in a given amount of solution. A **dilute** solution has little solute, while a **concentrated** solution has a lot of solute. You can change concentration by adding more solute, removing solvent (by heating), or adding more solvent. Concentration tells you the "strength" of a solution—for example, a concentrated salt solution tastes much saltier than a dilute one.

#### Factors Affecting Solubility

Temperature is the biggest factor, but **pressure** and the nature of the solvent matter too. Stirring or shaking speeds up dissolving by bringing fresh solvent into contact with the solute. Remember: just because a solute stops dissolving doesn't mean you have a pure substance—a **saturated solution** contains the maximum dissolved solute, but undissolved particles may sit at the bottom.
