#### Why Separate Mixtures?

A **mixture** is two or more substances combined but not chemically bonded. Sand and water mixed together is a mixture—you can separate them. A **compound** is different: hydrogen and oxygen chemically bonded together make water, and you can't just pull them apart mechanically. Scientists need to separate mixtures to get pure substances for experiments, medicines, and products. There are three main separation methods, and you choose based on what you're trying to separate.

#### Filtration: Separating Solids from Liquids

**Filtration** uses a **filter** (usually paper) to catch solid particles while liquid passes through. Pour your mixture through the filter, and the solid stays on top while the liquid drains into a container below. This works great for separating sand from water or coffee grounds from coffee. The solid left in the filter is the **residue**, and the liquid collected is the **filtrate**. Filter paper has tiny holes—big enough for water molecules to pass through but too small for sand grains. Filtration is quick and used everywhere, from coffee makers to water treatment plants.

#### Distillation: Separating Liquids with Different Boiling Points

**Distillation** separates **liquids** by heating the mixture. Each liquid has its own **boiling point**—the temperature where it turns into gas. If you heat salt water, the water (boiling point 100°C) vaporizes first and floats away, leaving salt behind. The water vapor cools down in a special tube called a **condenser**, turns back into liquid, and drips into a clean container. The salt stays in the original container because it doesn't boil at that temperature. You're left with pure water and pure salt. Distillation works because the liquids have different boiling points.

#### Chromatography: Separating Colored Dyes and Pigments

**Chromatography** separates substances by how they move through a special material. **Paper chromatography** is simple: draw a pencil line on filter paper, place a drop of mixture on the line, and set the paper upright in a shallow container with a small amount of liquid (the **solvent**). The solvent soaks up the paper, and each colored dye travels a different distance—some stay near the bottom, others move nearly to the top. After the solvent reaches the top, you remove the paper and see separate colored bands. Each color is a different **pigment** or dye. Scientists use chromatography to identify which dyes are in ink, food coloring, or paint.

#### Choosing the Right Method

How do you know which method to use? If you have a solid and liquid mixed together, try **filtration** first—it's fast and easy. If you have two liquids with different boiling points, use **distillation** with heat. If you have colored substances (like inks or dyes), use **chromatography** to separate and identify them. Sometimes you combine methods: filter out big solids first, then distill the remaining liquid. Real chemists think about cost, time, safety, and how pure the result needs to be before they choose their separation method.
