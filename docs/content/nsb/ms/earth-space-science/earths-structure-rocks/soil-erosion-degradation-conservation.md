#### How Soil Erodes
Soil erosion is the removal of topsoil by **water** (rainfall impact and runoff), **wind**, and **human activities** (tillage, deforestation, overgrazing). Water erosion is the most destructive globally — heavy rain dislodges soil particles, which flow downhill into streams. Wind erosion dominates in dry regions. Erosion is natural, but agriculture, construction, and deforestation accelerate it by removing the plant cover that holds soil in place. Globally, we lose ~24 billion tons of topsoil per year — 10× faster than natural soil formation.

#### The Dust Bowl: A Lesson in Soil Mismanagement
In the 1930s, the U.S. Great Plains experienced a catastrophic **Dust Bowl** — severe drought combined with decades of deep plowing that destroyed native grassland root systems. Without roots holding soil in place, wind picked up millions of tons of topsoil and created enormous **dust storms** (called "black blizzards") that buried farms and towns, reached the Atlantic Ocean, and forced 500,000 people to flee. The Dust Bowl was a human-caused ecological disaster that led to major soil conservation policies and the creation of the **Soil Conservation Service** (now the NRCS) in 1935.

#### Desertification: When Soil Dies
**Desertification** is the process by which fertile land becomes desert due to drought, deforestation, or inappropriate farming. It affects about 1/3 of Earth's land surface and threatens food security for over 1 billion people, especially in Africa, Asia, and South America. Once topsoil is lost, the land loses its ability to retain water and support plant growth — creating a positive feedback loop where conditions worsen. The **Sahara Desert** is expanding southward at rates of up to 48 km per year in some areas.

#### Soil Conservation Techniques
**Contour plowing**: plowing along hillside contours rather than up-down slopes reduces water runoff. **Terracing**: cutting level steps into hillsides reduces slope angle and erosion. **No-till farming**: leaving plant roots and residue in the ground protects soil structure and prevents wind erosion. **Cover crops**: planting crops that cover bare soil between growing seasons. **Windbreaks**: rows of trees planted along field edges to block wind. After the Dust Bowl, the U.S. government paid farmers to plant **shelter belts** — 220 million trees across the Great Plains.

#### Soil and Climate Change
Soil stores approximately **2,500 billion tons of carbon** — more than twice the carbon in the atmosphere and all living plants combined. When soil is disturbed or eroded, this stored carbon is released as CO₂. Conversely, healthy soils with high organic matter can **sequester** (absorb) carbon from the atmosphere, helping mitigate climate change. This is why sustainable farming practices (no-till, cover crops, composting) are increasingly recognized as climate solutions, not just agricultural ones.
