#### What Is Soil?
Soil is a mixture of weathered rock particles (minerals), organic matter, water, air, and living organisms including bacteria, fungi, earthworms, and insects. The **parent material** — the underlying bedrock or sediment from which soil forms — is broken down by **weathering** (physical and chemical breakdown of rock) over hundreds to thousands of years. A few centimeters of soil can take 500–1,000 years to form. Soil supports plant growth, filters water, cycles nutrients, and stores carbon.

#### Soil Horizons: Reading the Soil Profile
A vertical cross-section through soil reveals distinct **horizons** (layers) called a **soil profile**:
- **O horizon** (organic): surface layer of decomposing leaves and organic matter
- **A horizon** (topsoil): dark, mineral-rich layer mixed with humus; most fertile; where most plant roots live
- **B horizon** (subsoil): lighter color, accumulates minerals leached from above
- **C horizon**: partially weathered parent material (rock fragments)
- **R horizon**: solid bedrock (parent rock)

Healthy agricultural topsoil typically represents the O + A horizons.

#### Humus: The Living Heart of Soil
**Humus** is the dark, organic component of soil — decomposed plant and animal material. It gives topsoil its dark color and is the key to soil fertility. Humus improves soil structure, helps soil retain water, and slowly releases nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) as it decomposes. Earthworms are essential to humus formation: they eat organic matter, digest it, and excrete **castings** (worm manure) that enrich soil. One acre of healthy farmland may contain over a million earthworms.

#### Factors Affecting Soil Formation (CLORPT)
Five factors determine what type of soil forms in an area:
**C**limate (most important — more rain and warmth = faster weathering and more organic matter)
**L**iving organisms (plants, bacteria, fungi, animals)
**O**rganic matter (parent material)
**R**elief (slope — steep slopes have thinner soils)
**P**arent material (the bedrock being weathered)
**T**ime (more time = deeper, more developed soil profiles)

#### Soil Types and Their Properties
**Sandy soil**: large particles, drains quickly, low fertility. **Clay soil**: tiny particles, holds water, can become waterlogged, low aeration. **Loam**: ideal mix of sand, silt, and clay — best for agriculture because it drains well, holds nutrients, and has good structure. The USDA classifies soils by texture (particle size ratios) and designates prime farmland based on its agricultural potential. The U.S. Midwest's deep, humus-rich soil (called **mollisols**) is among the most productive farmland on Earth.
