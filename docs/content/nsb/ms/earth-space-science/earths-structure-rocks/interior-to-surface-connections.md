#### The Crust Is Just the Surface of a Dynamic System
It's easy to think of Earth's surface as all that matters, but the crust is merely the thin, cool skin of a constantly churning system. Tectonic plates (which carry continents and ocean floors) are just the top of the lithosphere, moving because of mantle convection below. Every major mountain range, every ocean basin, every volcanic chain traces back to mantle movement. The surface is the symptom; the interior is the cause.

#### Earthquake Depth Tells a Story
Where earthquakes originate (their **focus** or **hypocenter**) reveals which layer is involved. **Shallow earthquakes** (0–70 km) occur in the brittle crust and cause the most damage. **Intermediate earthquakes** (70–300 km) occur where oceanic plates subduct and begin to heat up but remain brittle. **Deep earthquakes** (300–700 km) occur in cold subducting slabs that penetrate far into the mantle. No earthquakes occur below 700 km because rock becomes too plastic to fracture.

#### Volcanoes: Windows to the Mantle
Volcanic eruptions bring mantle material to the surface. Lava is magma that has reached the surface; magma forms when pressure decreases (at mid-ocean ridges), temperature increases (at hot spots), or water is added (at subduction zones), lowering the melting point of rock. Studying lava compositions tells scientists what the mantle is made of — the mantle consists primarily of silicate minerals rich in magnesium and iron (olivine and pyroxene).

#### Earth's Interior and Climate
Volcanic eruptions inject SO₂ into the stratosphere, which forms sulfuric acid aerosols that reflect sunlight and temporarily cool Earth. The 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora in Indonesia caused the "Year Without a Summer" in 1816 — crop failures across Europe and North America. Over geologic time, volcanic CO₂ output (from plate tectonics) and carbon burial drive Earth's long-term carbon cycle and climate.

#### Practical Applications: Mining, Drilling, and Geothermal
Understanding Earth's layers has economic value. Knowing that certain ore deposits (gold, copper, nickel) form in specific geological environments helps miners find them. Oil and gas form in sedimentary rock of the crust — understanding crust structure guides drilling. Geothermal power plants drill into areas of high heat flow. Even GPS precision depends on correcting for how Earth's non-uniform mass (caused by mantle density variations) affects satellite orbits.
