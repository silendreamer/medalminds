#### The Layered Planet
Earth is not uniform throughout. Like a hard-boiled egg, it has distinct layers. Scientists know this not by drilling (the deepest drill hole, the Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia, reached only 12 km) but by analyzing seismic waves that travel through Earth during earthquakes. The four main layers from outside to inside are: crust, mantle, outer core, and inner core. Each layer has different chemical composition, temperature, pressure, and physical state.

#### The Crust: Earth's Thin Shell
The crust is Earth's outermost solid layer and the thinnest — just 5–70 km thick. There are two types: **oceanic crust** (5–10 km thick, made of dense basalt, found under oceans) and **continental crust** (30–70 km thick, made of less dense granite, forms the continents). The boundary between the crust and mantle is called the **Mohorovičić discontinuity** (the Moho), discovered by Croatian seismologist Andrija Mohorovičić in 1909.

#### The Mantle: The Thick Middle Layer
The mantle extends from the Moho to about 2,900 km depth and makes up about 84% of Earth's volume. It is solid rock, but under extreme heat and pressure it flows very slowly like a thick plastic — a property called **plasticity**. The upper part of the mantle (combined with the crust) forms the **lithosphere** (rigid). Below that is the **asthenosphere**, a semi-molten zone where tectonic plates float and move.

#### The Core: Iron and Nickel at the Center
The core begins at 2,900 km and extends to Earth's center at 6,371 km. The **outer core** (2,900–5,100 km) is liquid iron and nickel — its convective movement generates Earth's magnetic field. The **inner core** (5,100–6,371 km) is solid iron and nickel, kept solid by immense pressure despite temperatures reaching 5,000–6,000°C — as hot as the Sun's surface.

#### Why Layers Form: Density Sorting
When Earth formed 4.6 billion years ago, it was molten throughout. Denser materials (iron, nickel) sank to form the core while lighter materials (silicates) floated up to form the mantle and crust — a process called **differentiation**. This is why the densest layer (inner core, ~13 g/cm³) is at the center while the least dense (continental crust, ~2.7 g/cm³) is at the surface.
