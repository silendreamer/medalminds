#### Tsunamis: The Ocean's Response to Earthquakes
When a large submarine earthquake or underwater landslide rapidly displaces the seafloor, it moves an enormous column of water — creating a **tsunami**. Tsunamis in deep ocean are barely noticeable (wave height < 1 m, wavelength hundreds of km), but they travel at speeds up to 800 km/h (jet plane speed). As they approach shallow coastal water, they slow down and the wave compresses upward — reaching heights of 10–40 meters or more at shore. The **2004 Indian Ocean tsunami** (triggered by a 9.1 earthquake off Sumatra) killed ~228,000 people. The **2011 Tōhoku tsunami** in Japan reached 40 m high and caused the Fukushima nuclear disaster.

#### Liquefaction: When Ground Becomes Quicksand
**Liquefaction** occurs when saturated, loose sandy soil temporarily behaves like a liquid during strong shaking. Normally, water in the gaps between soil particles allows them to support loads. During shaking, pore water pressure increases, the grains lose contact with each other, and the soil loses its strength — buildings sink into it, buried objects float upward. Liquefaction destroyed much of Anchorage, Alaska during the 1964 earthquake (M9.2) and large areas of Christchurch, New Zealand during 2011 (M6.3). Engineers avoid building on liquefaction-prone soils or use deep pile foundations.

#### Fire: The Secondary Killer
After the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, broken gas lines ignited fires that burned for three days and destroyed 25,000 buildings — far more than the shaking itself. Fire after earthquakes kills because water mains break, leaving firefighters without water. **Modern building codes** require automatic gas shutoffs and flexible connections in buildings to minimize this risk.

#### Building Codes and Earthquake Engineering
Engineering has dramatically reduced earthquake deaths. **Base isolation**: buildings sit on rubber-and-lead pads that absorb horizontal shaking, protecting the building above. **Shear walls** and steel bracing resist horizontal forces. **Tuned mass dampers** — giant pendulums inside skyscrapers — counteract swaying. Tokyo's **Taipei 101** has a 728-ton tuned mass damper. The 1994 Northridge earthquake (M6.7) caused $20 billion damage in Los Angeles; a similar earthquake in Japan caused far less damage due to better building codes.

#### Early Warning Systems
**Earthquake early warning systems** (EEWS) use seismographs to detect the fast-moving but less destructive P-waves and broadcast a warning before the slower, destructive S-waves and surface waves arrive. Japan's system (J-Alert) gives up to 60 seconds of warning — enough to stop high-speed trains, alert surgeons to pause operations, and have people drop, cover, and hold on. California's **ShakeAlert** system began public warnings in 2021. Warning times range from seconds to over a minute depending on distance from the epicenter.
