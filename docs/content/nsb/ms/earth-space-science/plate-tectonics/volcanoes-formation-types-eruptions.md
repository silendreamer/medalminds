#### What Controls Eruption Style?
The most important factor controlling how a volcano erupts is **magma composition** — specifically its **silica (SiO₂) content** and **viscosity** (resistance to flow). High-silica magma is **thick and viscous** — gases can't escape easily, pressure builds, and the volcano erupts explosively. Low-silica magma is **thin and runny** — gases escape easily, and lava flows rather than explodes. Think of shaking a carbonated soda: with the cap loosened (low viscosity), gas escapes gently; with the cap sealed until you open it fast (high viscosity), it explodes.

#### Shield Volcanoes: Gentle Giants
**Shield volcanoes** form from low-silica, low-viscosity basaltic magma. Lava flows easily in all directions, building a broad, gently sloping structure resembling a warrior's shield lying flat. Eruptions are frequent but rarely life-threatening — lava flows slowly enough for people to walk away. **Mauna Loa** and **Kilauea** in Hawaii are shield volcanoes. Mauna Loa is the world's largest volcano by volume, rising 9 km from the ocean floor (taller than Mt. Everest measured from its base).

#### Composite (Stratovolcanoes): Dangerous Beauties
**Composite volcanoes** (stratovolcanoes) are built from alternating layers of lava and pyroclastic material. They have high-silica, high-viscosity magma and can explode catastrophically. They form the classic steep, symmetrical cone shape — **Mount Fuji** (Japan), **Mount St. Helens** (Washington, USA), **Krakatoa** (Indonesia), **Mount Pinatubo** (Philippines). The 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens blew away 400 m from the mountain's top and knocked down 600 km² of forest.

#### Cinder Cone Volcanoes
**Cinder cones** are the simplest and smallest type. They form when gas-rich magma erupts explosively, sending fragments (cinders) into the air that fall back and pile up into a cone. They erupt from a single vent, have steep sides (~30°), and typically only erupt once. **Paricutin** in Mexico appeared in a farmer's cornfield in 1943 and grew 336 m in a year — one of the only times humans witnessed a new volcano's birth.

#### Volcanic Hazards Beyond Lava
Most volcanic deaths are NOT from lava flows (which move slowly). The main killers are:
- **Pyroclastic flows**: avalanches of hot gas, ash, and rock fragments traveling at 100–700 km/h, up to 1,000°C. Destroyed Pompeii (79 AD).
- **Lahars**: volcanic mudflows caused when volcanic heat melts snow/ice, mixing with ash. Buried the Colombian city of Armero (1985), killing 23,000.
- **Volcanic ash**: disrupts aviation (clogs jet engines), collapses roofs, contaminates water. The 2010 Eyjafjallajokull eruption in Iceland halted European air travel for weeks.
- **Volcanic gases**: SO₂ and CO₂ released can suffocate or cause acid rain.
