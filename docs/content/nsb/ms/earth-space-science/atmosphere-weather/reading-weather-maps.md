#### Weather Fronts

**Fronts** mark the boundaries where two air masses meet. A **cold front** (shown as a blue line with triangles) happens when cold air moves into warmer air, often bringing thunderstorms and sudden temperature drops. A **warm front** (shown as a red line with semicircles) occurs when warm air moves into colder air, typically bringing gradual warming and steady rain. Occluded fronts happen when a cold front catches up to a warm front, and stationary fronts are stuck in one place. On weather maps, fronts help meteorologists predict where weather will change dramatically.

#### Understanding Isobars

**Isobars** are curved lines on weather maps that connect points of equal atmospheric pressure. Closely spaced isobars mean pressure changes quickly over a small distance, which signals strong winds and severe weather. Widely spaced isobars indicate gentler pressure changes and calmer conditions. **High pressure** areas (marked with an "H") usually bring clear, sunny skies and calm weather. **Low pressure** areas (marked with an "L") are associated with cloudy skies, precipitation, and stronger winds. By reading isobars, you can predict wind speed and identify storm systems.

#### Common Weather Symbols

Weather maps use simple symbols to show different conditions at different locations. A **cloud with a rain drop** indicates rain, a **cloud with a snowflake** shows snow, and a **cloud with a lightning bolt** represents thunderstorms. A **sun symbol** means clear skies, and a **half-filled circle** shows partial clouds. A **small triangle or circle** on isobars marks pressure values. These universal symbols let meteorologists and the public quickly understand weather conditions across large areas without reading many words.

#### How to Read a Weather Map

Start by finding the fronts, which show where weather will change. Next, look at the high and low pressure areas marked with "H" and "L." Check the isobars around them—if they're close together, expect stronger winds. Finally, look at the weather symbols scattered across the map to see what conditions are happening right now in different locations. Combine this information to predict: Will tomorrow be stormy? Will it be windy? Will it rain? Practice with real weather maps from your local news to get better at interpreting them.
