#### The Visible Spectrum

Light is a type of energy that travels in waves. The **visible spectrum** is the range of light wavelengths our eyes can detect, from red light (longer waves) to violet light (shorter waves). Between them are orange, yellow, green, blue, and indigo. Each color has a different wavelength. Red light has the longest wavelength of visible light, while violet has the shortest. White light, like sunlight, is actually a combination of all these colors mixed together. A **prism**—a transparent triangle made of glass—can separate white light into its component colors because each wavelength bends slightly differently.

#### Reflection of Light

**Reflection** occurs when light bounces off a surface. When you look in a mirror, light from your face bounces straight back, creating an image. A smooth, shiny surface reflects light in a single direction (specular reflection), which is why mirrors work well. A rough surface like paper scatters light in many directions (diffuse reflection), which is why we can see the page from different angles. The law of reflection says the angle at which light hits a surface equals the angle at which it bounces away. Polished surfaces reflect more light than dull surfaces.

#### Refraction of Light

**Refraction** is the bending of light when it passes from one material into another at an angle. Light travels at different speeds through different materials. When light enters water from air, it slows down and bends. This is why a stick in water looks bent where it enters the water, and why objects underwater appear closer than they actually are. Your eye's **lens** uses refraction to focus light onto the back of your eye so you can see clearly. Eyeglasses and contact lenses correct vision by refracting light to focus it properly.

#### How We See Color

Objects appear colored because they absorb some wavelengths of light and reflect others. A red shirt reflects red light and absorbs other colors. A green leaf reflects green light. Our eyes have special cells (cones) sensitive to red, green, and blue light. Our brain combines these signals to create all the colors we see. When white light shines on an object, the colors the object absorbs determine what color we see. This is why objects look different in different light—artificial light contains different colors than sunlight.
