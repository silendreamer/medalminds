#### Temperature vs. Heat

**Temperature** measures how fast molecules are vibrating or moving in an object. It's measured in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit and tells you how *hot* or *cold* something is. **Heat**, on the other hand, is the *transfer* of **thermal energy** from one object to another. Think of it this way: a cup of boiling water has a high temperature, but a swimming pool at room temperature might contain more total thermal energy because it has so much more mass. Temperature is a property of the object; heat is the energy moving between objects.

#### Thermal Energy and Motion

At the smallest level, **thermal energy** comes from atoms and molecules constantly jiggling and bouncing. When something is hot, its particles are vibrating fast. When something is cold, its particles are vibrating slowly. Every object has thermal energy, even ice—the molecules just aren't moving as quickly. When you feel heat from a fire, you're sensing the transfer of this particle motion to your skin.

#### How Heat Moves

Heat travels in three ways: **Conduction** is heat transfer through direct contact—touching a hot pan burns your hand because heat flows from the pan into your skin. **Convection** happens in fluids (liquids and gases) as hot material rises and cool material sinks, creating circulation—this is why warm air rises in your home. **Radiation** is heat transfer through space without touching anything—the sun warms Earth through radiation across the empty vacuum of space.

#### Measuring Temperature

Thermometers measure temperature by using materials that change with heat. Liquid thermometers use mercury or alcohol that expands when hot and contracts when cold. Digital thermometers have sensors that detect resistance changes. Celsius (used in science and most countries) sets water's freezing point at 0° and boiling point at 100°. Fahrenheit (used in the US) sets freezing at 32° and boiling at 212°.

#### Equilibrium and Heat Flow

Heat always flows from hotter objects to cooler ones until they reach **thermal equilibrium**—the same temperature. A hot cup of tea slowly cools as heat transfers to the cooler air around it. The tea will never become hotter than the room on its own. This is why covering food keeps it warm: it reduces the rate of heat loss by slowing conduction and convection.
