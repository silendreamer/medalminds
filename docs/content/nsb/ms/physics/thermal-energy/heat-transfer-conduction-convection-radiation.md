#### Conduction: Heat Through Contact

**Conduction** is the transfer of heat through direct contact between materials. When you hold a hot cup of tea, heat flows from the cup into your hand by conduction. The hot molecules in the cup vibrate faster and bump into cooler molecules, passing energy along. Metals like copper and aluminum are excellent heat conductors because their atoms pass energy very efficiently. This is why cooking pans have metal bottoms—heat conducts quickly from the stove to your food.

#### Convection: Heat Through Fluid Movement

**Convection** happens when heat moves through liquids or gases as the material itself flows. In a pot of boiling water, warm water near the bottom rises because it becomes less dense, while cooler water sinks. This circular motion carries heat throughout the liquid. Convection is why we use fans—moving air distributes heat (or cool air) around a room. Many heating systems work by convection, pushing warm air or water through pipes to heat buildings.

#### Radiation: Heat Without Contact

**Radiation** is heat energy that travels in waves, like light, and doesn't require any material to carry it. The Sun heats Earth through radiation across empty space—no air or matter between them. A campfire warms your face through radiation. Unlike conduction and convection, radiation can travel through a vacuum. Darker surfaces absorb more radiant heat than lighter surfaces, which is why dark clothing feels hotter in sunlight.

#### Everyday Examples

You experience all three types of heat transfer daily. When you boil pasta, the **conduction** through the pot warms the water, **convection** circulates the hot water, and **radiation** from the flame heats nearby objects. In your home, a radiator warms rooms through convection currents, while the Sun provides radiation heat through windows.
