#### Defining Momentum

**Momentum** is how much "oomph" an object has as it moves. It depends on two things: mass and velocity. The formula is **p = m × v**. A bowling ball rolling at 10 mph has much more momentum than a ping-pong ball at the same speed, because the bowling ball is heavier. A bowling ball rolling slowly also has less momentum than one rolling quickly. Momentum explains why it's harder to stop a heavy, fast-moving object than a light, slow one.

#### Conservation of Momentum

In a closed system with no outside forces, **momentum is always conserved**—it never disappears or appears out of nowhere. Before a collision, the total momentum equals the total momentum after. Imagine two roller skaters pushing off each other: skater A moves left and skater B moves right. They move in opposite directions with different speeds depending on their masses, but their combined momentum before the push equals their combined momentum after. This principle applies to all collisions.

#### Types of Collisions

In an **elastic collision**, objects bounce apart and kinetic energy is mostly preserved. Two billiard balls clicking together is nearly elastic—they scatter in different directions. In an **inelastic collision**, objects stick together or lose energy as heat, sound, or deformation. A car crash is highly inelastic because the vehicles crumple, lock together, and kinetic energy becomes noise and damage. Even in inelastic collisions, momentum is still conserved.

#### Real-World Examples

In a car crash, momentum conservation determines what happens. A heavy truck and a light car colliding head-on won't bounce equally—the truck keeps moving faster because it has more momentum. Airbags and crumple zones extend the collision time, reducing force (since **Force = Change in momentum / Time**). This protects passengers by making the stop gentler. In sports, a baseball has little momentum alone, but swinging a bat backwards just before impact increases the ball's final momentum dramatically.

#### Why This Matters

Momentum conservation predicts outcomes in sports (basketball passes), space (rocket propulsion), and safety engineering (seatbelts, padding). Understanding momentum helps you see why slamming on brakes suddenly is dangerous—you have momentum and must overcome it gradually. Skidding, sliding, and all motion changes follow momentum principles.
