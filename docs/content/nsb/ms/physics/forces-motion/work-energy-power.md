#### What is Work?

In physics, **work** has a specific meaning: it's the effort needed to move an object over a distance using **force**. The formula is **W = F × d**, where F is force and d is distance. Work only happens when both force and motion are in the same direction. If you push a box across the floor, you're doing work. If you hold a box still, you're not doing work even though your muscles feel tired—that's just maintaining tension, not moving anything.

#### Kinetic and Potential Energy

**Energy** is the ability to do work. There are two main types: **Kinetic energy (KE)** is the energy of motion—a rolling ball has kinetic energy because it's moving. **Potential energy (PE)** is stored energy waiting to be released. A ball lifted high on a shelf has gravitational potential energy; if you drop it, that PE converts into KE as it falls. This is why the formula is **KE = ½mv²** (mass and velocity) and **PE = mgh** (mass, gravity, and height).

#### Energy Conversion

Energy constantly changes forms but never disappears—this is the **law of conservation of energy**. When you slide down a playground slide, your PE at the top converts to KE as you slide down. When you catch a ball, the kinetic energy doesn't vanish; it transforms into heat, sound, and motion of your hand. In every machine and living creature, energy conversion is happening constantly.

#### What is Power?

**Power** measures how *fast* work gets done. A car engine that reaches 60 mph in 3 seconds is more powerful than one that takes 10 seconds, even if both move the same distance. The formula is **P = W/t**, where W is work and t is time. Power is measured in **watts**—a 100-watt light bulb uses 100 joules of energy every second.

#### Real-World Connections

A sprinter and a distance runner both run 100 meters, so they do similar work. But the sprinter finishes in 10 seconds while the distance runner takes 20 seconds—the sprinter is doing more *power*. Similarly, a crane lifting a car two stories in seconds uses far more power than a person climbing stairs slowly, even though both gain the same PE.
