#### Applying the Laws
A car crash is Newton's Laws in action. Before impact: First Law — passengers move at the car's speed. During impact: Second Law — a large net force decelerates the car rapidly. Without restraints: passengers keep moving (First Law) and hit the dashboard. Seat belts apply Newton's Second Law: F = ma. Same deceleration (Δv/t), but spreading the stopping time over a longer interval reduces force. Crumple zones extend collision time, reducing peak force.

#### The Impulse Connection
Impulse = force × time = change in momentum. This explains why: (1) catching a ball with a "give" reduces force; (2) a padded dashboard hurts less than a hard one; (3) landing with bent knees hurts less than stiff-legged. In all cases, the same change in momentum occurs over a longer time → smaller force. Airbags increase stopping time from milliseconds to tens of milliseconds, dramatically reducing injury.

#### Newton's Third Law in Collisions
When two cars collide, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other (Third Law). But their accelerations differ: the lighter car experiences greater acceleration (a = F/m). A compact car and a truck experience the same force in magnitude, but the truck's greater mass means it barely slows while the compact decelerates sharply.

#### Review Questions
1. How does a crumple zone reduce injury during a car crash? Use F = ma in your answer.
2. Two cars collide. Car A has mass 1000 kg; Car B has mass 2000 kg. If the collision force is 10,000 N on each, what is each car's acceleration?
3. Which of Newton's laws explains why you slide forward in your seat when a bus brakes suddenly?
