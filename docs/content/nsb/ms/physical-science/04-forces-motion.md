## Subtopic: Speed, Velocity & Acceleration

### What Is Speed? Distance, Time, and the Basic Formula
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-speed-basics
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** speed · distance · time · v=d/t
**Summary:** Speed measures how much distance an object covers per unit of time, calculated using the formula v = d/t.

#### Defining Speed
Speed is the rate at which an object moves — how much distance it covers in a given amount of time. The formula is simple: speed = distance ÷ time, or v = d/t. Speed is always a positive number, measured in units like meters per second (m/s), kilometers per hour (km/h), or miles per hour (mph). In Science Bowl, meters per second is the standard SI unit. If a car travels 120 meters in 10 seconds, its speed is 120 ÷ 10 = 12 m/s.

#### Units and Conversions
The SI unit for speed is meters per second (m/s). When you see km/h, multiply by 1,000 and divide by 3,600 to convert to m/s (or multiply by 0.278). For example, 72 km/h = 72 × 1000 / 3600 = 20 m/s. Science Bowl questions often give distance in kilometers and time in hours, then ask for the answer in m/s — always check your units before calculating.

#### Average vs. Instantaneous Speed
Average speed covers the whole trip: total distance ÷ total time. A car that drives 60 km in the first hour and 90 km in the next hour has an average speed of 150 km / 2 h = 75 km/h, even though it wasn't always moving at 75 km/h. Instantaneous speed is the speed at one specific moment — what a speedometer shows. Science Bowl problems usually deal with average speed unless they mention a specific instant.

#### Solving Speed Problems Step by Step
To solve v = d/t problems: (1) Identify what's given and what's unknown. (2) Write the formula. (3) Plug in numbers with units. (4) Calculate and include the unit in your answer. Example: A cyclist travels 500 m in 25 s. Speed = 500/25 = 20 m/s. If you know speed and time, find distance: d = v × t. If you know speed and distance, find time: t = d/v. Rearranging is a core skill.

#### Real-World Connections
Speed is everywhere: weather radar clocks raindrops, police radar guns measure vehicles, GPS chips update your location every second. Sprinters reach about 10 m/s; sound travels at 343 m/s; light travels at 3×10⁸ m/s. Knowing these benchmarks helps you check whether an answer is reasonable. If you calculate a walking speed of 50 m/s, something went wrong.

#### Review Questions
1. A train travels 900 km in 6 hours. What is its average speed in km/h?
2. If a ball rolls at 5 m/s for 8 seconds, how far does it travel?
3. What is the difference between average speed and instantaneous speed?

### Velocity: Speed with Direction
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-velocity
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** velocity · vector · direction · displacement
**Summary:** Velocity is a vector quantity that includes both speed (magnitude) and direction, making it different from scalar speed.

#### Speed vs. Velocity
Speed tells you how fast — velocity tells you how fast and in which direction. A car moving north at 30 m/s and a car moving south at 30 m/s have the same speed but opposite velocities. This distinction matters in physics: velocity is a vector (has both magnitude and direction), while speed is a scalar (magnitude only). When a problem asks for velocity, your answer must include a direction: "20 m/s east" or "15 m/s downward."

#### Vectors and Direction
A vector has two parts: magnitude (the number) and direction (north/south/up/down, or an angle). Velocity, force, acceleration, and displacement are all vectors. Speed, distance, mass, and temperature are scalars. In Science Bowl, questions often test whether you recognize that velocity must include direction. If someone runs in a circle at constant speed, their velocity is constantly changing — because direction changes.

#### Displacement vs. Distance
Distance is the total path length traveled (scalar). Displacement is the straight-line distance from start to finish, with direction (vector). If you run 400 m around a track and return to the start, your distance is 400 m but your displacement is 0 m. Average velocity = displacement ÷ time. If displacement is zero (round trip), average velocity is zero — even if average speed is not.

#### Relative Velocity
Velocity depends on your reference frame. A passenger walking at 2 m/s toward the front of a train moving at 20 m/s is moving at 22 m/s relative to the ground. A boat crossing a river at 4 m/s perpendicular to a 3 m/s current has a resultant speed of √(4² + 3²) = 5 m/s relative to the riverbank. Bowl questions about boats, planes, and trains often use this concept.

#### Review Questions
1. A runner completes a full lap around a 400 m track in 80 s. What is her average speed? What is her average velocity?
2. What makes velocity a vector but speed a scalar?
3. A car drives 60 km east, then 80 km north. What is the magnitude of its displacement?

### Acceleration: Changing Velocity
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-acceleration
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** acceleration · change in velocity · a=Δv/t · deceleration
**Summary:** Acceleration measures how quickly velocity changes, calculated as the change in velocity divided by time.

#### What Is Acceleration?
Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a = Δv / t = (v_final − v_initial) / t. The SI unit is meters per second squared (m/s²). An object accelerates when it speeds up, slows down, or changes direction — because all three change velocity. A car going from 0 to 20 m/s in 4 seconds has acceleration = (20 − 0)/4 = 5 m/s². Gravity near Earth's surface accelerates falling objects at 9.8 m/s² downward (often rounded to 10 m/s² in bowl problems).

#### Positive vs. Negative Acceleration
When acceleration and velocity point in the same direction, the object speeds up. When they point in opposite directions, the object slows down — this is called deceleration (or negative acceleration). A braking car has negative acceleration: if v goes from 30 m/s to 0 m/s in 6 s, a = (0 − 30)/6 = −5 m/s². The magnitude is 5 m/s², and the negative sign means it's slowing down.

#### Acceleration and Direction Change
A car moving in a circle at constant speed is still accelerating — because direction changes constantly. This centripetal acceleration always points toward the center of the circle. On a roller coaster loop, you feel pressed into your seat at the bottom (centripetal acceleration points up) and lighter at the top (centripetal acceleration points down). Understanding that "constant speed ≠ zero acceleration" is a classic Science Bowl trap.

#### Graphing Motion
On a position-time graph, slope = velocity. On a velocity-time graph, slope = acceleration. A horizontal line on a v-t graph means zero acceleration (constant velocity). A straight diagonal line means constant acceleration. The area under a v-t graph gives displacement. Science Bowl sometimes shows a graph and asks you to identify what's happening — learn to read these at a glance.

#### Review Questions
1. A bicycle speeds up from 4 m/s to 10 m/s in 3 seconds. What is its acceleration?
2. Why is a car turning a corner at constant speed still accelerating?
3. On a velocity-time graph, what does the slope represent?

### Speed Problems in the Real World
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-speed-application
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** v=d/t · unit conversion · average speed · problem solving
**Summary:** Apply the speed formula to multi-step real-world problems involving unit conversions and average speed calculations.

#### Worked Example: Multi-Leg Trip
A family drives 240 km at 80 km/h, stops for 30 minutes, then drives 120 km at 60 km/h. Find the total time and average speed for the entire trip. Leg 1: t = 240/80 = 3 h. Stop: 0.5 h. Leg 2: t = 120/60 = 2 h. Total time = 5.5 h. Total distance = 360 km. Average speed = 360/5.5 ≈ 65.5 km/h. Note: average speed is NOT the average of 80 and 60 — you must use total distance ÷ total time.

#### Worked Example: Unit Conversion
A cheetah runs at 30 m/s. Convert to km/h. Multiply by 3600 (seconds/hour) and divide by 1000 (meters/km): 30 × 3600 / 1000 = 108 km/h. Now reverse: A car travels at 90 km/h. Convert to m/s: 90 × 1000 / 3600 = 25 m/s. Shortcut: divide km/h by 3.6 to get m/s. Multiply m/s by 3.6 to get km/h.

#### Speed of Sound and Light
Sound travels at approximately 343 m/s in air at room temperature (20°C). Light travels at 3×10⁸ m/s in vacuum. The difference explains thunder — light from lightning arrives almost instantly; sound takes about 3 seconds per kilometer (use 340 m/s). "30/5 rule": count seconds between lightning and thunder, divide by 5 for miles to the storm, or divide by 3 for kilometers.

#### Review Questions
1. A swimmer completes 50 m in 40 s, rests 20 s, then swims back 50 m in 45 s. What is her average speed for the swimming portions only?
2. Convert 15 m/s to km/h.
3. Thunder is heard 6 seconds after lightning. Approximately how far away is the storm (in km)?

### Acceleration on a Ramp: Gravity and Inclines
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-acceleration-ramp
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** gravity · inclined plane · acceleration · free fall
**Summary:** Explore how gravity causes constant acceleration in free fall and on ramps, with worked examples connecting the formula to real scenarios.

#### Free Fall Acceleration
Near Earth's surface, gravity accelerates all objects downward at 9.8 m/s² (≈ 10 m/s²) regardless of mass — a feather and a hammer fall at the same rate in a vacuum (demonstrated on the Moon by astronaut Dave Scott in 1971). In free fall, after 1 second an object moves at ~10 m/s; after 2 seconds, ~20 m/s; after 3 seconds, ~30 m/s. The formula v = g × t gives velocity (with g ≈ 10 m/s²).

#### Objects on Ramps
On a frictionless inclined plane, gravity pulls the object straight down, but the ramp redirects the net force along the slope. A steeper ramp gives more acceleration; a gentler ramp gives less. At 90° (vertical), the object is in free fall at 9.8 m/s². At 0° (horizontal), no component of gravity acts along the ramp — zero acceleration. This is why Galileo used ramps to slow down free fall and measure acceleration accurately.

#### Calculating with g = 10 m/s²
A ball is dropped from rest. After 3 s: v = 10 × 3 = 30 m/s. Distance fallen: d = ½gt² = ½ × 10 × 9 = 45 m. Science Bowl often rounds g to 10 m/s² to keep arithmetic clean. Always use 10 unless the problem specifies 9.8.

#### Review Questions
1. A rock is dropped from a cliff. What is its speed after 4 seconds (use g = 10 m/s²)?
2. How far does the rock fall in those 4 seconds?
3. Why does a heavy bowling ball and a light tennis ball hit the ground at the same time when dropped in a vacuum?

### Graphing Motion: Reading Position, Velocity, and Acceleration Graphs
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** ms-ps-motion-graphs
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** position-time graph · velocity-time graph · slope · area under curve
**Summary:** Synthesize speed, velocity, and acceleration concepts by reading and interpreting motion graphs.

#### Position-Time Graphs
The slope of a position-time (x-t) graph equals velocity. Steep slope = fast; gentle slope = slow. Positive slope = moving in positive direction; negative slope = moving in negative direction; horizontal line = stationary. A curved line means changing velocity (acceleration). A straight line means constant velocity (zero acceleration). Bowl questions sometimes show you a graph and ask: "Which segment shows the greatest speed?" — find the steepest slope.

#### Velocity-Time Graphs
The slope of a velocity-time (v-t) graph equals acceleration. Positive slope = speeding up; negative slope = slowing down; horizontal line = constant velocity (zero acceleration). The area under the curve equals displacement. A v-t graph showing a horizontal line at v = 5 m/s for 10 s means the object moved 5 × 10 = 50 m. Science Bowl sometimes combines x-t and v-t questions for the same motion.

#### Matching Graphs to Motion Descriptions
"A car starts from rest, accelerates to 20 m/s over 4 s, maintains that speed for 6 s, then decelerates to rest over 2 s." Translate this to a v-t graph: line from (0,0) to (4,20); horizontal at 20 from t=4 to t=10; line from (10,20) to (12,0). Then find total displacement: area = triangle + rectangle + triangle = ½(4)(20) + 6(20) + ½(2)(20) = 40 + 120 + 20 = 180 m.

#### Review Questions
1. On a position-time graph, what does a horizontal line mean? What does a line with negative slope mean?
2. A velocity-time graph shows v = 8 m/s for 5 seconds. What is the displacement?
3. Draw a rough sketch of the velocity-time graph for an object thrown straight up and falling back down.

## Subtopic: Newton's Three Laws

### Newton's First Law: Inertia and the Tendency to Stay Put
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-newtons-first-law
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** inertia · Newton's First Law · net force · equilibrium
**Summary:** Newton's First Law states that objects maintain their state of motion unless acted on by a net external force.

#### The Law of Inertia
Newton's First Law: An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted upon by a net external force. This property — the tendency to resist changes in motion — is called **inertia**. Inertia is proportional to mass: a bowling ball has more inertia than a tennis ball and is harder to stop or start moving. Before Newton, people thought moving objects naturally slowed down; Newton showed that slowing is caused by friction, not a natural tendency.

#### Balanced and Unbalanced Forces
When all forces on an object cancel out, the net force is zero — the object is in **equilibrium** and does not accelerate. A book sitting on a table has gravity pulling down and the normal force pushing up; they're equal and opposite, so the book stays still. When forces are unbalanced (net force ≠ 0), the object accelerates in the direction of the net force. A soccer ball rolling on grass slows because friction creates an unbalanced force opposing motion.

#### Everyday Examples of Inertia
- A passenger lurches forward when a car brakes suddenly — the passenger's body wants to keep moving.
- A tablecloth trick: yank the cloth fast enough, and dishes stay put (inertia keeps them in place).
- An astronaut in space will drift forever unless something pushes them.
- Seat belts work because they apply a force to stop passengers whose bodies want to keep moving in a crash.

#### Review Questions
1. State Newton's First Law in your own words.
2. Why does a hockey puck sliding on ice eventually stop, even though there's no visible force pushing against it?
3. Which has more inertia: a 2 kg textbook or a 0.5 kg water bottle? How can you tell?

### Newton's Second Law: Force, Mass, and Acceleration
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-newtons-second-law
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** F=ma · Newton · net force · mass · acceleration
**Summary:** Newton's Second Law, F = ma, describes how the net force on an object determines its acceleration based on its mass.

#### F = ma: The Most Important Formula in Mechanics
Newton's Second Law: The net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. F = ma. The SI unit of force is the **newton (N)**: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². If a 5 kg object accelerates at 3 m/s², the net force is F = 5 × 3 = 15 N. If a 10 N force acts on a 2 kg object, acceleration = F/m = 10/2 = 5 m/s². This equation is the engine of classical mechanics — almost every force problem uses it.

#### How Mass and Force Affect Acceleration
For a constant force, doubling the mass halves the acceleration: a = F/m. For a constant mass, doubling the force doubles the acceleration. These are direct and inverse proportionalities — a favorite Science Bowl question type. "If the net force on an object doubles and its mass triples, what happens to acceleration?" a = 2F/3m — it changes by a factor of 2/3.

#### Weight vs. Mass
Mass (kg) measures how much matter an object contains. Weight (N) is the force gravity exerts on that mass: W = mg. On Earth, g = 9.8 m/s², so a 10 kg object weighs ~98 N. On the Moon (g = 1.6 m/s²), the same object weighs only 16 N — but its mass is still 10 kg. Mass doesn't change with location; weight does. Science Bowl regularly tests this distinction.

#### Solving F = ma Problems
Given: A 3 kg cart experiences forces of 12 N east and 6 N west. Find acceleration. Net force = 12 − 6 = 6 N east. a = F/m = 6/3 = 2 m/s² east. Always find the net force first; then divide by mass. Direction matters — include it in your answer for velocity-related quantities.

#### Review Questions
1. A 4 kg object accelerates at 5 m/s². What net force acts on it?
2. A 20 N net force acts on a 10 kg object. What is its acceleration?
3. A 70 kg person weighs how many newtons on Earth (g = 10 m/s²)?

### Newton's Third Law: Action and Reaction
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-newtons-third-law
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** Newton's Third Law · action-reaction pairs · equal and opposite forces
**Summary:** Newton's Third Law states that for every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force acting on a different object.

#### The Law of Action-Reaction
Newton's Third Law: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. When object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts an equally large force in the opposite direction on object A. The two forces are always: (1) equal in magnitude, (2) opposite in direction, (3) acting on different objects. A rocket pushes exhaust gases backward; the gases push the rocket forward. Your feet push backward on the ground; the ground pushes your feet forward (that's how walking works).

#### Why Don't Action-Reaction Pairs Cancel?
Students often ask: if forces are equal and opposite, why does anything move? The key: action-reaction forces act on **different** objects. When you push a book, the book pushes back on your hand — but the book and your hand are separate objects. Newton's Second Law (F = ma) applies to each object separately. The net force on the book is the force you applied; the book's reaction force acts on you, not on itself.

#### Examples to Know
- Gun and bullet: the bullet is pushed forward; the gun recoils backward.
- Swimmer pushes water back; water pushes swimmer forward.
- Earth pulls you down with gravity; you pull Earth up with equal force (but Earth barely moves because M is enormous).
- Balloon released: air rushes out one end; balloon moves the other way.

#### Review Questions
1. State Newton's Third Law in your own words and give one example.
2. A 60 kg skater pushes off a wall with 120 N. What force does the wall exert on the skater?
3. Why doesn't the Earth visibly accelerate toward you when you jump, even though you pull it with the same force it pulls you?

### Newton's Laws in Action: Crash-Test Science
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-newtons-laws-crash
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** inertia · impulse · seat belts · F=ma · Newton's laws
**Summary:** Apply all three of Newton's Laws to vehicle safety, analyzing why seat belts, crumple zones, and airbags save lives.

#### Applying the Laws
A car crash is Newton's Laws in action. Before impact: First Law — passengers move at the car's speed. During impact: Second Law — a large net force decelerates the car rapidly. Without restraints: passengers keep moving (First Law) and hit the dashboard. Seat belts apply Newton's Second Law: F = ma. Same deceleration (Δv/t), but spreading the stopping time over a longer interval reduces force. Crumple zones extend collision time, reducing peak force.

#### The Impulse Connection
Impulse = force × time = change in momentum. This explains why: (1) catching a ball with a "give" reduces force; (2) a padded dashboard hurts less than a hard one; (3) landing with bent knees hurts less than stiff-legged. In all cases, the same change in momentum occurs over a longer time → smaller force. Airbags increase stopping time from milliseconds to tens of milliseconds, dramatically reducing injury.

#### Newton's Third Law in Collisions
When two cars collide, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other (Third Law). But their accelerations differ: the lighter car experiences greater acceleration (a = F/m). A compact car and a truck experience the same force in magnitude, but the truck's greater mass means it barely slows while the compact decelerates sharply.

#### Review Questions
1. How does a crumple zone reduce injury during a car crash? Use F = ma in your answer.
2. Two cars collide. Car A has mass 1000 kg; Car B has mass 2000 kg. If the collision force is 10,000 N on each, what is each car's acceleration?
3. Which of Newton's laws explains why you slide forward in your seat when a bus brakes suddenly?

### Forces and Free-Body Diagrams
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-free-body-diagrams
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** free-body diagram · normal force · friction · tension · net force
**Summary:** Draw and interpret free-body diagrams to find net force and predict motion in real scenarios.

#### What Is a Free-Body Diagram?
A free-body diagram (FBD) shows all the forces acting on one object as arrows pointing in the direction of each force, with length proportional to magnitude. The object is drawn as a box or dot. Common forces: weight (W = mg, downward), normal force (perpendicular to surface, upward on horizontal), friction (opposing motion), tension (along a rope, away from object), applied force (in the direction pushed or pulled). Label each arrow with the force name and magnitude.

#### Example: Box on a Horizontal Surface
A 5 kg box is pushed right with 30 N. Friction opposes motion with 10 N. Draw the FBD: Weight = 50 N down; Normal = 50 N up; Applied = 30 N right; Friction = 10 N left. Vertical: 50 − 50 = 0 N (no vertical acceleration, as expected). Horizontal: 30 − 10 = 20 N right. Net force = 20 N right. a = F/m = 20/5 = 4 m/s² right.

#### Example: Hanging Object
A 2 kg ball hangs from a rope. Weight pulls down: W = 20 N. Tension pulls up. Since the ball doesn't accelerate, tension = weight = 20 N. If the ball accelerates upward at 3 m/s², then net force = ma = 2 × 3 = 6 N upward. Tension − Weight = 6 N → Tension = 26 N. The rope must exert more force when the ball accelerates upward.

#### Review Questions
1. Draw a free-body diagram for a book at rest on a table. Label all forces.
2. A 10 kg box on a table has an applied force of 40 N right and friction of 15 N left. What is the net force and acceleration?
3. Why does the normal force on an object on a horizontal surface equal the object's weight only when acceleration is zero?

### Newton's Laws Synthesis Review
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** ms-ps-newtons-laws-review
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** all three Newton's Laws · F=ma · inertia · action-reaction
**Summary:** Comprehensive review connecting all three Newton's Laws through mixed problems and concept synthesis.

#### Integrating the Three Laws
First Law sets the baseline: no net force = no change in motion. Second Law gives the relationship when there IS a net force: F = ma. Third Law explains how forces always come in pairs. A real scenario uses all three: A rocket sits on a launchpad. First Law: it stays at rest until thrust > weight. Second Law: net force = thrust − weight; a = (thrust − weight)/mass. Third Law: exhaust pushed down = rocket pushed up.

#### Mixed Practice Problems
1. A 6 kg object is in equilibrium. What is the net force? (0 N — First Law)
2. A 15 N force acts on a 3 kg object. Acceleration? (5 m/s² — Second Law)
3. A swimmer pushes the wall with 80 N. How hard does the wall push back? (80 N — Third Law)
4. Earth's gravity pulls a 50 kg student with 500 N. How hard does the student pull Earth? (500 N — Third Law)

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid
- Saying a third-law pair "cancels" — they can't cancel; they act on different objects.
- Forgetting units: force in newtons (N), not kg.
- Confusing mass and weight — mass is kg, weight is N.
- Assuming a moving object must have a net force — it doesn't, if moving at constant velocity.

#### Review Questions
1. A 5 kg object accelerates at 2 m/s². What is the net force? What is its weight?
2. If you double the mass of an object and triple the net force, what happens to acceleration?
3. A stationary book sits on a table. Identify all force pairs (not just the forces on the book).

## Subtopic: Friction & Gravity

### What Is Friction? Types and Real-World Effects
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-friction-basics
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** friction · static friction · kinetic friction · normal force · coefficient of friction
**Summary:** Friction is a contact force that opposes relative motion between surfaces, with static friction stronger than kinetic friction.

#### Defining Friction
Friction is a force that opposes relative motion (or attempted motion) between two surfaces in contact. It acts parallel to the surfaces and opposite to the direction of motion (or intended motion). Friction is caused by microscopic roughness at the contact surface — even surfaces that look smooth have tiny bumps and valleys that catch on each other. Friction is not always bad: without it, you couldn't walk, tires couldn't grip roads, and you couldn't hold a pencil.

#### Static vs. Kinetic Friction
**Static friction** acts when surfaces are not moving relative to each other. It prevents objects from starting to move. Static friction can vary up to a maximum value. **Kinetic friction** (sliding friction) acts when surfaces are sliding. It is constant and always less than the maximum static friction. This is why it's harder to start sliding a heavy box than to keep it sliding — you must overcome greater static friction to get it moving.

#### What Affects Friction?
Friction depends on: (1) the **normal force** (how hard the surfaces press together) — more weight means more friction; (2) the **materials** in contact, described by the coefficient of friction (μ). Rougher surfaces have higher μ. Friction does NOT depend on the contact area — a wide brick and a narrow brick with the same weight have the same friction. This surprises many students and appears in Science Bowl.

### Gravity: Universal Attraction
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-gravity
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** gravity · gravitational force · mass · distance · Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation
**Summary:** Gravity is a universal attractive force between masses that decreases with distance, described by Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation.

#### What Is Gravity?
Gravity is an attractive force between any two masses. Every object in the universe pulls on every other object. Near Earth's surface, gravity is approximately constant: g = 9.8 m/s² downward. The weight of an object equals W = mg. The more mass an object has, the greater its weight. Gravity is what keeps the Moon orbiting Earth, Earth orbiting the Sun, and your feet on the ground.

#### Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation
F_gravity = G × m₁ × m₂ / r², where G is the gravitational constant (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²), m₁ and m₂ are the two masses, and r is the distance between their centers. Key takeaways: (1) Force increases with mass — double either mass, double the force. (2) Force decreases with distance squared — double the distance, force drops to one-fourth. This inverse-square relationship is critical for Science Bowl.

#### Gravity Across the Solar System
Gravitational acceleration varies by planet. On the Moon: g ≈ 1.6 m/s² (about 1/6 of Earth's). On Mars: g ≈ 3.7 m/s². On Jupiter: g ≈ 24.8 m/s². An astronaut who weighs 700 N on Earth weighs only ~117 N on the Moon. Their mass doesn't change — just their weight. Science Bowl often asks you to calculate weight on other planets.

#### Review Questions
1. How does gravitational force change if the distance between two objects doubles?
2. A 60 kg astronaut is on the Moon (g = 1.6 m/s²). What is their weight?
3. Why doesn't the Moon fall into Earth, even though gravity pulls it toward Earth?

### Friction and Gravity Working Together
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-friction-gravity-together
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** friction · gravity · inclined plane · normal force · net force
**Summary:** Analyze how friction and gravity interact on inclined surfaces, including when objects slide vs. remain stationary.

#### Forces on an Incline
On an inclined plane, gravity pulls the object straight down, but the ramp surface exerts a normal force perpendicular to the slope. Gravity's component along the slope (mg sinθ) acts to slide the object down; friction (up the slope) opposes this. The normal force equals mg cosθ (not the full weight). As the angle increases: the along-slope gravity component increases, the normal force decreases, and friction eventually can't stop the object.

#### Critical Angle and Sliding
The object stays put when friction ≥ gravity component along slope. It slides when gravity component > maximum static friction. The angle at which sliding just begins depends on the coefficient of friction (μ): tan(θ) = μ. If μ = 0.5, the object starts sliding when the incline exceeds arctan(0.5) ≈ 27°. This principle applies to avalanches, sliding boxes, and road design.

#### Real Applications
Road engineers bank curves to reduce the reliance on friction for turning. Mountain goats have hooves with soft pads that increase friction and a large contact area for stability. Ski wax changes the friction coefficient between skis and snow. Lubricants like oil reduce friction between engine parts, decreasing energy loss and wear.

#### Review Questions
1. On an inclined plane, is the normal force equal to the object's full weight? Why or why not?
2. What happens to friction as an incline gets steeper?
3. Name one situation where high friction is useful and one where low friction is useful.

### Gravity on Different Worlds: Problem Solving
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-gravity-planets
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** weight · mass · gravitational acceleration · planet comparisons
**Summary:** Apply the weight formula W = mg to solve problems involving gravity on different planets and moons.

#### Gravity Comparison Problems
An astronaut has a mass of 80 kg. Find their weight on Earth, Moon, Mars, and Jupiter. Earth (g = 9.8 m/s²): W = 80 × 9.8 = 784 N. Moon (g = 1.6 m/s²): W = 80 × 1.6 = 128 N. Mars (g = 3.7 m/s²): W = 80 × 3.7 = 296 N. Jupiter (g = 24.8 m/s²): W = 80 × 24.8 = 1984 N. Mass stays 80 kg throughout — only the weight changes.

#### Using the Inverse-Square Law
If you move from Earth's surface (distance r from center) to twice that distance (2r above Earth's center), the gravitational force becomes: F ∝ 1/(2r)² = 1/4r² — one-fourth as strong. At 3r: 1/9 as strong. Gravity never reaches zero, but it decreases rapidly with distance. At the International Space Station (roughly 400 km altitude, r ≈ 1.06 × Earth radius), gravity is still about 89% as strong as at the surface — not zero! Astronauts are in free fall, which is why they feel weightless.

#### Why Astronauts Feel Weightless
Astronauts on the ISS are not outside Earth's gravity — they're in free fall around Earth. Both the station and astronauts fall together at the same rate, so there's no contact force between them (no "floor pushing up"). This is apparent weightlessness. True weightlessness only occurs infinitely far from all masses.

#### Review Questions
1. If a scale reads 600 N on Earth, what would the same person weigh on Mars (g = 3.7 m/s²)?
2. If gravity on a new planet is 4 times Earth's, how does the orbital period of a low satellite compare?
3. Why do astronauts on the ISS float, even though Earth's gravity still acts on them?

## Subtopic: Simple Machines & Mechanical Advantage

### What Is a Simple Machine? The Six Types
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-simple-machines-intro
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** simple machine · lever · inclined plane · pulley · wheel and axle · wedge · screw · mechanical advantage
**Summary:** Simple machines are devices that change the direction or magnitude of a force, and there are exactly six classical types.

#### The Six Simple Machines
1. **Lever** — a rigid bar on a fulcrum (teeter-totter, crowbar, bottle opener)
2. **Inclined plane** — a slanted surface (ramp, hill)
3. **Wedge** — two inclined planes back-to-back (ax blade, doorstop, knife)
4. **Screw** — an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder (screws, bolts, jar lids)
5. **Pulley** — a grooved wheel with a rope (flagpole, crane, window blinds)
6. **Wheel and axle** — a large wheel attached to a smaller cylinder (doorknob, steering wheel, bicycle gears)

All six are based on two principles: trading force for distance, or distance for force. You never get more work out than you put in — but you can change how much force you need.

#### Work and Simple Machines
Work = Force × Distance (W = Fd). A simple machine doesn't change the total work needed — it redistributes force and distance. Using a ramp to push a 500 N box up to a shelf requires the same work as lifting it straight up, but the ramp lets you use less force over a longer distance. Simple machines make tasks easier, not by creating energy, but by trading distance for force reduction.

#### Ideal vs. Real Machines
An ideal machine has 100% efficiency — all input work becomes output work. Real machines lose some energy to friction. Efficiency (%) = (useful output work / input work) × 100. A machine is more efficient with less friction. Ball bearings, lubricants, and smooth surfaces improve efficiency.

#### Review Questions
1. List all six simple machines and give one real-world example of each.
2. A machine requires 200 J of input work to do 160 J of useful output work. What is its efficiency?
3. Why doesn't a simple machine reduce the total work needed for a task?

### Mechanical Advantage: Getting More Force from Less
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-mechanical-advantage
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** mechanical advantage · effort force · resistance force · MA = F_out/F_in · IMA vs AMA
**Summary:** Mechanical advantage (MA) tells how many times a machine multiplies the input force, with ideal MA determined by geometry and actual MA reduced by friction.

#### Defining Mechanical Advantage
Mechanical advantage (MA) = output force ÷ input force = F_resistance / F_effort. If you apply 50 N to a lever and lift 200 N, MA = 200/50 = 4. The machine multiplied your force by 4. Mechanical advantage greater than 1 means the machine amplifies force (you use less force over a longer distance). MA less than 1 means you use more force but gain speed or distance. MA exactly 1 means the machine only changes direction (like a single fixed pulley).

#### Ideal Mechanical Advantage (IMA)
IMA is calculated from distances, not forces: IMA = distance of effort / distance of resistance = d_e / d_r. For an inclined plane: IMA = length of ramp / height. A 5 m ramp reaching a 1 m ledge: IMA = 5. For a lever: IMA = effort arm / resistance arm. A lever with a 3 m effort arm and 1 m resistance arm: IMA = 3.

#### Actual Mechanical Advantage (AMA)
AMA uses real forces and is always less than IMA due to friction: AMA = F_out / F_in. Efficiency = AMA/IMA × 100%. A pulley system with IMA = 4 but AMA = 3 has efficiency = 3/4 × 100% = 75%.

#### Review Questions
1. A lever requires 40 N of effort to lift a 160 N load. What is the mechanical advantage?
2. A ramp is 6 m long and 2 m high. What is its ideal mechanical advantage?
3. If the IMA of a machine is 5 and its efficiency is 80%, what is its AMA?

### Levers: Classes and Calculations
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-levers
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** lever · fulcrum · effort arm · resistance arm · first/second/third class lever
**Summary:** Levers come in three classes depending on the relative positions of the fulcrum, effort, and load.

#### Three Classes of Levers
**First class:** Fulcrum between effort and load. Examples: teeter-totter, scissors, pliers, crowbar. Can have MA > 1, < 1, or = 1 depending on arm lengths. **Second class:** Load between fulcrum and effort. Examples: wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener. Effort arm always longer than resistance arm → MA always > 1. **Third class:** Effort between fulcrum and load. Examples: tweezers, fishing rod, baseball bat, forearm lifting. Effort arm always shorter → MA always < 1 (but gain speed/distance).

#### The Lever Equation
For a lever in equilibrium: F_effort × d_effort = F_resistance × d_resistance. A first-class lever has a fulcrum 1 m from a 100 N load and 4 m from where you push. Effort needed: F_e × 4 = 100 × 1 → F_e = 25 N. MA = 100/25 = 4.

#### Recognizing Lever Class in Bowl
Bowl often describes a lever and asks for its class or mechanical advantage. Tip: find the fulcrum (pivot), then locate the effort and load. If the fulcrum is in the middle → first class. If the load is in the middle → second class. If the effort is in the middle → third class. Memorize examples for each.

#### Review Questions
1. A 60 N effort on a lever lifts a 240 N load. Effort arm = 4 m. Resistance arm = ?
2. Classify each lever: (a) scissors, (b) wheelbarrow, (c) tweezers.
3. Why does a third-class lever have MA < 1? Why would you ever use one?

### Simple Machines in Technology
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-simple-machines-technology
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** pulley · compound machine · wheel and axle · gears · mechanical advantage
**Summary:** Apply knowledge of simple machines to analyze real compound machines including pulley systems, bicycles, and cranes.

#### Pulley Systems
A single fixed pulley only changes the direction of force (MA = 1). A single movable pulley gives MA = 2 — you pull twice the distance but with half the force. A block-and-tackle system (multiple pulleys) multiplies force further. MA of a pulley system equals the number of rope segments supporting the load. A system with 4 supporting ropes: MA = 4. To lift a 400 N load, you apply 100 N over 4× the distance.

#### Bicycle: A Compound Machine
A bicycle combines wheel-and-axle (pedals to gears), pulleys (derailleurs), and levers (brakes). In high gear: the front sprocket is large, the rear sprocket small — high IMA means fast speed but high pedaling force. In low gear: small front, large rear — low IMA means slow but easy climbing. Gear ratios are the wheel-and-axle in action.

#### Cranes and Construction
A tower crane uses a combination of pulleys (block-and-tackle), a wheel-and-axle (winch drum), and a lever (boom). Modern cranes can lift hundreds of tonnes by multiplying mechanical advantage. The trade-off: the cable must travel many times the distance the load moves.

#### Review Questions
1. A pulley system has 3 rope segments supporting the load. What is the IMA? If the load is 600 N, what effort force is needed ideally?
2. Why do cyclists shift to a lower gear when climbing a steep hill?
3. List two simple machines found inside a bicycle.
