## Subtopic: Wave Properties (Frequency, Wavelength, Amplitude)

### What Is a Wave? Energy, Not Matter, on the Move
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-wave-basics
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** wave · energy transfer · medium · mechanical wave · electromagnetic wave
**Summary:** A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy through a medium (or through space) without permanently displacing the matter itself.

#### Defining a Wave
A wave is a repeating disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another. Crucially, waves move energy — not matter. When an ocean wave rolls past a buoy, the buoy bobs up and down but doesn't travel with the wave. The energy moves forward; the water molecules move locally. Two major categories: **mechanical waves** require a medium (solid, liquid, or gas) to travel through — sound is a mechanical wave. **Electromagnetic waves** (light, radio, X-rays) need no medium and can travel through a vacuum.

#### Transverse vs. Longitudinal Waves
**Transverse waves:** particles vibrate perpendicular (at 90°) to the direction of wave travel. Example: a wave on a rope; light waves. The wave travels horizontally but the rope moves up and down. **Longitudinal waves:** particles vibrate parallel to the direction of wave travel. Example: sound. Air molecules compress and expand in the same direction the sound travels. Knowing which type a wave is matters for Science Bowl questions.

#### What Waves Need
Mechanical waves need a medium — they cannot travel through a vacuum. That's why there is no sound in space. Electromagnetic waves don't need a medium — they carry energy through oscillating electric and magnetic fields. The speed of mechanical waves depends on the properties of the medium (density, elasticity). Sound travels faster in solids than liquids than gases.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between a transverse and a longitudinal wave? Give one example of each.
2. Can sound travel through a vacuum? Explain why or why not.
3. A wave in the ocean passes under a rubber duck. Does the duck move with the wave? What does move with the wave?

### Anatomy of a Wave: Frequency, Wavelength, and Amplitude
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-wave-anatomy
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** wavelength · frequency · amplitude · crest · trough · hertz · wave speed
**Summary:** Waves are described by wavelength (λ), frequency (f), and amplitude (A), with the wave speed formula v = fλ connecting the first two.

#### Key Wave Measurements
**Wavelength (λ):** The distance from one point on a wave to the next identical point — crest to crest or trough to trough. Measured in meters (m). **Frequency (f):** How many complete wave cycles pass a fixed point per second. Measured in hertz (Hz); 1 Hz = 1 cycle per second. **Amplitude (A):** The maximum displacement from the rest position — the height of a crest or depth of a trough. Amplitude is NOT related to frequency or wavelength.

#### The Wave Speed Formula
Wave speed (v) = frequency (f) × wavelength (λ): **v = fλ**. This is the single most important wave equation. If frequency increases and wave speed stays constant (same medium), wavelength decreases — they are inversely proportional. If a wave has frequency 500 Hz and wavelength 0.68 m, its speed = 500 × 0.68 = 340 m/s (sound in air). Rearrange to find any variable: f = v/λ; λ = v/f.

#### Period: The Inverse of Frequency
The period (T) is the time for one complete wave cycle: T = 1/f. If frequency = 5 Hz, period = 1/5 = 0.2 s. Frequency and period are always reciprocals. A wave with a 0.01 s period has a frequency of 100 Hz. Science Bowl regularly asks you to convert between period and frequency.

#### Energy and Amplitude
The energy carried by a wave is proportional to the square of its amplitude. Double the amplitude → four times the energy. A loud sound has larger amplitude than a quiet one; a bright light has larger amplitude than a dim one. Frequency doesn't determine energy for mechanical waves at the MS level (it does for photons, which is HS content).

#### Review Questions
1. A wave has frequency 200 Hz and wavelength 1.7 m. What is its speed?
2. What is the period of a wave with frequency 50 Hz?
3. If amplitude doubles, how does the energy carried by the wave change?

### Wave Interactions: Reflection, Refraction, and Diffraction
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-wave-interactions
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** reflection · refraction · diffraction · interference · wave behavior at boundaries
**Summary:** Waves interact with boundaries and other waves through reflection, refraction, diffraction, and interference.

#### Reflection
When a wave hits a boundary and bounces back, that's **reflection**. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection (angle measured from the normal — a line perpendicular to the surface). Echoes are sound reflections. Mirrors reflect light. Sonar uses sound reflection to map the ocean floor. Standing waves form when a reflected wave interferes with the incoming wave — critical for musical instruments.

#### Refraction
When a wave crosses from one medium into another and changes speed, it bends — this is **refraction**. A straw in a glass of water looks bent because light slows when it enters water, changing direction. The amount of bending depends on how much the speed changes. Lenses use refraction to focus light. Sound refracts in the atmosphere when temperature layers change its speed, which is why sounds carry farther at night.

#### Diffraction and Interference
**Diffraction:** waves bend around obstacles or through openings. The smaller the opening relative to wavelength, the more bending occurs. That's why you can hear a conversation around a corner (long-wavelength sound diffracts well) but not see around it (short-wavelength light diffracts minimally). **Constructive interference:** two waves overlap crest-to-crest → larger amplitude. **Destructive interference:** crest meets trough → smaller (or zero) amplitude. Noise-canceling headphones use destructive interference.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the law of reflection?
2. Why does a straw appear bent when placed in a glass of water?
3. What is the difference between constructive and destructive interference?

### Measuring Waves: Worked Problems
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-wave-problems
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** v=fλ · period · frequency · wavelength · unit analysis
**Summary:** Apply wave speed, frequency, wavelength, and period equations to solve multi-step wave problems.

#### Worked Example 1: Finding Wavelength
Radio station 101.1 FM broadcasts at 101.1 MHz (1.011 × 10⁸ Hz). Radio waves travel at 3 × 10⁸ m/s (speed of light). Find the wavelength: λ = v/f = (3 × 10⁸) / (1.011 × 10⁸) ≈ 2.97 m. FM radio waves have wavelengths of about 3 meters — much larger than visible light (~500 nm) but much smaller than AM waves (~300 m).

#### Worked Example 2: Period and Frequency
A tuning fork vibrates at 440 Hz (concert A). Find the period: T = 1/f = 1/440 ≈ 0.00227 s = 2.27 ms. Find the wavelength in air (v = 343 m/s): λ = v/f = 343/440 ≈ 0.78 m. This is about 78 cm — roughly the length of a guitar string vibrating at that pitch.

#### Worked Example 3: Ripple Tank
Water waves in a ripple tank have a frequency of 5 Hz and a wavelength of 0.04 m. Speed = 5 × 0.04 = 0.2 m/s. If frequency doubles to 10 Hz (same medium, same speed), new wavelength = 0.2/10 = 0.02 m — wavelength halved as frequency doubled.

#### Review Questions
1. A wave has speed 300 m/s and frequency 150 Hz. Find its wavelength and period.
2. A wave has wavelength 0.5 m and period 0.1 s. Find its frequency and wave speed.
3. If the frequency of a wave doubles while its speed stays constant, what happens to its wavelength?

## Subtopic: Sound Waves & the Doppler Effect

### How Sound Works: Compression, Rarefaction, and the Medium
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-sound-basics
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** sound wave · compression · rarefaction · longitudinal wave · medium · speed of sound
**Summary:** Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave produced by vibrations that create alternating compressions and rarefactions in a medium.

#### Sound as a Longitudinal Wave
Sound is produced when an object vibrates — a guitar string, vocal cords, a speaker cone. These vibrations push air molecules back and forth, creating regions of compression (high pressure, molecules crowded together) and rarefaction (low pressure, molecules spread apart). These pressure variations travel outward as a longitudinal wave. In compressions, particles move in the same direction as wave propagation; in rarefactions, they spread back. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum — no medium, no sound.

#### Speed of Sound in Different Media
Sound speed depends on the medium's properties (elasticity and density). **In air at 20°C:** ~343 m/s. **In water:** ~1,480 m/s (about 4× faster). **In steel:** ~5,100 m/s (about 15× faster). Sound travels faster in denser, stiffer materials because molecules are closer together and can pass vibrations along faster. Temperature also matters: sound travels faster in warmer air (molecules move faster). Every 1°C rise in temperature increases sound speed in air by about 0.6 m/s.

#### Pitch and Frequency, Volume and Amplitude
**Pitch** (how high or low a sound sounds) corresponds to **frequency**. High-pitched sounds (piccolo, whistle) have high frequencies; low-pitched sounds (bass guitar, thunder) have low frequencies. Human hearing: ~20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Sounds below 20 Hz are infrasound (earthquakes, elephants); above 20,000 Hz are ultrasound (bats, medical imaging). **Volume/loudness** corresponds to **amplitude** — larger amplitude means more air pressure variation, more energy, louder sound.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between a compression and a rarefaction in a sound wave?
2. In which medium does sound travel fastest: air, water, or steel? Explain why.
3. A bat uses 50,000 Hz sound waves. Is this in the human hearing range? What is this called?

### Pitch, Loudness, and the Decibel Scale
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-sound-pitch-loudness
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** pitch · frequency · loudness · amplitude · decibel · resonance
**Summary:** Pitch corresponds to frequency and loudness to amplitude; the decibel scale measures sound intensity on a logarithmic scale.

#### Frequency and Pitch
Frequency determines pitch. Standard tuning: A above middle C = 440 Hz. A piano covers roughly 27.5 Hz (lowest note) to 4,186 Hz (highest key). The human voice: adult male fundamental ≈ 85–180 Hz; adult female ≈ 165–255 Hz. In musical instruments: shorter strings, higher tension, or smaller air columns produce higher frequency (higher pitch). Doubling frequency raises pitch by exactly one octave.

#### Amplitude and Loudness: The Decibel Scale
Loudness is measured in decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale. Every 10 dB increase represents a tenfold increase in sound intensity. Reference levels: 0 dB = threshold of hearing; 60 dB = normal conversation; 85 dB = heavy traffic (prolonged exposure causes hearing damage); 120 dB = rock concert (pain threshold); 194 dB = loudest sound possible in Earth's atmosphere. Logarithmic scaling means that a 20 dB sound is not twice as loud as a 10 dB sound — it's 10 times more intense.

#### Resonance
Every object has a natural frequency at which it vibrates most easily. When a driving frequency matches the natural frequency, energy builds up dramatically — this is **resonance**. A singer can shatter a wine glass by singing at its resonant frequency. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940) collapsed partly due to wind-driven resonance. Resonance is how string instruments, wind instruments, and many electronics work.

#### Review Questions
1. If you double the frequency of a sound, what happens to its pitch? Its wavelength (if speed stays constant)?
2. How many times more intense is a 70 dB sound than a 50 dB sound?
3. What is resonance and give one example of it in everyday life?

### The Doppler Effect: Why Pitch Shifts with Motion
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-doppler-effect
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** Doppler effect · frequency shift · approaching · receding · speed of source
**Summary:** The Doppler effect is the apparent change in frequency observed when a source of waves moves relative to the observer.

#### What Causes the Doppler Effect?
When a sound source moves toward you, each successive compression is emitted from a slightly closer position, so the compressions arrive more frequently — you hear a higher pitch. When the source moves away, compressions spread out — lower pitch. This apparent frequency shift is the **Doppler effect**, named for Austrian physicist Christian Doppler (1842). The effect applies to all waves — sound, light, and water waves.

#### Everyday Examples
- A fire engine siren sounds higher as it approaches and drops to a lower pitch as it passes.
- Race cars sound different approaching vs. receding.
- Radar guns use the Doppler effect to measure the speed of cars and pitches.
- Bats use Doppler-shifted echoes to detect if prey is moving toward or away from them.
- Ultrasound Doppler imaging tracks blood flow in vessels by detecting frequency shifts.

#### The Doppler Effect and Light: Redshift and Blueshift
Light also shows the Doppler effect. If a star or galaxy moves away from Earth, its light shifts to longer (redder) wavelengths — **redshift**. If it moves toward Earth, light shifts to shorter (bluer) wavelengths — **blueshift**. Edwin Hubble observed that almost all galaxies show redshift — evidence that the universe is expanding. This is one of the key pieces of evidence for the Big Bang theory.

#### Review Questions
1. You're standing on a street corner as an ambulance drives toward you and then past you. How does the pitch of the siren change?
2. What does the Doppler effect on light from distant galaxies tell us about the universe?
3. A bat emits a 50,000 Hz pulse toward a moth. The returning echo is 50,200 Hz. Is the moth moving toward or away from the bat?

### Sound Waves in Technology
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-sound-technology
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** sonar · ultrasound · resonance · Doppler radar · echo
**Summary:** Apply sound wave principles to real technologies: sonar, medical ultrasound, Doppler radar, and musical instruments.

#### Sonar: Mapping the Ocean
SONAR (Sound Navigation And Ranging) uses echo timing to measure depth. A pulse of sound is emitted; the time until the echo returns gives the distance: d = v × (t/2), where t is the round-trip time and v ≈ 1,480 m/s in seawater. If an echo returns in 3 seconds: d = 1480 × 1.5 = 2,220 m. Submarines, fishing fleets, and ocean mapping all rely on sonar. Bats and dolphins use biological sonar called echolocation.

#### Medical Ultrasound
Ultrasound imaging uses high-frequency sound (1–20 MHz) to create images of internal organs. The probe emits pulses; reflections from different tissue boundaries return at different times. Harder tissues reflect more strongly. No radiation is used — ultrasound is considered safe for prenatal imaging. Doppler ultrasound can detect blood flow velocity from the frequency shift of the returning pulses.

#### Musical Instruments and Resonance
Strings, pipes, and membranes all work through resonance. A guitar string plucked at a certain tension produces a specific fundamental frequency. Placing a finger on the string shortens the vibrating length, raising the frequency (higher pitch). Wind instruments use resonance in air columns: shorter tube = higher pitch. A flute is an open pipe; a clarinet is a closed pipe — each produces different patterns of harmonics.

#### Review Questions
1. A sonar pulse is emitted and its echo returns after 2 seconds. The speed of sound in water is 1,500 m/s. How deep is the ocean at that point?
2. Why can ultrasound image soft tissue safely while X-rays cannot?
3. How does shortening a guitar string change its pitch? Use the concept of resonance.

## Subtopic: Light — Reflection & Refraction

### What Is Light? The Electromagnetic Wave
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-light-basics
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** light · electromagnetic wave · transverse wave · speed of light · photon
**Summary:** Light is a transverse electromagnetic wave that travels at 3×10⁸ m/s in a vacuum and does not require a medium.

#### Light as an Electromagnetic Wave
Light is a form of electromagnetic (EM) radiation — a transverse wave of oscillating electric and magnetic fields. Unlike sound, light requires no medium and travels through vacuum at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s (approximately 300,000 km/s). Nothing in the universe can travel faster. Light travels slightly slower in transparent materials: in glass, about 2 × 10⁸ m/s; in water, about 2.25 × 10⁸ m/s. The ratio of light speed in vacuum to speed in a medium is the index of refraction (n = c/v).

#### Visible Light and the Color Spectrum
Visible light occupies a narrow range of the EM spectrum with wavelengths from about 380 nm (violet) to 750 nm (red). **ROY G BIV** in order of increasing wavelength (decreasing frequency and energy): Red, Orange, Yellow, Green, Blue, Indigo, Violet. Red has the longest wavelength; violet has the shortest. White light is a combination of all visible wavelengths. Prisms separate white light into its component colors by refraction (each wavelength bends by a different amount).

#### The Dual Nature of Light
Light behaves as both a wave and a particle (photon). For most MS-level purposes, treat light as a wave. The photon model is needed to explain the photoelectric effect and color of light emitted by hot objects. Each photon carries energy E = hf, where h is Planck's constant and f is frequency. Higher frequency light (violet/UV) carries more energy per photon than lower frequency light (red/IR).

2. Arrange these colors in order of increasing wavelength: blue, red, green, violet.
3. Why doesn't light need a medium to travel, unlike sound?

### Reflection: How Mirrors Work
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-reflection
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** reflection · angle of incidence · angle of reflection · normal · mirror · specular vs diffuse
**Summary:** Reflection occurs when light bounces off a surface; the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, measured from the normal.

#### The Law of Reflection
When light hits a surface and bounces back, the angle of the incoming ray (angle of incidence, θᵢ) equals the angle of the reflected ray (angle of reflection, θᵣ), both measured from the **normal** (a line perpendicular to the surface at the point of contact). Law: θᵢ = θᵣ. This applies to all reflecting surfaces. A ray hitting a mirror at 30° from the normal reflects at 30° from the normal — not 30° from the mirror surface.

#### Specular vs. Diffuse Reflection
**Specular reflection:** from smooth surfaces (mirrors, still water, polished metal) — all rays reflect in parallel, producing a clear image. **Diffuse reflection:** from rough surfaces (paper, walls, skin) — rays scatter in many directions because the surface has many tiny, randomly oriented facets. Most of what you see depends on diffuse reflection — objects scatter light toward your eyes from all directions, making them visible.

#### Plane Mirrors and Virtual Images
A flat (plane) mirror forms a **virtual image** — the image appears to be behind the mirror, at the same distance as the object is in front of it. The image is the same size as the object and laterally inverted (left and right are swapped). The image is not real — you cannot project it onto a screen. Curved mirrors (concave and convex) produce real or virtual images of different sizes, covered in more detail at HS level.

#### Review Questions
1. State the law of reflection.
2. A light ray hits a mirror at 45° to the surface (not the normal). What is the angle of reflection (measured from the normal)?
3. What is the difference between specular and diffuse reflection? Give an example of each.

### Refraction: Why Light Bends
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-refraction
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** refraction · index of refraction · Snell's Law · total internal reflection · lens
**Summary:** Refraction is the bending of light as it passes between media of different optical densities, governed by the change in wave speed.

#### Why Refraction Happens
When light moves from one transparent medium to another, its speed changes. If it hits the boundary at an angle, the direction changes — refraction. Moving from a faster medium to a slower one (e.g., air to glass), light bends toward the normal. Moving from slower to faster (glass to air), light bends away from the normal. The index of refraction (n) tells how much a medium slows light: n = c/v. Air: n ≈ 1.0; water: n ≈ 1.33; glass: n ≈ 1.5; diamond: n ≈ 2.42.

#### Snell's Law (Qualitative)
Snell's Law: n₁ × sin(θ₁) = n₂ × sin(θ₂). At MS level, you need to know: (1) light entering a denser medium bends toward the normal; (2) light entering a less dense medium bends away. The amount of bending depends on the difference in refractive indices. Diamonds have a very high index (2.42) — light bends dramatically inside them, causing the characteristic sparkle (total internal reflection and rainbow-like dispersion).

#### Total Internal Reflection and Fiber Optics
When light travels from a denser to a less dense medium at a steep angle, it can be completely reflected back — **total internal reflection**. This only works when the angle exceeds the critical angle. Fiber optic cables use total internal reflection to guide light signals with almost no loss over thousands of kilometers. The internet and phone calls travel this way.

#### Review Questions
1. A light ray moves from air into glass. Does it bend toward or away from the normal? Why?
2. What is the index of refraction, and what does a high value mean?
3. What is total internal reflection, and what technology uses it?

### Lenses and Optical Instruments
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-lenses-optics
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** convex lens · concave lens · focal point · real image · virtual image · eye · glasses
**Summary:** Apply refraction to understand how convex and concave lenses form images, and how lenses are used in eyes, cameras, and telescopes.

#### Convex Lenses: Converging
A **convex lens** (thicker in the middle) converges parallel rays of light to a point — the **focal point**. When an object is beyond the focal point, the lens forms a real, inverted image on the other side. Cameras, projectors, and the human eye use convex lenses. The closer the object is to the focal point (from outside), the farther away the image forms. Objects closer than the focal point produce a virtual, magnified image (like a magnifying glass).

#### Concave Lenses: Diverging
A **concave lens** (thinner in the middle) spreads rays apart — a diverging lens. It always forms a virtual, upright, reduced image on the same side as the object. Concave lenses are used to correct nearsightedness (myopia) — the eye focuses incoming rays too soon (before the retina), so a concave lens spreads them first.

#### The Human Eye
The cornea and eye lens (both convex) focus light onto the retina. The eye adjusts focus by changing lens thickness (accommodation). Nearsightedness: eyeball too long → focus falls in front of retina → correct with concave lens. Farsightedness: eyeball too short → focus would fall behind retina → correct with convex lens.

#### Review Questions
1. What type of lens is used in a magnifying glass? What kind of image does it produce?
2. What type of lens corrects nearsightedness?
3. How does the human eye change focus for near vs. far objects?

## Subtopic: The Electromagnetic Spectrum

### The Full Electromagnetic Spectrum: From Radio Waves to Gamma Rays
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-em-spectrum
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** electromagnetic spectrum · radio waves · microwaves · infrared · visible · ultraviolet · X-rays · gamma rays
**Summary:** The electromagnetic spectrum organizes all EM radiation by wavelength and frequency, from long-wavelength radio waves to short-wavelength gamma rays.

#### The Seven Regions
From longest wavelength (lowest frequency/energy) to shortest (highest frequency/energy): **Radio waves → Microwaves → Infrared → Visible → Ultraviolet → X-rays → Gamma rays**. Memory trick: **R**eal **M**en **I**n **V**egas **U**se **X**tra-large **G**ambits. All EM waves travel at c = 3×10⁸ m/s in vacuum. Only the frequency/wavelength differs. As wavelength decreases, frequency and energy increase.

#### Wavelength Ranges (Approximate)
- Radio: > 1 mm (up to km-scale)
- Microwave: 1 mm – 1 m
- Infrared: 700 nm – 1 mm
- Visible: 380–700 nm
- Ultraviolet: 10–380 nm
- X-rays: 0.01–10 nm
- Gamma rays: < 0.01 nm (highest energy)

#### Uses of Each Region
Radio: communication (AM/FM/TV), radar. Microwave: microwave ovens (water absorption at 2.45 GHz), satellite communication, cell phones. Infrared: heat lamps, night-vision, TV remotes, thermal imaging. Visible: vision, photography, lighting. UV: sterilization, tanning, black lights, detecting forged documents. X-rays: medical imaging, airport security. Gamma: cancer treatment, nuclear reactions, sterilizing medical equipment.

2. Which type of EM radiation has the longest wavelength? The shortest?
3. Why can gamma rays penetrate matter more easily than radio waves?

### Electromagnetic Radiation in Medicine and Technology
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-em-spectrum-applications
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** X-rays · infrared · UV · microwave · medical imaging · technology
**Summary:** Apply knowledge of the electromagnetic spectrum to real-world medical and technological applications.

#### X-Rays in Medicine
X-rays penetrate soft tissue but are absorbed by dense tissue (bone, metal). The shadow image on X-ray film shows bones as white (absorbed X-rays) and soft tissue as gray (some absorption) or black (X-rays pass through). Computed Tomography (CT) scans take multiple X-ray images from different angles and combine them for 3D imaging. X-ray radiation can damage DNA — medical doses are minimized, and protective lead aprons are used.

#### Infrared: Heat Vision and Remote Controls
All objects emit infrared radiation based on temperature. Thermal cameras detect IR to "see" heat — used by firefighters to find people in smoke, by military for night vision, and by scientists to track heat loss in buildings. TV remote controls use near-infrared LEDs — press a button, the remote pulses IR at a specific frequency, and the TV sensor reads the code. No sound, no radio interference — simple and cheap.

#### UV Light: Benefits and Dangers
Ultraviolet light (UV) has enough energy to damage DNA — the cause of sunburns and a risk factor for skin cancer. Ozone in the stratosphere absorbs most UV-B and UV-C from the Sun. UV-A (longest UV wavelength) gets through more. Benefits: UV light kills bacteria and viruses (used to sterilize hospitals and water systems); UV causes fluorescent materials to glow (black lights in entertainment and forensics); UV helps skin produce vitamin D.

#### Review Questions
1. Why are X-rays used for medical imaging but not radio waves?
2. How does a thermal camera detect body heat?
3. Why does the ozone layer matter for life on Earth?
