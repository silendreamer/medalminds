#### What Are Isotopes?
**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element (same atomic number, same number of protons) that have different numbers of neutrons — and therefore different mass numbers. Examples:
- Carbon-12 (¹²C): 6 protons, 6 neutrons → most abundant (98.9%)
- Carbon-13 (¹³C): 6 protons, 7 neutrons → stable
- Carbon-14 (¹⁴C): 6 protons, 8 neutrons → radioactive; used in radiocarbon dating

All three are carbon (same chemical behavior), but they have different masses. Isotopes are written as: element name-mass number (e.g., Carbon-14) or with a superscript (¹⁴C).

#### Why Atomic Mass Is a Decimal
The atomic mass on the periodic table is a **weighted average** of the masses of all naturally occurring isotopes of an element, weighted by abundance. For example, chlorine has two stable isotopes: Cl-35 (75.77% abundant) and Cl-37 (24.23% abundant). Weighted average ≈ 35.45 — that's why the periodic table shows 35.45 for chlorine, not a whole number.

#### Important Isotopes to Know
- **Hydrogen-1 (protium)**: 1 proton, 0 neutrons — most abundant H (99.98%)
- **Hydrogen-2 (deuterium)**: 1 proton, 1 neutron — heavy hydrogen
- **Hydrogen-3 (tritium)**: 1 proton, 2 neutrons — radioactive
- **Carbon-14**: radioactive; half-life = 5,730 years; used for dating organic materials up to ~50,000 years old
- **Uranium-235**: used as fuel in nuclear fission reactors
- **Uranium-238**: most abundant uranium isotope; not fissile (can be converted to Pu-239)
