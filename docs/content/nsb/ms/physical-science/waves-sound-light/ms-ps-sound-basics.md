#### Sound as a Longitudinal Wave
Sound is produced when an object vibrates — a guitar string, vocal cords, a speaker cone. These vibrations push air molecules back and forth, creating regions of compression (high pressure, molecules crowded together) and rarefaction (low pressure, molecules spread apart). These pressure variations travel outward as a longitudinal wave. In compressions, particles move in the same direction as wave propagation; in rarefactions, they spread back. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum — no medium, no sound.

#### Speed of Sound in Different Media
Sound speed depends on the medium's properties (elasticity and density). **In air at 20°C:** ~343 m/s. **In water:** ~1,480 m/s (about 4× faster). **In steel:** ~5,100 m/s (about 15× faster). Sound travels faster in denser, stiffer materials because molecules are closer together and can pass vibrations along faster. Temperature also matters: sound travels faster in warmer air (molecules move faster). Every 1°C rise in temperature increases sound speed in air by about 0.6 m/s.

#### Pitch and Frequency, Volume and Amplitude
**Pitch** (how high or low a sound sounds) corresponds to **frequency**. High-pitched sounds (piccolo, whistle) have high frequencies; low-pitched sounds (bass guitar, thunder) have low frequencies. Human hearing: ~20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Sounds below 20 Hz are infrasound (earthquakes, elephants); above 20,000 Hz are ultrasound (bats, medical imaging). **Volume/loudness** corresponds to **amplitude** — larger amplitude means more air pressure variation, more energy, louder sound.
