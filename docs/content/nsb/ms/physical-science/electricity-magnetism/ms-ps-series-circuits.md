#### How a Series Circuit Works
In a **series circuit**, components are connected end-to-end in a single loop. There is only one path for current to flow. If any part of the loop breaks (a bulb burns out), the entire circuit stops — no alternative path exists. Total resistance: **R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...** Resistances simply add up in series. If three 4 Ω resistors are in series: R_total = 12 Ω.

#### Current and Voltage in Series
Current is the same at every point in a series circuit (only one path, so the same charge flows through all components): I_total = I₁ = I₂ = I₃. Voltage divides among components proportional to their resistance: V_total = V₁ + V₂ + V₃. A larger resistor gets a larger share of the voltage. With 12 V total, two equal 4 Ω resistors each get 6 V. This is Ohm's Law applied to each component: V_i = I × R_i.

#### Real Series Circuit Example
Holiday lights were once wired in series — one burned-out bulb made the whole string go dark. Modern strings use parallel wiring or bypass paths. Smoke detectors connected in series in a home: if any one detector fails, all lose power. This is why series wiring is rarely used for critical safety systems. Batteries in series add voltages: two 1.5 V AA batteries in series → 3 V total.
