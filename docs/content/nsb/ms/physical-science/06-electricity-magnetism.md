## Subtopic: Electric Charge & Static Electricity

### Electric Charge: Protons, Electrons, and the Two Types
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-electric-charge
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** electric charge · proton · electron · positive · negative · neutral · coulomb
**Summary:** Electric charge comes in two types — positive (protons) and negative (electrons) — and like charges repel while opposite charges attract.

#### The Two Types of Charge
Matter is made of atoms. Inside atoms: **protons** (positive charge, in the nucleus), **neutrons** (neutral, in the nucleus), and **electrons** (negative charge, orbiting the nucleus). Normal atoms have equal numbers of protons and electrons → net charge of zero (neutral). If an atom gains electrons, it becomes negatively charged (anion). If it loses electrons, it becomes positively charged (cation). Only electrons move freely between objects — protons stay fixed in the nucleus.

#### Like and Unlike Charges
The fundamental rule: **like charges repel; unlike charges attract**. Two positive charges push each other apart; two negatives push each other apart; a positive and a negative pull toward each other. This is the electrostatic force. The SI unit of charge is the **coulomb (C)**. One electron carries −1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C of charge. A one-coulomb charge would require about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons — an enormous number.

#### Conservation of Charge
Electric charge is conserved — it cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred. When you rub a balloon on your hair, electrons move from your hair to the balloon. The balloon gains negative charge; your hair becomes positively charged. The total charge of the system hasn't changed. This is why hair sticks to a negatively charged balloon — opposite charges attract.

#### Review Questions
1. What gives an object a negative charge? A positive charge?
2. Two charged spheres repel each other. Are they the same type of charge or different?
3. If a balloon gains 10 units of negative charge by rubbing against wool, what charge does the wool acquire?

### Static Electricity: Buildup, Discharge, and Grounding
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-static-electricity
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** static electricity · charging by friction · induction · conduction · grounding · lightning
**Summary:** Static electricity is the buildup of electric charge on an object's surface, which can transfer suddenly as a spark when discharged.

#### How Charge Builds Up
**Charging by friction (triboelectric effect):** Rubbing two materials together transfers electrons from one to the other. Which gains or loses depends on the triboelectric series. Rubber (balloon) gains electrons from wool. Glass rubbed with silk loses electrons to silk. **Charging by conduction:** touching a charged object transfers charge directly. **Charging by induction:** a charged object brought near (not touching) a neutral object causes charge redistribution — no electrons transfer, but the neutral object develops areas of positive and negative charge (polarization).

#### Sparks and Discharge
Charge builds up until the electric force is strong enough to push electrons through air. Air is normally an insulator, but a large enough voltage ionizes it, allowing current to flow briefly — a spark. Common examples: shock from a doorknob after shuffling on carpet; lightning (discharge between cloud and ground or between clouds). A single lightning bolt discharges 5–30 coulombs over 30 microseconds.

#### Grounding
Grounding means connecting a charged object to the Earth (or a large conductor), which has an effectively infinite capacity to absorb or supply charge. This neutralizes the object. Lightning rods work by grounding — they provide a low-resistance path for lightning to reach the ground safely, protecting buildings. Electronic equipment is grounded to prevent static buildup from damaging sensitive circuits.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between charging by friction, conduction, and induction?
2. Why do you get a shock when touching a metal doorknob after walking on carpet?
3. How does a lightning rod protect a building?

### Coulomb's Law: Force Between Charges
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-coulombs-law
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** Coulomb's Law · electrostatic force · distance · charge magnitude · inverse-square
**Summary:** Coulomb's Law describes how the force between two charges depends on their magnitudes and the distance between them.

#### Coulomb's Law
The electrostatic force between two charges: F = k × q₁ × q₂ / r², where k ≈ 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C², q₁ and q₂ are the charges in coulombs, and r is the distance between them. Key patterns: (1) Force is **directly proportional** to each charge — double one charge, double the force. (2) Force is **inversely proportional to distance squared** — double the distance, force drops to one-fourth. This inverse-square relationship is identical in form to Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation.

#### Comparing Gravitational and Electrostatic Force
Both forces: act at a distance, follow inverse-square law, are proportional to the "source quantities" (mass for gravity, charge for electric). Differences: gravity is always attractive; electrostatic can be attractive or repulsive. Electrostatic force is roughly 10³⁶ times stronger than gravity between two protons. Yet gravity dominates at cosmic scales because large objects are electrically neutral.

#### Electric Fields
A charge creates an **electric field** in the space around it — a region where other charges feel a force. Electric field lines point away from positive charges and toward negative charges. Field lines never cross. The density of field lines represents the field strength. Science Bowl may ask you to identify charge type from a field-line diagram.

#### Review Questions
1. Two charges are 2 m apart. If the distance doubles to 4 m, how does the force change?
2. What is the direction of the electric force between two negative charges?
3. In Coulomb's Law, which variable is in the denominator and why does that matter?

### Static Electricity in Real Life
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-static-electricity-applications
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** static electricity · photocopier · electrostatic precipitator · lightning · Van de Graaff
**Summary:** Apply static electricity principles to understand photocopiers, electrostatic precipitators, lightning protection, and laboratory demonstrations.

#### Photocopiers and Laser Printers
A photocopier works by static electricity. A drum is given a uniform positive charge. A laser beam shines the image onto the drum, neutralizing charges where light hits (light areas). Toner (negatively charged powder) sticks only to the remaining positively charged areas (dark areas of the image). The toner is then transferred and fused onto paper with heat. Laser printers work the same way. This is called **xerography** (Greek: "dry writing").

#### Electrostatic Precipitators
Smokestacks in power plants and factories use electrostatic precipitators to remove pollution particles. A high-voltage wire gives particles a negative charge as they pass by. Positively charged collector plates attract the particles, pulling them out of the exhaust before it exits the chimney. Efficiency can reach 99%+. This technology has dramatically reduced particulate pollution from power plants.

#### Van de Graaff Generators
The Van de Graaff generator uses a moving belt to accumulate charge on a metal sphere until voltages of millions of volts build up. If you touch the sphere, your hair stands on end — each hair gains the same charge and repels the others. The machine can produce 1–3 m long sparks in large models. It's used in physics demonstrations and as a particle accelerator in scientific research.

#### Review Questions
1. How does a laser printer use static electricity to put ink on paper?
2. How does an electrostatic precipitator reduce air pollution?
3. Why does hair stand on end when someone touches a Van de Graaff generator?

## Subtopic: Current, Voltage & Resistance

### Electric Current: Charge in Motion
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-electric-current
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** electric current · ampere · conductor · insulator · drift velocity · conventional current
**Summary:** Electric current is the flow of electric charge through a conductor, measured in amperes, driven by a potential difference (voltage).

#### What Is Electric Current?
Electric current (I) is the rate at which electric charge flows past a point: I = Q/t, where Q is charge in coulombs and t is time in seconds. The unit is the **ampere (A)**: 1 A = 1 C/s. Current flows through **conductors** — materials with free electrons that move easily. Metals (copper, aluminum, silver) are excellent conductors. **Insulators** (rubber, glass, plastic, wood) resist current flow. **Semiconductors** (silicon, germanium) conduct partially — used in transistors and computer chips.

#### Conventional Current vs. Electron Flow
Historically, current was defined as flowing from positive to negative terminals (conventional current) before we knew electrons carry charge. Electrons actually flow from negative to positive (opposite direction). Most physics uses conventional current direction. In circuit diagrams, current arrows point from + to −. Don't confuse this with electron flow — they're opposite, but both are "correct" depending on convention used.

#### Conductors at the Microscopic Level
In a metallic conductor, electrons don't really "run" through the wire. They drift slowly (about 1 mm/s on average) but the electric signal propagates nearly at the speed of light. Think of a pipe already full of water — pushing water in one end almost instantly pushes water out the other end. The electrical energy propagates as an electromagnetic field along the conductor, not as electron movement across the length.

3. If 6 coulombs of charge flow past a point in 2 seconds, what is the current?

### Voltage and Resistance: The Pressure and Friction of Electricity
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-voltage-resistance
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** voltage · potential difference · volt · resistance · ohm · Ohm's Law · V=IR
**Summary:** Voltage is the electric potential difference that drives current, while resistance opposes current flow; they're linked by Ohm's Law: V = IR.

#### Voltage: The Electric "Pressure"
**Voltage** (V), also called potential difference, is the amount of energy per unit charge: V = energy/charge = E/Q. Measured in **volts (V)**; 1 V = 1 J/C. Voltage is the driving force that pushes current through a circuit. A 9 V battery provides 9 joules of energy for every coulomb of charge it moves. Household outlets in the US provide 120 V AC. Voltage is always measured between two points — "across" a component.

#### Resistance: The Opposition to Current
**Resistance (R)** is a material's opposition to the flow of current. Measured in **ohms (Ω)**; 1 Ω = 1 V/A. Factors affecting resistance: length of conductor (longer → more resistance), cross-sectional area (thicker → less resistance), material (copper has low R; nichrome has high R), and temperature (hotter → more resistance in metals). Resistors convert electrical energy to heat (and sometimes light, as in a light bulb filament).

#### Ohm's Law: V = IR
Ohm's Law: **V = I × R** (voltage = current × resistance). This equation governs most circuit analysis. If voltage is 12 V and resistance is 4 Ω, current = V/R = 12/4 = 3 A. If current is 2 A and resistance is 6 Ω, voltage = 2 × 6 = 12 V. Rearrangements: I = V/R; R = V/I. Ohm's Law applies to ohmic materials (metals at constant temperature). Some components (diodes, LEDs) are non-ohmic.

#### Review Questions
1. What is voltage and what is its SI unit?
2. State Ohm's Law and give one example calculation.
3. A toaster has resistance 24 Ω connected to a 120 V outlet. What current flows through it?

### Electric Power: Energy and Cost
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-electric-power
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** electric power · watt · P=IV · P=V²/R · kilowatt-hour · energy cost
**Summary:** Electric power measures the rate of energy transfer; combined with Ohm's Law, it lets you calculate energy use and electricity costs.

#### Defining Electric Power
**Power (P)** is energy transferred per unit time: P = E/t. For electricity: **P = I × V** (power = current × voltage). The unit is the **watt (W)**; 1 W = 1 J/s. A 100 W light bulb converts 100 joules of electrical energy to light and heat every second. Combining with Ohm's Law: P = I²R; P = V²/R. These three forms let you find power given any two of V, I, R.

#### Kilowatt-Hours and Electricity Bills
Energy is measured in joules, but electric companies bill in **kilowatt-hours (kWh)**: 1 kWh = 1,000 W × 3,600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J. A 1,000 W (1 kW) device running for 1 hour uses 1 kWh. A typical US home uses ~900 kWh/month. At $0.12/kWh, that's about $108/month. Energy calculation: E (kWh) = P (kW) × t (hours). A 60 W bulb running 10 hours: E = 0.06 × 10 = 0.6 kWh.

#### Power, Heat, and Safety
Resistance converts electrical energy into heat (Joule heating): P = I²R. High current through a resistor generates significant heat. Fuses and circuit breakers protect circuits by interrupting current when it exceeds a safe level (preventing overheating/fire). A fuse is a thin wire that melts at the rated current. A circuit breaker uses an electromagnet or bimetallic strip to switch off automatically.

#### Review Questions
1. A device draws 3 A from a 120 V outlet. What is its power?
2. How much energy (in kWh) does a 500 W microwave use in 30 minutes?
3. Why do high-power devices use thick wires rather than thin ones?

### Ohm's Law in Practice: Circuit Calculations
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-ohms-law-practice
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** V=IR · P=IV · circuit analysis · ammeter · voltmeter · problem solving
**Summary:** Apply Ohm's Law and power equations to solve realistic circuit calculation problems step by step.

#### Worked Example 1: Finding Current
A lamp has resistance 60 Ω and is plugged into a 120 V outlet. Find: (a) current; (b) power; (c) energy used in 3 hours. (a) I = V/R = 120/60 = 2 A. (b) P = IV = 2 × 120 = 240 W. (c) E = P × t = 240 W × (3 × 3600 s) = 2,592,000 J = 2.592 MJ, or 0.72 kWh.

#### Worked Example 2: Finding Resistance
A hair dryer runs at 1,500 W on a 120 V circuit. Find its resistance. I = P/V = 1500/120 = 12.5 A. R = V/I = 120/12.5 = 9.6 Ω. Alternatively: R = V²/P = (120²)/1500 = 14,400/1500 = 9.6 Ω.

#### Using an Ammeter and Voltmeter
An **ammeter** measures current — it is always connected in **series** (the current to be measured flows through it). An **ammeter** must have very low resistance to avoid altering the circuit. A **voltmeter** measures voltage difference — it is always connected in **parallel** (across the component). A voltmeter must have very high resistance so negligible current flows through it. These placement rules are tested on Science Bowl frequently.

#### Review Questions
1. A resistor has 5 Ω resistance and carries 4 A. Find the voltage across it and the power dissipated.
2. A 60 W bulb is used for 8 hours per day for 30 days. How many kWh does it use?
3. Should an ammeter be connected in series or parallel? Why?

## Subtopic: Series & Parallel Circuits

### Series Circuits: One Path for Current
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-series-circuits
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** series circuit · total resistance · single path · current same throughout · voltage divides
**Summary:** In a series circuit, all components share a single path, current is the same throughout, and resistances add up directly.

#### How a Series Circuit Works
In a **series circuit**, components are connected end-to-end in a single loop. There is only one path for current to flow. If any part of the loop breaks (a bulb burns out), the entire circuit stops — no alternative path exists. Total resistance: **R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...** Resistances simply add up in series. If three 4 Ω resistors are in series: R_total = 12 Ω.

#### Current and Voltage in Series
Current is the same at every point in a series circuit (only one path, so the same charge flows through all components): I_total = I₁ = I₂ = I₃. Voltage divides among components proportional to their resistance: V_total = V₁ + V₂ + V₃. A larger resistor gets a larger share of the voltage. With 12 V total, two equal 4 Ω resistors each get 6 V. This is Ohm's Law applied to each component: V_i = I × R_i.

#### Real Series Circuit Example
Holiday lights were once wired in series — one burned-out bulb made the whole string go dark. Modern strings use parallel wiring or bypass paths. Smoke detectors connected in series in a home: if any one detector fails, all lose power. This is why series wiring is rarely used for critical safety systems. Batteries in series add voltages: two 1.5 V AA batteries in series → 3 V total.

#### Review Questions
1. In a series circuit, what happens to the rest of the circuit if one bulb burns out?
2. Three resistors of 2 Ω, 5 Ω, and 3 Ω are in series with a 20 V battery. What is the total resistance and the current?
3. How does voltage divide among resistors in a series circuit?

### Parallel Circuits: Multiple Paths for Current
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-parallel-circuits
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** parallel circuit · multiple paths · voltage same throughout · current divides · total resistance decreases
**Summary:** In a parallel circuit, components share the same voltage, current divides among branches, and adding more branches decreases total resistance.

#### How a Parallel Circuit Works
In a **parallel circuit**, components are connected side-by-side between the same two points. Each component has its own independent path. If one path breaks (a bulb burns out), current continues through the other paths. Voltage is the same across all branches: V₁ = V₂ = V₃ = V_total. This is why household wiring is parallel — every appliance gets the full 120 V, and one failing device doesn't affect others.

#### Current and Resistance in Parallel
Current divides among branches: I_total = I₁ + I₂ + I₃. More current flows through the branch with less resistance (I = V/R). For two resistors in parallel: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂. The total resistance is always LESS than the smallest branch resistance. Adding more branches gives current more paths, reducing total resistance. This means more current flows from the battery when more devices are plugged in.

#### Why Homes Use Parallel Wiring
In parallel: each device gets full voltage (required for proper operation), failing one device doesn't affect others, you can turn devices on/off independently. The trade-off: as more devices are added, total resistance drops, total current increases, and eventually the circuit can overheat → circuit breaker trips. "Overloading a circuit" means too many high-power devices on the same parallel branch.

#### Review Questions

2. Two resistors (6 Ω and 3 Ω) are connected in parallel to a 12 V battery. Find the total resistance and total current.
3. Why does adding more devices in parallel increase the total current drawn from the battery?

### Series vs. Parallel: Comparing the Two
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-series-vs-parallel
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** series · parallel · comparison · circuit diagrams · household wiring · fault tolerance
**Summary:** Compare series and parallel circuits systematically, including how each responds to component failure and how current, voltage, and resistance behave differently.

#### Side-by-Side Comparison

| Property | Series | Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Current | Same throughout | Divides among branches |
| Voltage | Divides among components | Same across all branches |
| Resistance | R_total = R₁+R₂+... (increases) | 1/R_T = 1/R₁+1/R₂+... (decreases) |
| One component fails | Whole circuit breaks | Rest of circuit unaffected |
| Use case | Simple switches, old lights | Household wiring, most electronics |

#### Reading Circuit Diagrams
Standard symbols: battery (long/short alternating lines), resistor (zigzag), bulb (circle with X), switch (gap in line), ammeter (A in circle, series), voltmeter (V in circle, parallel). Series elements are on the same branch; parallel elements are on separate branches between the same two nodes. Practice identifying from diagram whether components are in series or parallel.

#### Mixed Circuits
Real circuits often combine both: a main series element (like a switch) with parallel branches (like multiple bulbs). Use systematic analysis: find equivalent resistance of parallel sections first, then add series resistances.

### Circuit Problem Solving
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-circuit-problems
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** series circuit calculation · parallel circuit calculation · V=IR · circuit analysis · total resistance
**Summary:** Solve multi-step series and parallel circuit problems systematically using Ohm's Law.

#### Worked Example: Series Circuit
Four resistors: R₁ = 2 Ω, R₂ = 3 Ω, R₃ = 5 Ω are in series with a 20 V battery. Step 1: R_total = 2+3+5 = 10 Ω. Step 2: I = V/R = 20/10 = 2 A (same through all resistors). Step 3: Voltage across each: V₁ = 2×2 = 4 V; V₂ = 2×3 = 6 V; V₃ = 2×5 = 10 V. Check: 4+6+10 = 20 V ✓. Step 4: Power in each: P₁ = I²R = 4×2 = 8 W; P₂ = 4×3 = 12 W; P₃ = 4×5 = 20 W. Total = 40 W = IV = 2×20 ✓.

#### Worked Example: Parallel Circuit
Two resistors R₁ = 4 Ω, R₂ = 12 Ω in parallel across 12 V. Step 1: V across each = 12 V (parallel — voltage same). Step 2: I₁ = V/R₁ = 12/4 = 3 A; I₂ = V/R₂ = 12/12 = 1 A. Step 3: I_total = 3+1 = 4 A. Step 4: R_total = V/I = 12/4 = 3 Ω. Verify via formula: 1/R_T = 1/4+1/12 = 3/12+1/12 = 4/12 → R_T = 3 Ω ✓.

#### Identifying Connections in Diagrams
To determine series vs. parallel: trace the current path. If there's only one route from battery + to battery −, it's series. If the path splits into multiple routes that rejoin, those split components are in parallel.

2. A 12 Ω and 6 Ω resistor are in parallel across 24 V. Find total R and total current.
3. A circuit has a 2 Ω resistor in series with two parallel resistors (4 Ω and 4 Ω). The battery is 10 V. Find total R and current from battery.

## Subtopic: Magnetism & Electromagnetism

### Magnetism: Poles, Fields, and Magnetic Materials
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-magnetism-basics
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** magnet · north pole · south pole · magnetic field · magnetic field lines · ferromagnetic materials
**Summary:** Magnets have north and south poles that attract opposites and repel likes; all magnets produce a magnetic field described by field lines.

#### Magnetic Poles
Every magnet has two poles: **north (N)** and **south (S)**. Like poles repel; unlike poles attract (N attracts S; N repels N). This is analogous to electric charges but for magnets. Crucially, you cannot isolate a single magnetic pole — break a magnet in half and you get two smaller magnets, each with both poles. This is the fundamental difference from electric charges, where you can have isolated + or − charges. Magnetic monopoles have never been observed.

#### Magnetic Field Lines
A magnetic field fills the space around a magnet, exerting force on other magnets and magnetic materials. We visualize it with **magnetic field lines**: they exit the north pole, curve through space, and enter the south pole. Where lines are dense: strong field. Where lines are spread: weak field. Field lines never cross. The Earth itself is a giant magnet — its geographic north pole is actually a magnetic south pole (the north end of a compass needle is attracted to it).

#### Ferromagnetic Materials
Only a few materials are strongly magnetic: **iron, nickel, cobalt**, and some alloys (like steel and ferrite). These are **ferromagnetic** materials. Inside them, tiny magnetic regions called **domains** align with an external field. When domains align, the material becomes magnetized. Heating above the **Curie temperature** or repeated mechanical shock disrupts domain alignment, demagnetizing the material.

1. What happens when two north poles are brought near each other?
2. Can you isolate a single magnetic pole? What happens if you cut a magnet in two?
3. Name three ferromagnetic materials.

### Earth's Magnetic Field and Compass Navigation
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-earth-magnetic-field
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** Earth's magnetic field · compass · geographic vs magnetic poles · magnetic declination · aurora
**Summary:** Earth generates a magnetic field from its liquid outer core, which deflects charged particles and guides compass navigation.

#### Earth as a Magnet
Earth's magnetic field is generated by the movement of molten iron in the outer core — the **dynamo effect**. The field resembles a bar magnet tilted about 11° from Earth's rotation axis. The geographic North Pole ≠ the magnetic north pole. A compass needle aligns with Earth's magnetic field and points toward **magnetic north**. The difference between true north and magnetic north at any location is called **magnetic declination** — important for precise navigation.

#### The Magnetosphere: Earth's Shield
Earth's magnetic field extends far into space, forming the **magnetosphere**. It deflects most of the charged particles streaming from the Sun (the solar wind). Without it, these particles would strip away Earth's atmosphere over time (this may have happened to Mars, which has a weak field). The Van Allen radiation belts are regions of trapped charged particles within the magnetosphere.

#### Auroras
When solar wind particles are energetic enough to enter the magnetosphere near the poles, they collide with atmospheric gas molecules, exciting them to glow — **auroras** (aurora borealis in the north, aurora australis in the south). The colors depend on which gas is excited and at what altitude: oxygen at high altitude glows red; at low altitude, green; nitrogen glows blue/purple. Auroras are more intense after solar flares.

#### Review Questions
1. What generates Earth's magnetic field?
2. Why is magnetic north different from geographic north?
3. What causes the northern lights (aurora borealis)?

### Electromagnetism: Current Creates Magnetic Fields
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ms-ps-electromagnetism
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** electromagnetism · Oersted's discovery · right-hand rule · solenoid · electromagnet
**Summary:** Moving electric charges create magnetic fields; this connection between electricity and magnetism, discovered by Oersted, enables electromagnets and countless technologies.

#### Oersted's Discovery (1820)
Hans Christian Oersted discovered that a compass needle deflected when placed near a wire carrying current — current creates a magnetic field. This was the first demonstration that electricity and magnetism are related. The magnetic field wraps around the wire in concentric circles. **Right-hand rule:** point the right-hand thumb in the direction of conventional current; the fingers curl in the direction of the magnetic field.

#### Electromagnets
Wrapping a current-carrying wire into a coil (**solenoid**) concentrates and strengthens the magnetic field. Adding an iron core inside the coil amplifies the field dramatically (the iron's domains align with the coil's field). The result is an **electromagnet** — a magnet you can turn on and off by switching the current. Strength increases with: more turns of wire, higher current, iron core. Electromagnets in MRI machines reach field strengths thousands of times stronger than a fridge magnet.

#### The Motor Effect
A current-carrying wire in an external magnetic field experiences a force — the motor effect. The direction of force is given by the left-hand rule (or Fleming's left-hand rule): thumb = force direction; index = field direction; middle = current direction. This is the principle behind electric motors: current in a coil rotates in a magnetic field, converting electrical energy to rotational mechanical energy.

#### Review Questions
1. Who discovered that electric current creates a magnetic field?
2. How can you increase the strength of an electromagnet?
3. What is the motor effect and what device uses it?

### Electromagnetic Induction: Generators and Transformers
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ms-ps-electromagnetic-induction
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** electromagnetic induction · Faraday's Law · generator · transformer · Lenz's Law
**Summary:** Apply Faraday's Law of electromagnetic induction to understand how generators produce electricity and how transformers change voltage.

#### Faraday's Discovery
Michael Faraday (1831) discovered the reverse of Oersted's finding: a changing magnetic field induces an electric current in a conductor. This is **electromagnetic induction**. No motion = no change in field = no current. Moving a magnet into a coil induces current. Removing it induces current in the opposite direction. Faster motion = stronger induced current. This principle powers every electrical generator.

#### Electric Generators
A **generator** converts mechanical energy to electrical energy using induction. A coil of wire rotates inside a magnetic field (or a magnet rotates inside a coil). The continuously changing magnetic flux induces alternating current (AC). Sources of rotation: steam turbines (powered by coal, nuclear, gas, or geothermal heat), wind turbines, water turbines in hydroelectric dams. This is how virtually all grid electricity is generated.

#### Transformers
A **transformer** changes the voltage of AC electricity using mutual induction between two coils (primary and secondary) wrapped around an iron core. Voltage ratio = turns ratio: V₁/V₂ = N₁/N₂. A step-up transformer (more turns on secondary) increases voltage; a step-down transformer decreases it. Power is conserved (P = IV): higher voltage = lower current. Long-distance transmission uses high voltage (~345,000 V) to minimize resistive power loss (P_loss = I²R).

#### Review Questions
1. What is electromagnetic induction and who discovered it?
2. A transformer has 100 turns on the primary and 1,000 on the secondary, connected to a 12 V source. What is the output voltage?
3. Why is electricity transmitted at high voltage over long distances?
