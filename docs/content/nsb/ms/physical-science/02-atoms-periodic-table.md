## Subtopic: Atomic Structure

### Inside the Atom: Protons, Neutrons, and Electrons
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** inside-the-atom-protons-neutrons-electrons
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** protons · neutrons · electrons · nucleus
**Summary:** Learn the three subatomic particles, their charges, masses, and locations inside the atom.

#### The Three Subatomic Particles
Atoms are the smallest units of matter that still have the chemical identity of an element. But atoms themselves are made of smaller particles:
- **Proton**: positive charge (+1), found in the nucleus, mass ≈ 1 atomic mass unit (amu)
- **Neutron**: no charge (neutral), found in the nucleus, mass ≈ 1 amu
- **Electron**: negative charge (−1), found in the electron cloud (orbitals) outside the nucleus, mass ≈ 1/1836 amu (essentially negligible)

The number of protons defines the element — change the proton count and you have a different element. The number of protons is the **atomic number**.

#### The Nucleus and Electron Cloud
The **nucleus** is the tiny, dense core at the center of the atom — it contains protons and neutrons. Despite holding nearly all the atom's mass, the nucleus is incredibly small compared to the whole atom. If an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be a marble at the center. Most of the atom is empty space occupied by the electron cloud.

**Electrons** occupy regions of space called orbitals outside the nucleus. They move so fast that they form a "cloud" of probability around the nucleus. Electrons are attracted to the positive nucleus by electromagnetic force.

#### Atomic Number vs. Mass Number
- **Atomic number** (Z) = number of protons in the nucleus. This identifies the element.
- **Mass number** (A) = number of protons + number of neutrons. Also called atomic mass (approximately).
- **Number of neutrons** = mass number − atomic number

Example: Carbon has atomic number 6 (6 protons). The most common carbon isotope has mass number 12, so it has 6 neutrons.

In a neutral atom, number of electrons = number of protons (equal positive and negative charges balance out).

#### Review Questions
1. What is the charge, mass, and location of each of the three subatomic particles?
2. An atom has an atomic number of 8 and a mass number of 16. How many protons, neutrons, and electrons does it have?
3. Why is the mass of an atom concentrated in the nucleus rather than the electron cloud?

### Atomic Models: How Our Understanding Changed
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** atomic-models-history
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** Dalton · Thomson · Rutherford · Bohr model · modern model
**Summary:** Trace the evolution of the atomic model from Dalton's solid sphere through Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr, and the modern quantum model.

#### Dalton's Solid Sphere Model (1808)
John Dalton proposed that atoms are solid, indivisible spheres — like tiny billiard balls. He correctly argued that elements consist of atoms and that atoms of different elements differ. He was wrong that atoms are indivisible — subatomic particles were later discovered.

#### Thomson's Plum Pudding Model (1897)
J.J. Thomson discovered the **electron** using cathode ray tubes. He proposed that atoms are a positive "pudding" with negative electrons embedded throughout — like raisins in a pudding. This was the first model to include subatomic particles.

#### Rutherford's Nuclear Model (1911)
Ernest Rutherford conducted the famous **gold foil experiment**: he fired alpha particles (positive, heavy) at a thin gold foil. Most passed straight through (atoms are mostly empty space), but a few bounced back. Conclusion: atoms have a tiny, dense, positively charged **nucleus**. This disproved Thomson's model.

#### Bohr's Planetary Model (1913)
Niels Bohr proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus in specific **energy levels** (like planets orbit the Sun). Electrons can jump between levels by absorbing or emitting specific amounts of energy (photons). This model explains the line spectra of hydrogen.

#### Modern Quantum Model
Today we know electrons don't orbit like planets — they exist in **probability clouds** called orbitals. The exact position of an electron cannot be known precisely (Heisenberg uncertainty principle). Science Bowl sticks largely to Bohr-model vocabulary (energy levels, shells).

#### Review Questions
1. What did Rutherford's gold foil experiment reveal about atomic structure?
2. How does Bohr's model improve on Thomson's plum pudding model?
3. What is the main difference between the Bohr model and the modern quantum model of the atom?

### Isotopes and Atomic Mass
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** isotopes-atomic-mass
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** isotopes · atomic mass · carbon-14 · mass number
**Summary:** Understand what isotopes are, why elements have fractional atomic masses on the periodic table, and how isotopes are used in science.

#### What Are Isotopes?
**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element (same atomic number, same number of protons) that have different numbers of neutrons — and therefore different mass numbers. Examples:
- Carbon-12 (¹²C): 6 protons, 6 neutrons → most abundant (98.9%)
- Carbon-13 (¹³C): 6 protons, 7 neutrons → stable
- Carbon-14 (¹⁴C): 6 protons, 8 neutrons → radioactive; used in radiocarbon dating

All three are carbon (same chemical behavior), but they have different masses. Isotopes are written as: element name-mass number (e.g., Carbon-14) or with a superscript (¹⁴C).

#### Why Atomic Mass Is a Decimal
The atomic mass on the periodic table is a **weighted average** of the masses of all naturally occurring isotopes of an element, weighted by abundance. For example, chlorine has two stable isotopes: Cl-35 (75.77% abundant) and Cl-37 (24.23% abundant). Weighted average ≈ 35.45 — that's why the periodic table shows 35.45 for chlorine, not a whole number.

#### Important Isotopes to Know
- **Hydrogen-1 (protium)**: 1 proton, 0 neutrons — most abundant H (99.98%)
- **Hydrogen-2 (deuterium)**: 1 proton, 1 neutron — heavy hydrogen
- **Hydrogen-3 (tritium)**: 1 proton, 2 neutrons — radioactive
- **Carbon-14**: radioactive; half-life = 5,730 years; used for dating organic materials up to ~50,000 years old
- **Uranium-235**: used as fuel in nuclear fission reactors
- **Uranium-238**: most abundant uranium isotope; not fissile (can be converted to Pu-239)

#### Review Questions
1. What makes two atoms isotopes of each other?
2. Chlorine-35 makes up about 75% of all chlorine atoms, and chlorine-37 makes up about 25%. Estimate the weighted average atomic mass of chlorine.
3. Why is carbon-14 useful for dating archaeological artifacts?

### Atomic Structure in Real Science
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** atomic-structure-real-science
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** ions · ionization · practical applications of atomic structure
**Summary:** Apply atomic structure knowledge to understand ions, ionization, and how atomic structure drives real chemical and technological applications.

#### Ions: Atoms with Unequal Charges
A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. When an atom gains or loses electrons, it becomes an **ion**:
- **Cation** (positive ion): atom LOSES electrons → more protons than electrons → net positive charge. Example: Na⁺ (sodium loses 1 electron).
- **Anion** (negative ion): atom GAINS electrons → more electrons than protons → net negative charge. Example: Cl⁻ (chlorine gains 1 electron).

Ions are how salts form: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract each other to form NaCl (table salt). Many biological processes depend on ions: nerve signals use Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, and Cl⁻ flowing across cell membranes.

#### Applications of Atomic Structure
- **Medical imaging (PET scans)**: use radioactive isotopes (positron-emitting) to image metabolic activity in the brain and body
- **Cancer radiation therapy**: radioactive isotopes target tumors
- **Nuclear power**: uranium and plutonium isotopes split in fission to release energy
- **Smoke detectors**: contain tiny amounts of Americium-241 (radioactive), which ionizes air to detect smoke

#### The Charge on Common Ions
Science Bowl asks about specific charges:
- Group 1 metals: +1 (Na⁺, K⁺, Li⁺)
- Group 2 metals: +2 (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺)
- Aluminum: +3 (Al³⁺)
- Oxygen: −2 (O²⁻)
- Chlorine/Fluorine: −1 (Cl⁻, F⁻)

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between a cation and an anion?
2. If a calcium atom (atomic number 20) loses two electrons, what is its charge and how many electrons does it have?
3. Name one real-world application of radioactive isotopes in medicine.

## Subtopic: Elements & Compounds

### Elements: Pure Single-Element Substances
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** elements-pure-substances
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** elements · atomic symbol · diatomic elements · metals nonmetals metalloids
**Summary:** Understand what elements are, their chemical symbols, diatomic elements, and how elements are categorized as metals, nonmetals, and metalloids.

#### What Is an Element?
An **element** is a pure substance made of only one type of atom. Elements cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means. There are 118 known elements (as of 2024), each listed on the periodic table with a unique symbol. The first 92 elements occur naturally; elements 93–118 are synthetic.

#### Chemical Symbols
Each element has a 1- or 2-letter symbol. Most come from the English name, but some come from Latin:
- Iron = Fe (ferrum)
- Gold = Au (aurum)
- Silver = Ag (argentum)
- Lead = Pb (plumbum)
- Mercury = Hg (hydrargyrum)
- Potassium = K (kalium)
- Sodium = Na (natrium)
- Copper = Cu (cuprum)
- Tungsten = W (wolfram)

These Latin-derived symbols appear frequently in Science Bowl.

#### Diatomic Elements
Seven elements exist naturally as diatomic molecules (pairs of atoms): **H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂**. Memory trick: "Have No Fear Of Ice Cold Beer" (first letter of each). When writing chemical equations, these elements must be written as diatomic. For example, oxygen gas is O₂, not O.

#### Metals, Nonmetals, and Metalloids
- **Metals** (left and center of PT): shiny, conduct heat and electricity, malleable, ductile. ~75% of elements are metals.
- **Nonmetals** (upper right of PT): dull, poor conductors, brittle as solids. Include C, N, O, S, P, halogens, noble gases.
- **Metalloids** (along the staircase): have intermediate properties; semiconductors. Include Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te, At, Po.

#### Review Questions
1. Name four elements whose chemical symbols come from Latin names, and give the Latin word each comes from.
2. Why is oxygen gas written as O₂ rather than just O in a chemical equation?
3. What is a metalloid, and give two examples?

### Compounds: Elements Combined in Fixed Ratios
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** compounds-fixed-ratios
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** compound · chemical formula · law of definite proportions · naming
**Summary:** Learn what makes a compound different from a mixture of elements, how chemical formulas describe compounds, and the law of definite proportions.

#### What Is a Compound?
A **compound** is a pure substance made of two or more different elements chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio. The properties of a compound are completely different from the properties of its constituent elements:
- Hydrogen (H₂) = flammable gas; Oxygen (O₂) = supports combustion; together → Water (H₂O) = liquid that extinguishes fire
- Sodium (Na) = silvery metal that explodes in water; Chlorine (Cl₂) = toxic yellow-green gas; together → Sodium chloride (NaCl) = table salt (harmless white crystals)

#### Law of Definite Proportions
**The Law of Definite Proportions** (Proust's Law): A chemical compound always contains the same elements in the same ratio by mass. For example, water is always 88.9% oxygen and 11.1% hydrogen by mass, regardless of where the sample comes from or how it was made. This distinguishes a compound from a mixture (which can have any ratio).

#### Chemical Formulas
A **chemical formula** shows which elements are in a compound and how many atoms of each. Subscripts indicate the number of atoms:
- H₂O: 2 hydrogen, 1 oxygen
- CO₂: 1 carbon, 2 oxygen
- NaCl: 1 sodium, 1 chlorine
- C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose): 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen, 6 oxygen
- H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid): 2 hydrogen, 1 sulfur, 4 oxygen

#### Key Common Compounds
Water (H₂O), carbon dioxide (CO₂), ammonia (NH₃), salt (NaCl), glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), methane (CH₄), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), hydrochloric acid (HCl).

#### Review Questions
1. How does a compound differ from a mixture of the same elements?
2. How many atoms of each element are in one molecule of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)?
3. State the Law of Definite Proportions and explain what it means.

## Subtopic: Periodic Table Patterns

### Reading the Periodic Table
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** reading-the-periodic-table
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** periods · groups · atomic number · atomic mass
**Summary:** Navigate the periodic table with confidence — understand how elements are arranged, what periods and groups mean, and what the numbers in each element's box represent.

#### How the Periodic Table Is Organized
The periodic table arranges all 118 known elements in order of increasing **atomic number** (number of protons). It's arranged in:
- **Periods** (rows): 7 periods, numbered 1–7. Elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells.
- **Groups** (columns): 18 groups, numbered 1–18 (or IA–VIIIA in the older system). Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons and similar chemical properties.

#### What's in Each Element Box?
Typically:
- **Atomic number** (top): number of protons
- **Chemical symbol** (middle): 1–2 letter abbreviation
- **Element name** (below symbol)
- **Atomic mass** (bottom): weighted average mass in amu

#### Periods and What They Tell Us
- Period 1: H and He (1 electron shell)
- Period 2: Li through Ne (2 shells)
- Period 3: Na through Ar (3 shells)
As you move down the table, each period adds one more electron shell, making atoms larger.

#### Groups and Properties
- Group 1 (Alkali metals): Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr — very reactive metals
- Group 2 (Alkaline earth metals): Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra — reactive metals
- Groups 3–12 (Transition metals): Fe, Cu, Zn, Ag, Au, etc.
- Group 17 (Halogens): F, Cl, Br, I, At — very reactive nonmetals
- Group 18 (Noble gases): He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn — nearly inert

#### Review Questions
1. What does the atomic number of an element tell you?
2. Elements in the same group of the periodic table share what key characteristic?
3. How many electron shells does a period 3 element have?

### Periodic Trends: Patterns Across the Table
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** periodic-trends
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** atomic radius · ionization energy · electronegativity · trends
**Summary:** Learn how atomic radius, ionization energy, and electronegativity change across periods and down groups, and why these trends exist.

#### Atomic Radius
**Atomic radius** is roughly half the distance between adjacent nuclei. Trend:
- **Across a period (left → right)**: radius DECREASES. Why? More protons pull electrons closer, but electrons stay in the same shell.
- **Down a group (top → bottom)**: radius INCREASES. Why? Each new period adds an electron shell farther from the nucleus.

Largest atoms: lower-left of periodic table (Fr, Cs). Smallest atoms: upper-right (He, F).

#### Ionization Energy
**First ionization energy**: energy needed to remove the first electron from a neutral atom. Trend:
- **Across a period**: INCREASES (harder to remove electrons from a smaller atom with more nuclear charge)
- **Down a group**: DECREASES (outer electrons farther from nucleus, easier to remove)

Metals have LOW ionization energies (easily lose electrons). Noble gases have VERY HIGH ionization energies (very stable).

#### Electronegativity
**Electronegativity**: tendency of an atom to attract electrons in a bond. Trend:
- **Across a period**: INCREASES
- **Down a group**: DECREASES

Fluorine (F) has the highest electronegativity of all elements (3.98 on the Pauling scale). Cesium (Cs) and francium (Fr) have the lowest.

#### Memory Device for Trends
For atomic radius: DECREASES going RIGHT and INCREASES going DOWN (remember: "Bigger atoms down and left").

#### Review Questions
1. As you move left to right across a period, what happens to atomic radius and why?
2. Which element has the highest electronegativity? Where is it on the periodic table?
3. Why do ionization energies decrease going down a group?

### Using the Periodic Table: Application
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** periodic-table-application
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** predicting properties · group families · reactivity
**Summary:** Use periodic table position to predict an element's properties, reactivity, and behavior in chemical reactions.

#### Predicting Properties from Group
Knowing a group tells you an element's likely behavior:
- **Alkali metals (Group 1)**: All react vigorously with water to produce hydrogen gas and a metal hydroxide. Reactivity increases down the group (Fr is most reactive). Lithium reacts mildly; potassium ignites; cesium explodes.
- **Halogens (Group 17)**: All react to gain one electron and form −1 ions. F₂ is most reactive (will react with almost everything); I₂ is least reactive of the stable halogens.
- **Noble gases (Group 18)**: Full outer shell → nearly zero reactivity. He, Ne, Ar essentially form no compounds.

#### Predicting Ion Charges
Groups tell you what charge ions will form:
- Group 1 → +1 ions (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺)
- Group 2 → +2 ions (Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺)
- Group 17 → −1 ions (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻)
- Group 16 → −2 ions (O²⁻, S²⁻)
- Group 15 → −3 ions (N³⁻, P³⁻)

#### The "Staircase" and Metalloids
The staircase line on the PT divides metals (left) from nonmetals (right). Elements touching the staircase (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te, Po) are metalloids/semiconductors. Silicon (Si) is the most important semiconductor, used in nearly all computer chips.

#### Review Questions
1. Predict the charge of the ion formed by fluorine. How did you determine this?
2. Why are noble gases the least reactive elements on the periodic table?
3. Where are metalloids located on the periodic table, and what property makes silicon valuable in electronics?

## Subtopic: Chemical Bonding Basics

### Why Atoms Bond: The Octet Rule and Valence Electrons
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** why-atoms-bond-octet-rule
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** valence electrons · octet rule · stability · bonding drive
**Summary:** Understand why atoms form chemical bonds by learning about valence electrons, the octet rule, and how bonding creates more stable electron configurations.

#### Valence Electrons
**Valence electrons** are the electrons in the outermost energy level (outer shell) of an atom. They are responsible for all chemical bonding. The number of valence electrons is determined by group number:
- Group 1 → 1 valence electron
- Group 2 → 2 valence electrons
- Groups 13–18 → 3–8 valence electrons (group number − 10 for groups 13–18, or just use the ones digit for groups 1–2)
- Noble gases (Group 18) → 8 valence electrons (except He, which has 2)

#### The Octet Rule
Most atoms are most stable with **8 valence electrons** in their outer shell (the "octet"). This is called the **octet rule**. Noble gases already have 8 (or 2 for He), which is why they're unreactive. Other atoms bond to gain, lose, or share electrons and achieve a noble gas configuration. Exception: hydrogen needs only 2 (like helium).

#### Why Atoms Bond
Atoms bond because the resulting compound is more stable (lower energy) than the separate atoms. This stability comes from having a filled outer shell. Bonding is essentially atoms "cooperating" to achieve noble-gas electron configurations.

#### Valence Electrons and Reactivity
- 1 valence electron (alkali metals): very reactive — easily lose 1 electron to achieve noble gas config
- 7 valence electrons (halogens): very reactive — easily gain 1 electron to achieve noble gas config
- 8 valence electrons (noble gases): unreactive — already have full outer shell

#### Review Questions
1. What are valence electrons, and how do they relate to chemical bonding?
2. Sodium has 1 valence electron and chlorine has 7. What does each tend to do (gain or lose electrons) to achieve a stable octet?
3. Why are noble gases generally unreactive?

### Ionic and Covalent Bonds
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ionic-vs-covalent-bonds
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** ionic bond · covalent bond · electronegativity difference · examples
**Summary:** Learn the two major types of chemical bonds, what distinguishes them, and examples of each type of compound.

#### Ionic Bonds
An **ionic bond** forms when one atom transfers one or more electrons to another atom. This creates oppositely charged ions that attract each other electrostatically. Ionic bonds typically form between metals and nonmetals.
- Sodium (Na, metal) gives up 1 electron → Na⁺
- Chlorine (Cl, nonmetal) accepts 1 electron → Cl⁻
- Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract → NaCl (sodium chloride, table salt)

Properties of ionic compounds: high melting points, crystalline solids at room temperature, conduct electricity when dissolved in water (electrolytes), soluble in water.

#### Covalent Bonds
A **covalent bond** forms when two atoms **share** electrons. Covalent bonds typically form between nonmetals. The shared electrons count toward both atoms' octets:
- H₂: two hydrogen atoms share 2 electrons (single bond)
- O₂: two oxygen atoms share 4 electrons (double bond)
- N₂: two nitrogen atoms share 6 electrons (triple bond)
- H₂O: oxygen shares one pair each with two hydrogen atoms

Properties of covalent compounds (molecules): often gases or liquids at room temperature, lower melting/boiling points than ionic compounds, don't conduct electricity.

#### How to Predict Bond Type
Use electronegativity difference:
- Large difference (>1.7): ionic bond (metal + nonmetal)
- Small/moderate difference (<1.7): covalent bond (nonmetal + nonmetal)
- Zero difference: nonpolar covalent (same element: H₂, N₂)

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between ionic and covalent bonding in terms of what happens to electrons?
2. Is the bond in NaCl ionic or covalent? What about the bond in H₂O?

### Bonding in Action: Predicting and Modeling
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** bonding-predicting-modeling
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** Lewis dot structures · predicting formulas · ionic compound rules
**Summary:** Apply bonding rules to predict compound formulas and draw simple Lewis dot structures.

#### Predicting Ionic Formulas
When ionic compounds form, the total charge must equal zero. Use group numbers to predict charges, then balance:
- Sodium (Na⁺) + Chlorine (Cl⁻): 1+ and 1− → **NaCl**
- Magnesium (Mg²⁺) + Oxygen (O²⁻): 2+ and 2− → **MgO**
- Aluminum (Al³⁺) + Oxygen (O²⁻): need 2 Al³⁺ and 3 O²⁻ to balance (total charge = 0) → **Al₂O₃**
- Calcium (Ca²⁺) + Chlorine (Cl⁻): need 1 Ca²⁺ and 2 Cl⁻ → **CaCl₂**

#### Lewis Dot Structures
**Lewis dot structures** (electron dot diagrams) show valence electrons as dots around the element symbol. Steps:
1. Determine valence electrons for each atom (group number = valence electrons for main-group elements)
2. Place dots around the symbol (up to 2 per side, max 8 total)
3. When two atoms bond covalently, show shared electrons between them

For NaCl: Na (1 dot) gives its electron to Cl (7 dots → gains 1 → 8 dots → brackets with −1 charge).
For H₂O: O has 6 valence electrons; each H shares 1 pair with O → O has 2 bonding pairs and 2 lone pairs.

3. In calcium chloride (CaCl₂), what are the charges on the calcium and chloride ions?

#### Rapid Classification
Classify each as ionic (I) or covalent (C):
- NaCl → **I** (metal + nonmetal)
- H₂O → **C** (nonmetal + nonmetal)
- MgO → **I**
- CO₂ → **C**
- KBr → **I**
- CH₄ → **C**
- CaCl₂ → **I**
- N₂ → **C** (nonpolar covalent)

#### Key Differences: Ionic vs. Covalent
| Property | Ionic | Covalent |
|---|---|---|
| Electron behavior | Transfer | Share |
| Typical components | Metal + nonmetal | Nonmetal + nonmetal |
| State at room temp | Solid | Gas, liquid, or solid |
| Melting point | High | Usually low |
| Conductivity in water | Yes (electrolyte) | Usually no |
