## Subtopic: States of Matter

### What Is Matter? Solids, Liquids, and Gases
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** what-is-matter-states
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** states of matter · particle arrangement · phase changes
**Summary:** Learn the three main states of matter, how particle arrangement differs in each, and what happens during phase changes.

#### Defining Matter and Its States
Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. Scientists organize matter into three common states: solid, liquid, and gas. In a **solid**, particles are packed tightly together in fixed positions — they vibrate in place but don't move freely. This is why solids have a definite shape and volume. In a **liquid**, particles are close together but can slide past each other, giving liquids a definite volume but no fixed shape (they take the shape of their container). In a **gas**, particles move rapidly and far apart, so gases have neither a definite shape nor a definite volume — they expand to fill any container.

#### How Particles Move in Each State
Temperature determines how fast particles move. At low temperatures, particles move slowly and attractions between them hold them in place → solid. As temperature rises, particles move faster, breaking free to slide → liquid. At higher temperatures still, particles move fast enough to overcome all attractions → gas. The key word is **kinetic energy**: the energy of motion. Higher temperature = more kinetic energy = particles move faster = less structure.

#### Phase Changes and Their Names
When matter changes state, energy is either absorbed or released — but the substance stays the same substance. Phase change names:
- **Melting**: solid → liquid (absorbs energy)
- **Freezing**: liquid → solid (releases energy)
- **Evaporation/Boiling**: liquid → gas (absorbs energy)
- **Condensation**: gas → liquid (releases energy)
- **Sublimation**: solid → gas directly (e.g., dry ice, CO₂)
- **Deposition**: gas → solid directly (e.g., frost forming)

Temperature stays constant during a phase change — all the energy goes into changing state, not raising temperature.

#### Why This Matters for Science Bowl
Phase change questions appear frequently. Key trap: students think temperature rises continuously when heat is added — it actually plateaus during phase changes. Also memorize: dry ice sublimates (solid CO₂ → gas). Water's boiling point is 100°C (212°F) at sea level; melting point is 0°C (32°F).

#### The Fourth State: Plasma
Plasma is sometimes called the fourth state of matter. It forms when a gas is heated so intensely that electrons are stripped from atoms, creating a mix of ions and free electrons. Plasma is the most common state of matter in the universe — stars (including the Sun) are made of plasma. Lightning bolts and neon signs also contain plasma. In Science Bowl, if a question asks "what is the most abundant state of matter in the universe," the answer is plasma.

#### Review Questions
1. What happens to particle motion when a solid melts into a liquid?
2. Name the phase change in which a gas becomes a solid directly, and give one real-world example.
3. Why does temperature remain constant while a substance is changing phase?

### Temperature, Heat, and Thermal Energy
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** temperature-heat-thermal-energy
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** temperature vs. heat · thermal energy · Celsius and Kelvin scales
**Summary:** Understand the difference between temperature and heat, how thermal energy relates to particle motion, and how temperature scales work.

#### Temperature Is Not the Same as Heat
This is one of the most common Science Bowl traps. **Temperature** is a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles in a substance. **Heat** is the transfer of thermal energy from a warmer object to a cooler one. **Thermal energy** is the total kinetic energy of all particles in an object. A bathtub of warm water has more thermal energy than a small cup of boiling water — even though the cup is hotter — because the bathtub has far more particles.

#### Temperature Scales
Three temperature scales matter for Science Bowl:
- **Celsius (°C)**: Water freezes at 0°C, boils at 100°C. Used in science worldwide.
- **Fahrenheit (°F)**: Water freezes at 32°F, boils at 212°F. Common in the U.S.
- **Kelvin (K)**: Absolute scale. 0 K is absolute zero, the coldest possible temperature (−273.15°C). Kelvin = Celsius + 273. There are no negative Kelvin temperatures.

Conversion: K = °C + 273 (for Science Bowl, use 273 as the offset).

#### Heat Transfer Methods
Heat moves in three ways:
- **Conduction**: direct contact (metal spoon in hot soup gets hot)
- **Convection**: through fluid movement (hot air rising, ocean currents)
- **Radiation**: through electromagnetic waves, no medium needed (sunlight warming Earth)

#### Science Bowl Quick Facts
- Absolute zero = 0 K = −273°C — the temperature at which particle motion theoretically stops
- Body temperature ≈ 37°C = 98.6°F = 310 K
- Room temperature ≈ 20–25°C

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between temperature and thermal energy?
2. Convert 100°C to Kelvin.
3. Which method of heat transfer does not require matter (a medium)?

### Plasma: The Fourth State of Matter
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** plasma-fourth-state
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** plasma · ionization · stars and lightning
**Summary:** Discover the fourth state of matter, how plasma forms, and why it's the dominant state of matter in the universe.

#### What Is Plasma?
Plasma forms when a gas is heated to such extreme temperatures that electrons are ripped away from atoms, creating a collection of positively charged ions and free-moving electrons. Because the particles carry electric charge, plasma conducts electricity and responds strongly to magnetic fields. Plasma is NOT simply a hot gas — it behaves fundamentally differently.

#### Where Plasma Exists
Plasma is everywhere — it's actually the most abundant state of matter in the observable universe. Examples:
- **Stars** (including the Sun) — made entirely of plasma
- **Lightning bolts** — a plasma channel through air
- **Neon/fluorescent lights** — gas inside is ionized into plasma when electricity flows through
- **Auroras (Northern/Southern Lights)** — solar wind plasma interacting with Earth's magnetic field
- **Fusion reactors** — superheated plasma for experimental energy production

#### Why Plasma Matters for Competition
Science Bowl frequently asks: "What is the most common state of matter in the universe?" — **Plasma**. Also watch for: "What state of matter is found in the Sun?" — Plasma. Common wrong answers students choose: gas (the Sun looks like it could be a gas, but it's plasma). The distinguishing feature is ionization.

#### Review Questions
1. How does plasma differ from a gas?
2. Name three natural or human-made examples of plasma.
3. Why is plasma the most common state of matter in the universe?

### States of Matter in Everyday Life
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** states-of-matter-everyday-life
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** real-world states · phase changes in cooking · atmospheric water cycle
**Summary:** Apply your knowledge of states of matter to cooking, weather, and everyday household observations.

#### Cooking and Phase Changes
Your kitchen is a phase-change laboratory. When you boil pasta, water (liquid) absorbs heat and becomes steam (gas) — evaporation. When you make ice cubes, liquid water loses heat and freezes (liquid → solid). Butter melts when heated (solid → liquid). When you see steam rising from a pot, the water is evaporating; when that steam hits a cold window and forms water droplets, that's condensation (gas → liquid). If you've ever seen frost on a cold morning, that's deposition — water vapor going directly from gas to solid without becoming liquid first.

#### Weather and the Water Cycle
The entire water cycle is driven by phase changes: evaporation from oceans/lakes (liquid → gas), condensation in clouds (gas → liquid droplets), and precipitation (rain = liquid, snow = solid). Fog is suspended liquid water droplets in air — condensation happening close to ground level. Dew forms by condensation on cool surfaces overnight.

#### Dry Ice Demonstration
Dry ice is solid carbon dioxide (CO₂) that sublimates at −78.5°C. Unlike water ice, it goes directly from solid to gas — no liquid puddle. This is why it's used for shipping perishables and creating fog effects in theater. Carbon dioxide's triple point (where all three states coexist) is at 5.1 atm and −56.6°C — at normal atmospheric pressure, liquid CO₂ cannot exist.

#### Review Questions
1. When steam from a pot hits a cold lid and water droplets form, which phase change is occurring?
2. Explain why dry ice is more useful than regular ice for keeping things cold during shipment.
3. In the water cycle, what phase change occurs when clouds form?

### Comparing the States: A Visual Review
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** comparing-states-visual-review
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** particle diagrams · density differences · compressibility
**Summary:** Use particle-level diagrams and real examples to compare solids, liquids, and gases across multiple properties.

#### Particle Diagrams and What They Show
If you draw a box representing each state:
- **Solid**: particles touching, arranged in neat rows/patterns (crystalline) or random but tight (amorphous). Barely any space between particles.
- **Liquid**: particles close but disordered, touching their neighbors but able to shift. Small spaces.
- **Gas**: particles far apart with large empty spaces between them. A gas at room conditions is mostly empty space.

This explains key differences in properties: solids are nearly incompressible, liquids barely compress, but gases compress easily (think: inflating a tire — you're packing gas particles closer).

#### Density Across States
Generally, solids > liquids > gases in density. Water is a famous exception: ice (solid) is LESS dense than liquid water — that's why ice floats. This is due to water's hydrogen bonding creating an open hexagonal lattice in ice. This exception is critical for Science Bowl.

#### Compressibility and Shape
- Solids: definite shape, definite volume, not compressible
- Liquids: no definite shape, definite volume, barely compressible
- Gases: no definite shape, no definite volume, highly compressible

#### Review Questions
1. Why can you compress a gas but not a liquid or solid?
2. Water is unusual among substances because its solid form is less dense than its liquid form. What causes this?
3. A substance has a definite volume but no definite shape. What state of matter is it?

## Subtopic: Physical vs. Chemical Properties

### Identifying Physical and Chemical Properties
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** physical-vs-chemical-properties
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** physical properties · chemical properties · observable vs. reactive
**Summary:** Learn to distinguish physical properties (what you observe) from chemical properties (how a substance reacts), with concrete examples of each.

#### What Are Physical Properties?
A **physical property** is a characteristic you can observe or measure without changing the chemical identity of the substance. Physical properties include:
- Color, odor, taste
- Mass, volume, density
- Melting point, boiling point, freezing point
- Hardness, malleability (ability to be hammered flat), ductility (ability to be drawn into wire)
- Solubility (ability to dissolve)
- Electrical conductivity
- State of matter (solid/liquid/gas) at room temperature

None of these measurements create a new substance — the substance is still the same chemical after you measure it.

#### What Are Chemical Properties?
A **chemical property** describes how a substance can change into a different substance through a chemical reaction. You can only observe a chemical property by changing the substance. Examples:
- **Flammability**: wood is flammable (burns to produce CO₂ and H₂O)
- **Reactivity with acid**: iron reacts with hydrochloric acid to produce hydrogen gas
- **Oxidation (rusting)**: iron rusts in the presence of oxygen and water
- **Toxicity**: some substances are toxic because of how they react with body chemistry
- **Corrosiveness**: bleach corrodes metals

After a chemical property is demonstrated, you have different substances than you started with.

#### The Key Test Question
Ask: "After I measure this property, do I still have the same substance?" If yes → physical. If no → chemical.
- Cutting wood into smaller pieces: still wood → **physical**
- Burning wood: ash, CO₂, H₂O remain → **chemical**
- Melting gold: still gold (just liquid) → **physical**
- Rusting iron: iron + oxygen → iron oxide (a new compound) → **chemical**

#### Science Bowl Clue Words
Chemical property clue words: burns, reacts, corrodes, oxidizes, decomposes, ferments. Physical property clue words: color, density, melting point, boiling point, solubility, luster, hardness.

#### Review Questions
1. Is melting point a physical or chemical property? Explain why.
2. Is flammability a physical or chemical property? What happens when you observe it?
3. A student measures the density of aluminum foil. Has the aluminum changed chemically?

### Physical vs. Chemical Changes
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** physical-vs-chemical-changes
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** physical change · chemical change · evidence of chemical reaction
**Summary:** Distinguish physical changes (same substance, different form) from chemical changes (new substances formed), and recognize the evidence of each.

#### Physical Changes
A **physical change** alters the appearance, shape, or state of matter but does NOT produce a new substance. The substance can usually be returned to its original form. Examples:
- Cutting, tearing, crushing
- Dissolving salt in water (the salt and water are still there — you can evaporate the water to get salt back)
- Melting ice → water → steam: all still H₂O
- Bending a paperclip

#### Chemical Changes
A **chemical change** produces one or more new substances with different properties. Chemical changes are usually hard or impossible to reverse. Signs that a chemical change has occurred:
- **Color change** (unexpected — not just mixing colors)
- **Gas production** (bubbles not from boiling)
- **Precipitate** (solid forms in a liquid)
- **Temperature change** without adding heat (energy released or absorbed)
- **Light or flame** produced
- **Odor change**

Examples: burning, rusting, cooking an egg, baking bread, photosynthesis, digestion.

#### Tricky Cases
- Dissolving sugar in water: **physical** (the sugar molecules are still present, just spread out)
- Dissolving an antacid tablet: **chemical** (produces CO₂ gas — new substance)
- Cooking an egg: **chemical** (protein structure permanently changes — can't uncook an egg)

#### Review Questions
1. You see bubbles forming when you drop a tablet into water. Is this a physical or chemical change? What evidence supports your answer?
2. Why is dissolving salt in water a physical change but burning wood is a chemical change?
3. List three signs (other than flame) that indicate a chemical change is occurring.

### Properties in Action: Identifying Real Materials
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** properties-real-materials
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** identifying unknowns · using multiple properties · density as identifier
**Summary:** Apply physical and chemical properties to identify unknown substances and explain why scientists use multiple properties together.

#### Why Use Multiple Properties?
No single property uniquely identifies every substance. Water and isopropyl alcohol are both clear liquids — color alone can't distinguish them. But density, boiling point, and odor together quickly separate them. Scientists build a **property profile** to identify unknowns. In forensic science, crime labs use density, melting point, spectroscopy, and chemical reactions to identify unknown substances from crime scenes.

#### Density as an Identifier
Density (mass ÷ volume, in g/cm³ or g/mL) is one of the most useful physical properties for identification:
- Water: 1.00 g/cm³ at 4°C
- Aluminum: 2.70 g/cm³
- Iron: 7.87 g/cm³
- Gold: 19.3 g/cm³ (much denser than most metals — this is how Archimedes allegedly checked the king's crown)

Density is an **intensive property** — it doesn't change with sample size. A small piece of gold and a large bar of gold have the same density. This makes density ideal for identification.

#### Chemical Properties as Identifiers
Testing for a chemical property can confirm identity:
- Add acid to limestone: CO₂ bubbles form → confirms carbonate (CaCO₃)
- Hold unknown gas near flame: explodes with a pop → hydrogen gas
- Add iodine solution to starch: turns dark blue/black → confirms starch

#### Review Questions
1. Why do scientists use multiple properties rather than just one to identify an unknown?
2. A gold-colored metal has a density of 5.1 g/cm³. Could it be real gold? Explain.
3. When you add hydrochloric acid to an unknown white powder and CO₂ gas is produced, what does this tell you about the substance?

#### Classification Practice
Classify each as physical (P) or chemical (C) property:
- Melting point of iron = 1538°C → **P**
- Iron rusts in moist air → **C**
- Gold is yellow and shiny → **P**

- Tearing paper → **P**

Physical properties split further:
- **Intensive**: don't depend on sample size — density, melting point, boiling point, color. These are best for identification.

## Subtopic: Mixtures & Solutions

### Pure Substances vs. Mixtures
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** pure-substances-vs-mixtures
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** elements · compounds · mixtures · composition
**Summary:** Learn the difference between pure substances (elements and compounds) and mixtures (homogeneous and heterogeneous), and how to classify any sample of matter.

#### The Big Picture: Classifying Matter
All matter fits into one of two categories: **pure substances** or **mixtures**.

A **pure substance** has a uniform and definite composition — every sample has exactly the same properties and same ratio of parts. Pure substances come in two types:
- **Elements**: made of only one kind of atom (e.g., gold Au, oxygen O₂, iron Fe, helium He). Cannot be broken down chemically.
- **Compounds**: made of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio (e.g., water H₂O, salt NaCl, carbon dioxide CO₂). Can be broken down chemically into elements.

A **mixture** contains two or more substances physically combined — NOT chemically bonded. The substances keep their individual properties and can be separated by physical means. The ratio of components can vary.

#### Types of Mixtures
- **Homogeneous mixture** (solution): uniform throughout — you can't see individual components. Examples: saltwater, air, lemonade, steel (metal alloy).
- **Heterogeneous mixture**: not uniform — you can see different components. Examples: salad, sand + gravel, granite, Italian dressing (oil and vinegar separate).

#### How to Separate Mixtures
- **Filtration**: separates insoluble solids from liquids (sand from water)
- **Evaporation**: removes liquid to leave dissolved solute (salt from saltwater)
- **Distillation**: separates liquids with different boiling points
- **Magnetism**: separates magnetic materials (iron filings from sand)
- **Chromatography**: separates mixtures by how far components travel through a medium

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between a compound and a mixture?
2. Is air a pure substance or a mixture? What type of mixture?
3. You have a mixture of sand and iron filings. What physical separation technique would you use?

### Solutions: Solutes, Solvents, and Concentration
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** solutions-solutes-solvents-concentration
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** solute · solvent · concentration · solubility
**Summary:** Understand what makes a solution, the roles of solute and solvent, how concentration works, and what factors affect solubility.

#### Solute and Solvent
A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. The **solvent** is the substance present in the largest amount — it does the dissolving. The **solute** is the substance that dissolves into the solvent. In saltwater: water = solvent, salt = solute. In a sugar/water solution: water = solvent, sugar = solute.

Rule of thumb: **"Like dissolves like."** Polar solvents (like water) dissolve polar solutes (like salt, sugar). Non-polar solvents (like gasoline) dissolve non-polar solutes (like grease). This is why oil doesn't dissolve in water.

#### Concentration
**Concentration** describes how much solute is dissolved in a given amount of solution. Key terms:
- **Concentrated**: a lot of solute (strong coffee)
- **Dilute**: a little solute (weak coffee)
- **Saturated**: the maximum amount of solute is dissolved at a given temperature
- **Unsaturated**: more solute could still dissolve
- **Supersaturated**: more solute than normally dissolves (unstable — will crystallize if disturbed)

#### Factors Affecting Solubility
- **Temperature**: for most solid solutes in water, higher temperature → greater solubility. For gases in liquids, higher temperature → LESS solubility (warm soda goes flat faster).
- **Pressure**: affects gas solubility significantly. Higher pressure → more gas dissolves (carbonation in sealed bottles).
- **Stirring/Agitation**: speeds up dissolving (doesn't change solubility, just rate).

#### Review Questions
1. In a solution of sugar water, which substance is the solute and which is the solvent?
2. Why does a carbonated drink go flat faster when it's warm than when it's cold?
3. What does it mean for a solution to be "saturated"?

### Types of Mixtures and Separation Methods
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** mixture-separation-methods
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** filtration · distillation · chromatography · real applications
**Summary:** Apply separation techniques to real-world scenarios, and understand which method works best for each type of mixture.

#### Filtration: Solids Out of Liquids
Filtration uses a porous barrier (filter paper, screen, membrane) to separate an insoluble solid from a liquid. The liquid (filtrate) passes through; the solid (residue) stays behind. Examples:
- Water treatment plants filter solids from water using sand filters and membranes
- Coffee makers filter grounds from liquid coffee
- Air filters in cars and HVAC systems
Limitation: won't separate dissolved solutes — only works for insoluble solids.

#### Distillation: Liquids with Different Boiling Points
Distillation heats a mixture until one component boils, then collects and cools the vapor. Used to:
- Separate water from saltwater (the water evaporates; salt stays behind)
- Refine crude oil into gasoline, diesel, kerosene, etc. (petroleum distillation)
- Produce purified water or alcohol

#### Chromatography: Separating by Attraction
Chromatography separates substances based on how fast they move through a material (stationary phase) when carried by a fluid (mobile phase). Different substances travel different distances. Used in:
- Crime labs (identifying ink or drug samples)
- Food science (identifying food colorings)
- Biology (separating pigments in leaves — reveals chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, carotenoids)

#### Paper Chromatography Example
If you put a drop of black ink on filter paper and let water wick upward, the different dyes in the ink separate into visible bands — black ink is often a mixture of blue, red, and yellow dyes.

#### Review Questions
1. Why can't filtration remove dissolved salt from saltwater?
2. How does distillation separate a mixture of alcohol and water?
3. A forensic scientist needs to identify which pen wrote a forged document. Which separation technique would she likely use?

### Mixtures in Everyday Science: Alloys, Air, and More
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** mixtures-alloys-air-applications
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** alloys · composition of air · real-world solutions
**Summary:** Explore common real-world mixtures — metallic alloys, atmospheric air, and biological solutions — and understand why mixing matters.

#### Metallic Alloys
An **alloy** is a solid homogeneous mixture of two or more metals (or metal + non-metal). Alloys often have superior properties to pure metals:
- **Steel**: iron + carbon → stronger than pure iron; used in construction
- **Stainless steel**: iron + chromium + nickel → rust resistant; used in cutlery, medical tools
- **Bronze**: copper + tin → harder than copper; used historically for weapons and coins
- **Brass**: copper + zinc → gold-colored, used in instruments and plumbing fittings
- **24K gold** = pure gold; **18K gold** = 75% gold alloy (more durable)

#### Composition of Air
Air is a homogeneous gaseous mixture (not a compound). Approximate composition:
- Nitrogen (N₂): ~78%
- Oxygen (O₂): ~21%
- Argon (Ar): ~0.93%
- Carbon dioxide (CO₂): ~0.04%
- Other gases: traces

Common trap: students think air is mostly oxygen — it's mostly **nitrogen**. The oxygen is only about 21%.

#### Biological Solutions
Blood is a heterogeneous mixture (plasma + cells). Ocean water is a solution (water + dissolved salts, gases). Cytoplasm inside cells is a solution of water, salts, and organic molecules. Stomach acid is a dilute solution of hydrochloric acid (HCl).

#### Review Questions
1. What is an alloy? Give two examples with their component metals.
2. What is the most abundant gas in Earth's atmosphere?
3. Is blood a homogeneous or heterogeneous mixture? Explain.

## Subtopic: Density & Buoyancy

### What Is Density? Mass, Volume, and the Formula
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** what-is-density-formula
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** density formula · mass and volume · units
**Summary:** Understand density as mass per unit volume, learn the formula, and practice applying it to real materials.

#### The Density Formula
**Density = Mass ÷ Volume**, or D = m/V.

Units: grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm³) for solids and liquids, or grams per milliliter (g/mL) — these are numerically equivalent (1 cm³ = 1 mL). For gases, density is often given in g/L.

Density is an **intensive property** — it doesn't depend on how much of the substance you have. A tiny pebble of granite and a boulder of granite have the same density (~2.7 g/cm³). This makes density useful for identification.

#### Reference Densities to Know
- Water: 1.00 g/cm³ at 4°C (densest; ice = 0.917 g/cm³, which is why ice floats)
- Air: ~0.0013 g/cm³
- Aluminum: 2.70 g/cm³
- Iron: 7.87 g/cm³
- Lead: 11.3 g/cm³
- Gold: 19.3 g/cm³
- Mercury (liquid metal): 13.6 g/cm³

#### Worked Example
A rock has a mass of 45 g and a volume of 15 cm³. What is its density?
D = m/V = 45 g ÷ 15 cm³ = **3 g/cm³**

Would this rock sink or float in water (density = 1 g/cm³)? Since 3 > 1, the rock is denser than water → it **sinks**.

#### Review Questions
1. A liquid has a mass of 60 g and a volume of 50 mL. Calculate its density.
2. An object has a density of 0.8 g/cm³. Will it sink or float in water? Explain.
3. If you cut a brick in half, what happens to its density?

### Buoyancy: Why Things Float or Sink
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** buoyancy-why-things-float-sink
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** buoyant force · Archimedes' principle · displaced fluid
**Summary:** Understand the buoyant force, Archimedes' principle, and the rule that determines whether an object floats or sinks.

#### The Buoyant Force
**Buoyancy** is the upward force that a fluid (liquid or gas) exerts on an object. Fluids push up on objects submerged in them. When you lift a rock underwater, it feels lighter — the water is exerting an upward buoyant force. This force exists because pressure in a fluid increases with depth, so the pressure on the bottom of an object is greater than on the top → net upward force.

#### Archimedes' Principle
**Archimedes' principle** states: the buoyant force on an object equals the weight of the fluid that the object displaces.

If you submerge a 500 cm³ object in water:
- It displaces 500 cm³ of water
- 500 cm³ of water weighs 500 g (since water density = 1 g/cm³)
- Buoyant force = weight of 500 g of water = 4.9 N upward

#### Float or Sink Rule
- Object density < fluid density → object floats (buoyant force ≥ weight)
- Object density > fluid density → object sinks (buoyant force < weight)
- Object density = fluid density → object is neutrally buoyant (hovers)

A steel ship floats because its average density (including all the air inside) is less than water. If you crush the ship into a solid ball of steel, it sinks — average density now exceeds water.

3. Why does a steel ship float even though steel is denser than water?

### Density and Buoyancy in Real Life
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** density-buoyancy-real-life
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** submarines · hot air balloons · ocean layers
**Summary:** Apply density and buoyancy principles to submarines, hot air balloons, ocean stratification, and the human body.

#### Submarines: Controlling Density
Submarines use **ballast tanks** to control their average density. To dive: the tanks are flooded with seawater, increasing average density → sub sinks. To surface: compressed air is blown into the tanks, pushing water out → average density decreases → sub rises. The submarine's density is adjusted to match, exceed, or be less than seawater density as needed. This is a direct application of the float/sink rule.

#### Hot Air Balloons: Buoyancy in Air
Air has density. Hot air is LESS dense than cool air (same pressure, fewer molecules per unit volume when hot). A hot air balloon heats the air inside the envelope, making it less dense than the surrounding cooler atmosphere. The buoyant force from the denser surrounding air lifts the balloon. Same principle as an object floating in water — the balloon's average density (hot air + fabric + basket) must be less than surrounding air density.

#### Ocean Density Layers
Seawater density varies with temperature and salinity. Cold, salty water is denser and sinks; warm, fresh water is less dense and stays near the surface. This creates **density stratification** — layers at different depths. Deep ocean currents (thermohaline circulation) are driven by these density differences. The Dead Sea is so salty (salinity ~33%) that its density is ~1.24 g/cm³, making people float easily.

#### Review Questions
1. How do ballast tanks allow a submarine to dive and surface?
2. Why does hot air rise inside a hot air balloon?
3. The Dead Sea has a salinity much higher than regular seawater. How does this affect whether people float or sink in it?

### Density and Buoyancy: Complete Review
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** density-buoyancy-review
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** density calculations · buoyancy rules · Archimedes
**Summary:** Full synthesis review of density calculations, Archimedes' principle, and floating/sinking rules.

#### Key Formulas
- Density: D = m/V
- Buoyant force = weight of displaced fluid = ρ_fluid × V_displaced × g

#### Float/Sink Decision Tree
Object in fluid → compare densities:
- D_object < D_fluid → floats (partially submerged)
- D_object > D_fluid → sinks
- D_object = D_fluid → neutrally buoyant

#### Practice Problems
1. Mass = 120 g, Volume = 40 cm³ → D = 120/40 = **3 g/cm³** (sinks in water)
2. Mass = 25 g, Volume = 50 cm³ → D = 25/50 = **0.5 g/cm³** (floats in water)
3. A 200 cm³ object submerged in water displaces 200 cm³ of water. Buoyant force = weight of 200 g water = ~2 N upward.

#### Archimedes Summary
Archimedes (~287–212 BCE) discovered his principle when tasked with checking whether a king's crown was pure gold. He noticed that he displaced water when getting into a bath — the volume of water displaced equaled the volume of the submerged object. The story goes he ran through the streets shouting "Eureka!" ("I have found it!").
