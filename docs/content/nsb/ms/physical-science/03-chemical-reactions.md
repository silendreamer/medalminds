## Subtopic: Reactants, Products & Balancing

### What Is a Chemical Reaction?
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** what-is-a-chemical-reaction
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** reactants · products · chemical equation · reaction types
**Summary:** Learn the definition of a chemical reaction, how to read a chemical equation, and the main types of reactions.

#### Defining a Chemical Reaction
A **chemical reaction** is a process in which the atoms of one or more substances (reactants) are rearranged to form new substances (products). The key is that **new substances with different properties are formed** — this distinguishes a chemical reaction from a physical change. In every chemical reaction, atoms are neither created nor destroyed; they are rearranged.

#### Reading a Chemical Equation
A **chemical equation** uses symbols and formulas to represent a reaction. The general format is:
**Reactants → Products**

For example: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
- Reactants (left of arrow): methane (CH₄) and oxygen (O₂)
- Products (right of arrow): carbon dioxide (CO₂) and water (H₂O)
- Coefficients (numbers in front): show how many molecules of each substance are involved (the "2" in front of O₂ means 2 molecules of O₂)
- Arrow means "reacts to form" or "yields"

#### Types of Chemical Reactions
Five main types covered in Science Bowl:
1. **Synthesis (combination)**: A + B → AB. Two reactants combine to form one product.
2. **Decomposition**: AB → A + B. One reactant breaks down into two or more products.
3. **Single replacement**: A + BC → AC + B. One element replaces another in a compound.
4. **Double replacement**: AB + CD → AD + CB. Ions switch partners.
5. **Combustion**: fuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (+energy). Burning in oxygen.

#### Review Questions
1. How do you identify reactants and products in a chemical equation?
2. What does a coefficient in a chemical equation represent?
3. Name the five types of chemical reactions and give a brief description of each.

### Balancing Chemical Equations
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** balancing-chemical-equations
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** law of conservation of mass · balancing · coefficients
**Summary:** Master the process of balancing chemical equations using coefficients to satisfy the law of conservation of mass.

#### Why Equations Must Be Balanced
The **Law of Conservation of Mass** (Lavoisier's Law) states that mass cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. The total mass of reactants must equal the total mass of products. In terms of atoms: the number of each type of atom on the left must equal the number on the right. An **unbalanced** equation violates this law.

#### The Balancing Rules
1. Write the unbalanced equation with correct formulas.
2. Count atoms of each element on each side.
3. Add **coefficients** (whole numbers) in front of formulas to make atom counts equal.
4. **NEVER** change the subscripts within a formula (that would change the substance itself).
5. If a coefficient is 1, you don't write it (it's implied).

#### Balancing Step-by-Step
Balance: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O
- Left: 2H, 2O | Right: 2H, 1O → Oxygen not balanced
- Add coefficient 2 in front of H₂O: H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
- Now: Left 2H, 2O | Right 4H, 2O → Hydrogen not balanced
- Add coefficient 2 in front of H₂: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
- Check: Left 4H, 2O | Right 4H, 2O ✓ Balanced!

Balance: Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃
- Start with Fe₂O₃: left 1Fe, 2O | right 2Fe, 3O
- Balance Fe: 2Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃ | left 2Fe, 2O | right 2Fe, 3O
- Balance O: 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ | left 4Fe, 6O | right 4Fe, 6O ✓

#### Review Questions
1. Why must chemical equations be balanced?
2. Balance the equation: __ N₂ + __ H₂ → __ NH₃
3. Why can't you balance an equation by changing the subscripts in a formula?

### Types of Chemical Reactions in Detail
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** types-chemical-reactions-detail
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** synthesis · decomposition · single/double replacement · combustion
**Summary:** Deep dive into each type of chemical reaction with examples, patterns, and how to recognize each type.

#### Synthesis (Combination)
A + B → AB — two or more reactants form a single product.
Examples:
- 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl (sodium + chlorine → table salt)
- 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (water formation)
- CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (lime + water → calcium hydroxide)
Clue: one product.

#### Decomposition
AB → A + B — one reactant breaks into two or more products. Often requires energy (heat, light, electricity).
Examples:
- 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ (electrolysis of water)
- 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂ (mercury oxide decomposed by heat — historically important in discovering oxygen)
- 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ (hydrogen peroxide decomposing)
Clue: one reactant, multiple products.

#### Single Replacement
A + BC → AC + B — a more reactive element displaces a less reactive one.
Examples:
- Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂ (zinc replaces hydrogen in acid)
- Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu (iron displaces copper)
Reactivity series determines if replacement occurs.

#### Double Replacement
AB + CD → AD + CB — ions switch partners, often forming a precipitate, water, or gas.
Examples:
- AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl (precipitate) + NaNO₃
- HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O (acid-base neutralization)

#### Combustion
Fuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (+energy). The fuel contains carbon and/or hydrogen.
Examples:
- CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (natural gas burning)
- C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O (propane burning)
Clue: O₂ as a reactant; CO₂ and H₂O as products.

#### Review Questions
1. In the reaction 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂, what type of reaction is this?
2. When magnesium burns in oxygen to form magnesium oxide (MgO), what type of reaction is this?
3. Combustion reactions always produce what two products (assuming complete combustion of a hydrocarbon)?

## Subtopic: Conservation of Mass

### Law of Conservation of Mass: The Foundation
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** law-of-conservation-of-mass
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** Lavoisier · conservation · mass before and after · closed system
**Summary:** Understand the Law of Conservation of Mass, its historical discovery, and why it's the bedrock principle of all chemistry.

#### Antoine Lavoisier and the Discovery
Antoine Lavoisier (1743–1794), often called the "father of modern chemistry," established the **Law of Conservation of Mass** through careful experiments. He worked in **closed systems** — sealed containers where no material could enter or leave — and measured mass before and after reactions. He consistently found: mass before = mass after. This was revolutionary at a time when chemists thought burning substances actually gained mass from "phlogiston."

#### The Law in Simple Terms
**Mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.** The total mass of all reactants equals the total mass of all products. Atoms are simply rearranged — none are created or destroyed. Because atoms are rearranged but not lost:
- Number of each type of atom must be the same before and after
- This is WHY we balance chemical equations

#### Closed vs. Open Systems
The law applies in **closed systems** (no material enters or leaves). In an open system (like burning a log in the open air), the mass of ashes appears less than the log — but that's because CO₂ and H₂O escape as gases. In a sealed container, the total mass remains constant.

#### Calculating Mass of Products
If reactant masses are known: mass of products = mass of reactants.
Example: 4 g of hydrogen + 32 g of oxygen → ? g of water
Answer: 4 + 32 = **36 g of water** (all mass goes into product).

#### Review Questions
1. State the Law of Conservation of Mass.
2. A student burns a log that had a mass of 2.0 kg. Only 0.1 kg of ash remains. Did the experiment violate the Law of Conservation of Mass? Explain.
3. Who is credited with establishing the Law of Conservation of Mass, and how was it discovered?

### Conservation of Mass in Action
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** conservation-of-mass-in-action
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** mass calculations · stoichiometry intro · closed vs. open systems
**Summary:** Apply conservation of mass to calculate unknown masses in reactions and explain surprising observations.

#### Mass Calculations Using Conservation of Mass
If you know the mass of all reactants and all but one product, you can calculate the unknown:
- 28 g of iron (Fe) + 8 g of sulfur (S) → ? g of iron sulfide (FeS)
- Total reactant mass = 28 + 8 = 36 g → 36 g FeS forms (conservation of mass)

Reverse: If 90 g of water is electrolyzed into hydrogen and oxygen, and 80 g of oxygen is collected, how much hydrogen was produced?
- 90 g total products; oxygen = 80 g → hydrogen = 90 − 80 = **10 g**

#### Seeming Violations and Their Explanations
- **Burning a candle**: Candle appears to lose mass → wax converts to CO₂ and H₂O vapors that escape into the air. Total mass is conserved in a closed system.
- **Rusting iron**: Mass of rusty iron is GREATER than pure iron → iron absorbed oxygen from air. Conservation holds; the iron gained mass from O₂.
- **Fizzing tablet in water**: Mass of tablet + water = mass of solution + CO₂ gas escaping. Open system; CO₂ leaves, so remaining solution appears lighter.

#### Industrial Application
Chemical plants use conservation of mass (and stoichiometry) to calculate exactly how much raw material they need to produce a certain amount of product — minimizing waste and cost.

#### Review Questions
1. A reaction produces 44 g of CO₂ and 18 g of H₂O. If one of the reactants was oxygen (32 g), what was the mass of the other reactant?
2. A student observes that a piece of iron gains mass after sitting outside for several weeks. Explain this using the Law of Conservation of Mass.
3. If 100 g of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) decomposes completely, what is the total mass of water and oxygen gas produced?

**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** conservation-mass-review
**Estimated time:** 9 min

**Summary:** Quick synthesis of conservation of mass concepts, calculations, and common misconceptions.

#### Key Fact Recap
- Law of Conservation of Mass: mass of reactants = mass of products (in a closed system)
- Atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed
- Named for: Antoine Lavoisier (18th century France)
- This law is why we balance equations

#### Calculation Quick-Check
1. Reactants: 12 g carbon + 32 g oxygen → Products: ? g CO₂ → **44 g**
2. Reactants: 46 g Na + 71 g Cl₂ → Products: ? g NaCl → **117 g**

- "Burning destroys mass" → False: gases escape in open system but total mass conserved

## Subtopic: Acids & Bases

### Introduction to Acids and Bases
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** introduction-to-acids-bases
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** acid · base · pH scale · indicators
**Summary:** Learn what acids and bases are, how they're defined, and how the pH scale measures acidity and basicity.

#### Arrhenius Definition
The simplest definition (Arrhenius):
- **Acid**: a substance that produces H⁺ ions (or H₃O⁺) when dissolved in water
- **Base**: a substance that produces OH⁻ ions when dissolved in water

Examples of acids: HCl (hydrochloric acid, stomach acid), H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid), HNO₃ (nitric acid), CH₃COOH (acetic acid, vinegar)
Examples of bases: NaOH (sodium hydroxide, lye), KOH (potassium hydroxide), Ca(OH)₂ (calcium hydroxide, lime), NH₃ (ammonia)

#### The pH Scale
The **pH scale** measures how acidic or basic a solution is:
- pH 0–6: acidic (lower pH = stronger acid)
- pH 7: neutral (pure water at 25°C)
- pH 8–14: basic/alkaline (higher pH = stronger base)

Key values to memorize:
- Stomach acid: pH 1–2 (very acidic)
- Lemon juice: pH ~2
- Vinegar: pH ~3
- Coffee: pH ~5
- Pure water: pH 7 (neutral)
- Baking soda solution: pH ~9
- Ammonia: pH ~11
- Bleach: pH ~13
- Drain cleaner (NaOH): pH ~14 (very basic)

Each pH unit represents a 10x change in H⁺ concentration. pH 3 is 10× more acidic than pH 4.

#### Indicators
An **indicator** is a substance that changes color based on pH:
- **Litmus**: red in acid, blue in base
- **Phenolphthalein**: colorless in acid, pink in base
- **Universal indicator/pH paper**: shows a range of colors across the pH scale

#### Properties of Acids and Bases
- Acids: sour taste, conduct electricity in water, react with metals to produce H₂ gas, react with carbonates to produce CO₂
- Bases: bitter taste, slippery feel, conduct electricity in water, react with fats/oils

#### Review Questions
1. According to the Arrhenius definition, what ion does an acid produce in water?
2. What is the pH of pure water at 25°C? Is it acidic, basic, or neutral?
3. A student adds litmus to a solution and it turns red. What does this indicate about the solution?

### Neutralization and Salt Formation
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** neutralization-salt-formation
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** neutralization · salt · acid + base → salt + water
**Summary:** Understand how acids and bases react together in neutralization reactions to form salts and water, and explore everyday and industrial applications.

#### Neutralization Reaction
When an acid and a base react together, they produce a **salt** and **water**. This is called a **neutralization reaction**:
Acid + Base → Salt + Water

Examples:
- HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O (hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → table salt + water)
- H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
- HNO₃ + NaOH → NaNO₃ + H₂O

The resulting solution's pH depends on the strengths of the acid and base used. Strong acid + strong base → neutral solution (pH 7).

#### What Is a Salt?
In chemistry, a **salt** is an ionic compound formed when the H⁺ from an acid is replaced by a metal ion. Table salt (NaCl) is the most familiar, but salts include many compounds: KNO₃ (potassium nitrate), CaSO₄ (calcium sulfate), MgCl₂ (magnesium chloride). Note: in chemistry, "salt" is NOT just NaCl — it's a whole class of compounds.

#### Real-World Applications of Neutralization
- **Antacids**: neutralize excess stomach acid (HCl). Antacids like Tums (CaCO₃), Maalox (Mg(OH)₂), or Rolaids contain bases that react with HCl.
- **Treating acid rain**: lime (CaO or Ca(OH)₂) is spread on acid-rain-damaged lakes to neutralize pH.
- **Agriculture**: farmers add lime to acidic soils to raise pH for crops.
- **Wastewater treatment**: industrial acidic or basic waste is neutralized before release.

#### Review Questions
1. Write the general formula for a neutralization reaction.
2. What two products are always formed when a strong acid reacts with a strong base?
3. Why do antacids relieve heartburn? What type of chemical reaction occurs?

### Acids and Bases in Everyday Life
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** acids-bases-everyday-life
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** pH of common substances · acids and metals · buffer concept
**Summary:** Connect acid-base chemistry to food, medicine, the environment, and laboratory applications.

#### Acids in Food
Many foods are acidic:
- Citrus fruits (oranges, lemons): contain citric acid (pH ~2–3)
- Vinegar: acetic acid (~5% solution, pH ~2.4)
- Soda: carbonic acid + phosphoric acid (pH ~2.5–3.5)
- Milk: slightly acidic (pH ~6.5)
- Yogurt/cheese: lactic acid from bacterial fermentation (pH ~4)

Fermentation makes food sour — bacteria produce acids (lactic acid in yogurt, acetic acid in vinegar).

#### Acids and Metals
Active metals (Zn, Fe, Mg, Al) react with acids to produce hydrogen gas and a salt:
Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂↑

You can test for hydrogen gas: burning splint test — a popping sound ("squeaky pop") confirms H₂.

#### Environmental Acids and Bases
- **Acid rain**: SO₂ and NO₂ from burning fossil fuels dissolve in water to form sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) and nitric acid (HNO₃). pH of acid rain < 5.6 (normal rain pH ~5.6 from CO₂).
- **Ocean acidification**: ocean absorbs CO₂ → forms carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) → lowers ocean pH, threatening coral reefs (calcium carbonate dissolves in acid).
- **Baking**: baking soda (NaHCO₃) is a base; baking powder contains baking soda + an acid that reacts when wet to produce CO₂ bubbles → bread rises.

#### Review Questions
1. Magnesium reacts with hydrochloric acid. What two products are formed?
2. How does acid rain form, and what pH does it have compared to normal rain?
3. Why does bread rise when baking soda is used? What type of reaction is involved?

## Subtopic: Exothermic & Endothermic Reactions

### Energy in Chemical Reactions
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** energy-in-chemical-reactions
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** exothermic · endothermic · activation energy · energy diagrams
**Summary:** Understand why chemical reactions either release or absorb energy, the concept of activation energy, and how to read energy diagrams.

#### Why Reactions Involve Energy
Chemical bonds store energy. When bonds break, energy is absorbed; when bonds form, energy is released. The energy difference between bonds broken (reactants) and bonds formed (products) determines whether a reaction releases or absorbs energy overall:
- If energy released from forming new bonds > energy absorbed breaking old bonds → **exothermic** (releases energy to surroundings)
- If energy absorbed breaking old bonds > energy released forming new bonds → **endothermic** (absorbs energy from surroundings)

#### Exothermic Reactions
**Exothermic**: energy is released to the surroundings (usually as heat and/or light). The products have lower energy than the reactants. Surroundings get warmer.
Examples:
- Combustion (burning): CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + energy
- Rusting of iron (slow)
- Hand warmers (iron oxidation)
- Explosions
- Cellular respiration
- Condensation (gas → liquid)

#### Endothermic Reactions
**Endothermic**: energy is absorbed from the surroundings. The products have higher energy than the reactants. Surroundings get cooler.
Examples:
- Photosynthesis (absorbs light)
- Cooking an egg (absorbs heat)
- Dissolving ammonium nitrate in water (cold packs)
- Evaporation (liquid → gas)
- Electrolysis of water

#### Activation Energy
**Activation energy** is the minimum energy needed to start a reaction — even exothermic reactions need a "push" to get started. Example: wood doesn't spontaneously burst into flame — you need a spark to provide activation energy.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between an exothermic and endothermic reaction?
2. Hand warmers release heat when you open them and expose them to air. Are they exothermic or endothermic?
3. What is activation energy, and why is it needed even for exothermic reactions?

### Catalysts and Reaction Rates
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** catalysts-and-reaction-rates
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** catalyst · reaction rate · factors affecting rate · enzyme
**Summary:** Learn what affects how fast a reaction occurs, how catalysts speed up reactions without being consumed, and the role of biological catalysts (enzymes).

#### Factors That Affect Reaction Rate
The rate of a chemical reaction depends on how often and how effectively reactant particles collide:
- **Temperature**: higher temperature → faster particles → more frequent, harder collisions → faster rate
- **Concentration**: more particles → more collisions → faster rate
- **Surface area**: smaller particle size → more surface exposed → faster rate (powder vs. chunks)
- **Pressure** (for gases): higher pressure → particles closer → more collisions → faster rate
- **Catalyst**: substance that speeds up a reaction without being consumed

#### Catalysts
A **catalyst** speeds up a chemical reaction by lowering the **activation energy** — it provides an alternate pathway with a lower energy barrier. The catalyst is not consumed in the reaction (it can be used repeatedly). Key point: a catalyst changes the RATE, not the final equilibrium or products.

Examples:
- Platinum/palladium in car catalytic converters: convert toxic CO and NOₓ to CO₂ and N₂
- MnO₂ catalyzes the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂
- Iron catalyst in the Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

#### Enzymes: Biological Catalysts
**Enzymes** are protein molecules that act as catalysts in living organisms. They lower activation energy to allow biochemical reactions to occur at body temperature (37°C). Most enzymes are specific — they only catalyze one or a few reactions (lock-and-key model). Amylase in saliva catalyzes starch breakdown; pepsin in the stomach catalyzes protein digestion.

**Inhibitors** slow reactions (opposite of catalysts) — some drugs work as enzyme inhibitors.

#### Review Questions
1. List four factors that can increase the rate of a chemical reaction.
2. How does a catalyst speed up a reaction? Is the catalyst consumed in the process?
3. What is an enzyme, and how does it relate to the concept of a catalyst?

### Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions in Real Life
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** exothermic-endothermic-real-life
**Estimated time:** 13 min

**Summary:** Explore how exothermic and endothermic reactions power technology, biology, and everyday products.

#### Cold Packs and Hot Packs
- **Cold packs** (instant ice): contain ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃) and water in separate compartments. Cracking the pack mixes them: NH₄NO₃ dissolves endothermically — absorbs heat from the surroundings → pack feels cold. Used for sports injuries.
- **Hot packs**: contain iron powder, salt, activated carbon, and water. Iron oxidizes (rusts) exothermically when exposed to air — releases heat → pack warms up.
- Chemical hand warmers: same iron oxidation principle; can stay warm for hours.

#### Combustion Engines
Internal combustion engines (cars) rely on the exothermic combustion of gasoline to produce heat → kinetic energy. The rapid expansion of hot gases pushes pistons down, converting chemical potential energy → thermal energy → mechanical energy → motion.

#### Photosynthesis: The Sun Powers Life
Photosynthesis is the most important endothermic reaction on Earth: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. Plants absorb light energy and store it as chemical energy in glucose — making photosynthesis the primary entry point of all energy into the biosphere. Essentially every food calorie you eat originated as sunlight captured by photosynthesis.

#### Respiration: Reverse of Photosynthesis
Cellular respiration (C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP energy) is exothermic — it releases the energy stored by photosynthesis. This energy drives all cellular functions. It's the same as combustion energetically, just slower and controlled by enzymes.

### Exothermic & Endothermic: Synthesis Review
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** exothermic-endothermic-review
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** classification · catalysts · energy diagrams · applications
**Summary:** Rapid review of exothermic/endothermic reactions, catalysts, and energy concepts.

#### Classify Each Reaction
- Burning wood → **Exothermic** (releases heat and light)
- Photosynthesis → **Endothermic** (absorbs light energy)
- Cold pack (NH₄NO₃ dissolving) → **Endothermic**
- Hand warmer (iron rusting) → **Exothermic**
- Cooking an egg → **Endothermic**
- Explosion → **Exothermic**
- Electrolysis of water → **Endothermic**
- Cellular respiration → **Exothermic**

#### Catalyst vs. Inhibitor
- Catalyst: lowers activation energy → speeds reaction; not consumed
- Inhibitor: raises effective activation energy → slows reaction
- Enzyme: biological protein catalyst
- Examples: MnO₂ (H₂O₂ decomposition); platinum (catalytic converter)

#### Energy Diagram Key Points
- Exothermic: products at LOWER energy than reactants; energy released = "negative enthalpy change" (ΔH < 0)
- Endothermic: products at HIGHER energy than reactants; energy absorbed = "positive enthalpy change" (ΔH > 0)
- Activation energy = height of energy barrier above reactants (both types have activation energy)

**Pattern 3:**
*"For 10 points, what term describes a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction by lowering its activation energy, without being consumed in the reaction?"*
Answer: **Catalyst**. Buzz on "speeds up" + "not consumed."

**Pattern 4:**
*"An instant cold pack becomes cold when you crack it. What type of reaction is occurring?"*
