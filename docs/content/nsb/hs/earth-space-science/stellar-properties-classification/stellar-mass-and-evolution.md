### Introduction to Stellar Mass

Stellar mass is a fundamental property that significantly influences a star's formation, evolution, and ultimate fate. It is typically measured in solar masses (M☉), where one solar mass is equivalent to the mass of our Sun. The mass of a star determines its temperature, luminosity, and lifespan, leading to a diverse range of stellar phenomena. Understanding stellar mass is crucial for comprehending the life cycles of stars and their eventual end states.

### The Hertzsprung-Russell Diagram

The Hertzsprung-Russell (H-R) diagram is a pivotal tool in astrophysics that plots stars according to their luminosity and temperature. Stars in the H-R diagram are categorized into distinct groups: main sequence stars, giants, supergiants, and white dwarfs. The position of a star on this diagram is closely related to its mass; for instance, more massive stars are found on the upper left, where they exhibit high luminosity and temperature. This classification helps astronomers predict the life stages of stars based on their initial mass.

### Life Cycle of Low-Mass Stars

Low-mass stars, such as those with masses less than about 2 M☉, undergo a relatively gentle life cycle. They spend the majority of their lives in the main sequence phase, fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores. As they exhaust their hydrogen fuel, they expand into red giants and eventually shed their outer layers, leaving behind a hot core that becomes a white dwarf. This white dwarf will gradually cool and fade over billions of years, ultimately becoming a cold, dark remnant known as a black dwarf.

### Life Cycle of High-Mass Stars

In contrast, high-mass stars (greater than 8 M☉) experience a much more dramatic life cycle. After exhausting hydrogen in their cores, they undergo successive fusion processes, creating heavier elements until iron is formed. Once iron accumulates, fusion ceases, leading to a catastrophic collapse of the core. This collapse triggers a supernova explosion, dispersing the star's outer layers and enriching the interstellar medium with heavy elements. The remnant core may become a neutron star or collapse further into a black hole, depending on its mass.

### Supernovae and Their Importance

Supernovae are among the most energetic events in the universe and play a crucial role in cosmic evolution. They not only mark the death of high-mass stars but also contribute to the chemical enrichment of galaxies. The explosion disperses elements like carbon, oxygen, and iron into space, which are essential for the formation of new stars and planets. Additionally, supernovae can trigger the formation of new stellar nurseries by compressing surrounding gas and dust, thus continuing the cycle of stellar evolution.

### Stellar Classification Systems

Stars are classified based on their spectral characteristics, which are influenced by their temperature and composition. The most common classification system is the Morgan-Keenan (MK) system, which categorizes stars into spectral types O, B, A, F, G, K, and M, with O being the hottest and M the coolest. Each spectral type is further divided into subclasses (e.g., G2, K5) based on temperature variations. This classification not only aids in identifying stars but also provides insights into their evolutionary stages and physical properties.
