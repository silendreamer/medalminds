#### The Hadean and Archean Atmosphere (4.6–2.5 Ga)
Earth's primordial atmosphere was lost early, blown away by solar wind before the planet had a strong magnetic field. The **secondary atmosphere** was built by volcanic outgassing: primarily N₂, CO₂, H₂O, H₂S, and SO₂ — virtually no free O₂. This is a **reducing atmosphere**, chemically very different from today's oxidizing one. There was likely abundant methane (from early microbial life) and the sky may have had an orange haze. No ozone shield existed, meaning ultraviolet radiation was intense at the surface — early life likely stayed underwater for protection.

#### The Great Oxidation Event (~2.4 Ga)
The most dramatic atmospheric transition in Earth history is the **Great Oxidation Event (GOE)** ~2.4 billion years ago, when free O₂ first accumulated in the atmosphere. The trigger: oxygenic photosynthesis, evolved by cyanobacteria, which split water molecules to release O₂ as a byproduct. For ~200 million years before the GOE, O₂ was produced but immediately consumed by oxidizing dissolved iron (Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺), creating **banded iron formations (BIFs)** — alternating layers of iron oxide (rust) and chert in the rock record. Once the ocean's iron was exhausted, O₂ began accumulating in the atmosphere. The GOE triggered a global glaciation ("Snowball Earth") as methane — a potent greenhouse gas — was oxidized by the new O₂.

#### The Ozone Shield and the Colonization of Land
As O₂ levels rose, UV radiation in the upper atmosphere converted O₂ to O₃ (ozone), building the **stratospheric ozone shield**. By ~500 Ma (Ordovician), O₂ had reached perhaps 10% of current levels and the ozone shield was substantial enough to allow life to colonize land. Today's atmosphere is ~78% N₂, 21% O₂, 0.93% Ar, 0.04% CO₂ (420 ppm and rising), with trace amounts of other gases.

#### Ocean Evolution: From Anoxic Iron-Rich to Oxygenated
The early ocean was **anoxic** (no dissolved O₂) and **ferruginous** (rich in dissolved Fe²⁺). The GOE and subsequent biological productivity oxygenated the ocean surface over hundreds of millions of years. Deep ocean oxygenation lagged the surface by perhaps 1–2 billion years. Modern oceans are well-oxygenated in the mixed layer but can develop oxygen minimum zones in poorly ventilated intermediate waters.
