#### The Logic of Biostratigraphy
**Biostratigraphy** uses the vertical distribution of fossils in rock sequences to establish relative ages and correlate strata. The key insight: species appear at a specific time (first appearance datum, FAD) and go extinct at a specific time (last appearance datum, LAD). If two rock layers in different locations both contain the same species with the same FAD and LAD, they were deposited during overlapping time intervals.

#### What Makes a Good Index Fossil?
An ideal index fossil has: (1) **Short stratigraphic range** — the species existed for a brief interval, giving high temporal precision; (2) **Wide geographic distribution** — found in many locations so correlation works across distances; (3) **Abundant and easily recognized** — common enough to find, distinctive enough to identify. Ammonites satisfy all three criteria: they evolved rapidly, produced distinctive shell morphologies, floated on ocean currents after death (achieving wide distribution), and are abundant in Mesozoic marine sediments.

#### Zone Fossils and Biozones
A **biozone** (or zone) is a rock unit defined by the presence of a particular fossil taxon or assemblage. The **concurrent range zone** is defined by the overlap in ranges of two or more taxa. Petroleum geologists rely heavily on microfossil biostratigraphy — particularly foraminifera and nannofossils — to date subsurface rock cores without bringing them to the surface for radiometric dating.

2. If ammonite species X is found in rocks from Europe, North America, and South America, what does this suggest about Mesozoic ocean connectivity?
