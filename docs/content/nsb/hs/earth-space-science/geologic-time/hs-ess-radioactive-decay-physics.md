#### The Decay Process
Radioactive isotopes (parent isotopes) spontaneously decay to stable isotopes (daughter isotopes) by emitting particles (alpha, beta) or energy (gamma). The decay rate is characterized by the **half-life (t½)**: the time required for half of any given quantity of the parent isotope to decay. After one half-life, 50% parent remains; after two, 25%; after three, 12.5%; after n half-lives, (½)ⁿ of the original parent remains.

#### The Decay Equation
N(t) = N₀ × e^(−λt), where N(t) is the number of parent atoms at time t, N₀ is the original number of parent atoms, and λ is the decay constant (λ = ln2/t½ = 0.693/t½). In practice, geochronologists measure the **parent/daughter ratio** in a mineral — the more daughter relative to parent, the older the sample.

#### Closed System Requirement
Radiometric dating requires that the mineral acted as a **closed system** — no parent or daughter atoms entered or left after crystallization. A rock that has been metamorphosed, weathered, or hydrothermally altered may have lost Ar gas or gained/lost Sr, leading to incorrect ages. This is why geologists prefer to date fresh, unaltered minerals and check using multiple isotopic systems.

#### Key Isotopic Systems
| System | Parent → Daughter | Half-life | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| U-Pb | U-238 → Pb-206 | 4.47 Ga | Zircons, old rocks |
| U-Pb | U-235 → Pb-207 | 704 Ma | Zircons, cross-check |
| K-Ar | K-40 → Ar-40 | 1.25 Ga | Volcanic rocks, micas |
| Rb-Sr | Rb-87 → Sr-87 | 48.8 Ga | Old rocks, isochrons |
| C-14 | C-14 → N-14 | 5,730 years | Organic material, <50 ka |
