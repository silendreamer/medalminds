#### What Is Bowen's Reaction Series?
**Bowen's reaction series**, developed by N. L. Bowen in the early 20th century, describes the sequence in which silicate minerals crystallize from a cooling magma. As a magma loses heat, minerals form in a predictable order determined by their melting points. High-temperature minerals crystallize first; lower-temperature minerals form later as the melt becomes progressively enriched in silica, water, and alkali elements.

#### Discontinuous and Continuous Branches
The series has two parallel branches that converge at potassium feldspar and muscovite:

- **Discontinuous branch (mafic minerals):** Olivine → Pyroxene → Amphibole → Biotite mica. Each mineral is structurally distinct from the previous one; early crystals react with the remaining melt to form the next phase.
- **Continuous branch (plagioclase feldspar):** Ca-rich plagioclase (anorthite) → progressively more Na-rich compositions → Na-rich plagioclase (albite). The crystal structure remains the same but composition shifts continuously as Ca is replaced by Na.

Both branches converge at **potassium feldspar**, **muscovite**, and finally **quartz**, which crystallizes last at the lowest temperatures.

#### Mafic vs. Felsic Minerals
Early-crystallizing minerals — olivine and pyroxene — are **mafic** (rich in magnesium and iron, dark-colored, high density). Late-crystallizing minerals — plagioclase, K-feldspar, muscovite, and quartz — are **felsic** (silicon- and aluminum-rich, light-colored, lower density). **Mafic rocks** such as basalt and gabbro are dominated by early-series minerals; **felsic rocks** such as granite and rhyolite are dominated by late-series minerals.

#### Fractional Crystallization
**Fractional crystallization** occurs when early-formed crystals are physically removed from the melt (by sinking or being carried away). This prevents the back-reaction that would normally consume them and leaves the remaining melt progressively more silica-rich and felsic. Through fractional crystallization a single basaltic parent magma can produce a range of igneous rock types — a process called **magmatic differentiation**. It is the principal mechanism by which Earth's crust has evolved toward more felsic compositions over geologic time.
