#### Mechanical Weathering
Mechanical (physical) weathering breaks rocks into smaller pieces without changing their chemical composition. Frost wedging: water expands 9% upon freezing, wedging cracks apart — most effective in freeze-thaw cycles (alpine, polar). Exfoliation (unloading): as overlying rock is eroded, pressure decreases — outer rock expands and peels in sheets (granitic domes like Half Dome). Abrasion: wind, water, glaciers physically grind rocks. Root wedging: plant roots grow into cracks. The result is smaller fragments with increased surface area, accelerating chemical weathering.

#### Chemical Weathering
Chemical weathering changes mineral composition through chemical reactions. Hydrolysis: water reacts with silicate minerals — feldspar → kaolinite clay + silica + potassium ions (the dominant weathering process for most crustal rocks). Oxidation: iron-bearing minerals react with O₂ → iron oxides (hematite, limonite — the source of red/orange/brown coloring in soils; "rusting" of rocks). Carbonation: CO₂ dissolves in water → carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) → dissolves limestone/marble (karst topography: caves, sinkholes). Dissolution: soluble minerals (halite, calcite) dissolve directly in water. Warm, wet climates accelerate chemical weathering; cold, dry climates favor mechanical.

#### Soil Formation and Profiles
Soil is the product of weathered rock (regolith) plus organic matter (humus), water, air, and living organisms. Soil forms in distinct horizontal layers (horizons): O horizon (organic litter/humus, surface); A horizon (topsoil — humus + minerals, biologically active zone); B horizon (subsoil — leached minerals from A accumulate; clay, iron oxides); C horizon (partially weathered parent rock); R horizon (unweathered bedrock). Climate (especially precipitation and temperature), parent material, organisms, topography, and time control soil development. Tropical soils (oxisols) are deeply weathered and leached; arid soils (aridisols) are shallow with mineral accumulations near the surface.
