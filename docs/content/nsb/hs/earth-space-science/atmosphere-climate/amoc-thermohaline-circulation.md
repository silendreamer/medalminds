#### Mechanism and Heat Transport
The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation consists of the warm Gulf Stream in the upper ocean transporting heat northward, with compensating return flow of cold deep water southward. Cold Arctic surface water becomes denser through evaporation (increasing salinity) and cooling, sinks in the Nordic Seas and Labrador Sea, forming North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW) at ~1,000–2,000 meters depth. This sinking requires the water to be colder and/or saltier than surrounding water—a density inversion. The sinking drives the overturning circulation. AMOC heat transport is ~1.3 petawatts northward in the Atlantic, comparable to the net solar energy input to the entire planet. This heat transport warms Northern Europe by ~5°C relative to similar latitudes in North America (e.g., Britain is ~5°C warmer than Canada at the same latitude).

#### Observational Evidence of Weakening
Direct measurements from the RAPID array (sensors across the Atlantic at 26°N) since 2004 show AMOC has weakened by ~15% on average. Proxy records (sea surface temperature, salinity, radiocarbon dating of deep cores) suggest AMOC is now weaker than any 100-year period in the last 1,000 years. Some studies suggest it declined ~3% per decade since the 1950s. The causes include: (1) freshwater from Greenland ice melt reducing surface salinity (less dense), (2) warming reducing water density, (3) increased precipitation over the Atlantic. Any of these reduce the density gradient needed for sinking.

#### Climate Projections and Tipping Points
Climate models consistently project further AMOC weakening over the 21st century—by 2100, most models show 20–50% decline. Some extreme models suggest a "tipping point" where AMOC collapses entirely (becomes bistable—unable to restart if stopped). This would have severe impacts: Northern Europe would cool by 3–8°C, Atlantic hurricane intensity might increase, Atlantic tropical cyclone frequency could shift, Atlantic overturning circulation-driven heat transport would cease. However, model uncertainty remains high about whether a true tipping point exists or whether AMOC would stabilize at a new, weaker state. Observations don't yet show collapse, only gradual weakening.

#### Consequences of AMOC Slowdown
Even gradual weakening has impacts. Warming in the Atlantic is currently more modest than the global average because AMOC import of heat is declining. This creates the "Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation" (AMO)—a pattern of Atlantic warming/cooling over decades affecting North Atlantic hurricane frequency and Sahel rainfall. If AMOC weakens sharply: (1) Atlantic hurricane season could intensify (warmer sea surface temperature, less wind shear), (2) UK/Northern Europe cool while the rest of the world warms (large regional contrast), (3) North Atlantic upwelling could intensify (compensating for reduced overturning), (4) Atlantic tropical cyclones could intensify if they shift westward into warmer water. The probability and timeline of significant AMOC weakening remain uncertain—a key climate modeling frontier.
