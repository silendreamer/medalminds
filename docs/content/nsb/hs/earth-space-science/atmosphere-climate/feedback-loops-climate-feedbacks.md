#### Albedo Feedback (Positive)
As temperature rises, ice and snow retreat, exposing darker land and ocean. This reduces the planetary albedo (reflectivity), allowing more solar radiation to be absorbed, further warming the planet. This is a positive feedback—warming causes changes (reduced ice) that cause more warming. Snow/ice albedo feedback is ~0.5–1 W/m²K (each 1°C of warming produces 0.5–1 W/m² of additional radiative forcing). Arctic sea ice has declined ~15% per decade since 1979; if this trend continues, albedo feedback will accelerate warming. The Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (~56 million years ago) saw extreme warming partly due to albedo feedback—reduced ice/snow and expanded ocean coverage reduced planetary albedo, amplifying warming from CO2. Conversely, during ice ages, high albedo from extensive ice slowed the warming from Milankovitch cycles.

#### Water Vapor Feedback (Positive)
Warm air holds more moisture: saturation mixing ratio increases ~7% per °C (Clausius-Clapeyron relation). If relative humidity stays constant, atmospheric water vapor increases by ~7%/°C. Since water vapor is a potent greenhouse gas, this amplifies warming: each °C of warming produces ~1.8 W/m² of additional radiative forcing through water vapor feedback. This is the largest feedback in climate models. Water vapor feedback is well-constrained by observations (satellite humidity data), with good agreement between models and observations on its magnitude. The uncertainty in total climate sensitivity is mainly from cloud feedbacks, not water vapor.

#### Cloud Feedback (Uncertain)
Clouds affect climate by reflecting solar radiation (cooling) and trapping infrared (warming). As climate warms, cloud cover changes—but the sign and magnitude remain uncertain. Low clouds (marine boundary layer clouds) reflect solar well and trap little infrared, so they cool the climate (negative feedback). High clouds (cirrus) trap infrared well and reflect little solar, so they warm the climate (positive feedback). If warming increases low clouds, the feedback is negative; if it increases high clouds, the feedback is positive. Models disagree on which dominates. Most models show cloud feedbacks are weakly positive (amplifying warming), but with large uncertainty. Some models show cloud feedback could be negative. This uncertainty (~±1 W/m²K) is a major source of climate sensitivity uncertainty.

#### Lapse Rate Feedback (Negative)
The lapse rate (temperature change with altitude) affects the radiative balance. If the troposphere warms faster near the surface than aloft, the temperature at which radiation escapes to space increases, reducing outgoing longwave radiation and dampening the temperature rise at the surface (negative feedback). This effect is ~-0.3 W/m²K. Some processes (e.g., stratospheric cooling) contribute additional negative lapse rate feedbacks. The combination of water vapor feedback (+1.8 W/m²K) and lapse rate feedback (-0.3 W/m²K) produces net positive feedback of ~1.5 W/m²K.

#### Climate Sensitivity
Climate sensitivity (ΔT for a doubling of CO2) depends on feedbacks. Without feedbacks, CO2 doubling produces ~1.1°C warming (forcing of ~3.7 W/m² divided by ~3.3 W/m²K per °C). With feedbacks, the warming is amplified. If feedbacks sum to +1 W/m²K, the net forcing-to-feedback ratio is 3.7/(3.3+1) ≈ 0.9°C. If feedbacks sum to +2 W/m²K (including cloud feedback), the ratio is 3.7/(3.3+2) ≈ 1.3°C. Observations and models suggest feedbacks sum to ~1.5–2 W/m²K, giving climate sensitivity of ~3°C (2.5–4°C range including model uncertainty). This means a doubling of CO2 from 280 to 560 ppm produces ~3°C warming, while 1.5× CO2 (to 420 ppm, current level) produces ~1.7°C warming. Additional warming of ~0.5°C is "in the pipeline" even if CO2 stopped increasing.

#### Review Questions

2. Why does water vapor feedback amplify warming even if relative humidity stays constant?
3. If feedbacks total +2 W/m²K and the CO2 forcing is 3.7 W/m² for a doubling, calculate the equilibrium warming (climate sensitivity).
