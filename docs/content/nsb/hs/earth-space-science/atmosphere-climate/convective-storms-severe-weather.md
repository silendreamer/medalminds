#### Instability and CAPE
Convective Available Potential Energy (CAPE) measures atmospheric instability—the energy available to fuel rising parcels. It's calculated as the integral of buoyancy along an air parcel's ascent path. CAPE = ∫g × (T_parcel - T_environment)/T_environment × dz from LFC (level of free convection) to LNB (level of neutral buoyancy). Typical values: <1,000 J/kg (weak convection), 1,000–2,500 J/kg (moderate risk of strong storms), >4,000 J/kg (extreme instability). The May 22, 2011 Joplin, Missouri EF5 tornado occurred in environments with CAPE >6,000 J/kg. However, CAPE alone doesn't create severe storms; wind shear is also essential.

#### Wind Shear and Storm Rotation
Wind shear (change in wind with height) is crucial for strong rotating updrafts. If wind speed and direction change with height, the shear can create horizontal rotation vorticity. Strong updrafts tilt this horizontal vorticity vertical, creating a rotating column of air—a mesocyclone. Supercell thunderstorms (long-lived, rotating storms) have the strongest rotation and highest potential for large hail and tornadoes. The combination of high CAPE and strong shear defines the "sweet spot" for supercells. The Bulk Richardson Number (BRN) = CAPE/(wind shear²) quantifies the balance; BRN ~10–50 is optimal for supercells.

#### Hail Formation and Severe Downdrafts
Supercells produce large hail through repeated cycling: a hailstone is lofted into the -20°C to -40°C layer (where ice grows efficiently), accumulates a layer of ice, falls, is caught by a new updraft, and rises again. Large hail (>2 inches) indicates violent updrafts (>100 mph). The June 11, 1990 Denver hailstorm produced golf-ball-sized hail and $1+ billion in damage. Hail formation requires high water content, strong updrafts, and efficient ice growth—supercells provide all three. The largest hailstone ever recorded was 8 inches diameter (Vivian, South Dakota, 2010). Downdrafts form when cool air (from evaporating precipitation and cold air aloft) sinks. These can produce wind gusts >80 knots (derechos) that can level forests. The June 2012 Midwest derecho had gusts exceeding 100 mph, causing $11 billion damage.

#### Tornado Genesis
Tornadoes form when a mesocyclone (rotating column in a supercell) extends down to the surface. The tightest rotation occurs in the subcloud vortex, where converging air below the mesocyclone focuses rotation. Downbursts can also create tornadoes through horizontal vorticity. Most strong tornadoes (EF3+) occur in supercells; weaker tornadoes can form in regular thunderstorms. Tornadogenesis is not fully understood—why some supercells produce tornadoes while others don't remains an open question. The lowest pressure in a tornado core is ~250 mb, creating extreme wind speeds (>200 mph in EF5 tornadoes). Fortunately, tornadoes cover small areas (~1 km² for average tornadoes) and last minutes to hours, limiting damage to paths.
