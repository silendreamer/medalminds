#### The Hadley Cell (Tropical Circulation)
The Hadley cell is the dominant circulation in the tropics, extending from ~0° to 30° latitude. At the equator, intense solar heating warms the surface, causing air to rise (convection). This rising air moves poleward at altitude, cooling by radiation. Around 30° latitude, this cooled air descends, creating high pressure (subtropical highs). The descending air diverges at the surface: some returns toward the equator (deflected westward by the Coriolis effect, creating the northeast trade winds in the Northern Hemisphere and southeast trades in the Southern Hemisphere), while some flows poleward. The trade winds are remarkably consistent—ancient sailors relied on them for transoceanic voyages. The descending branch (30°N/S) is the driest part of the troposphere, creating the world's major deserts: the Sahara, Arabian, Australian, Kalahari, and American Southwest deserts all lie near 30°.

#### The Ferrel Cell (Mid-Latitude Circulation)
The Ferrel cell occupies the mid-latitudes (30–60°), driven partly by energy from the Hadley cell and partly by the polar cell. Air flows poleward at the surface (deflected eastward by Coriolis, creating westerly winds—the "Roaring Forties" between 40–50°S). At high altitude, air flows toward the pole. Unlike the Hadley and polar cells, the Ferrel cell is not a direct thermally driven convection cell but an indirect circulation maintaining momentum balance. It features strong baroclinic instability—the temperature gradient between equator-ward and pole-ward air allows formation of cyclones and anticyclones (low and high-pressure systems). These traveling weather systems bring mid-latitude "weather" (changing conditions), unlike the stable trade wind zones of the tropics.

#### The Polar Cell (Polar Circulation)
The polar cell (60–90°) consists of cold descending air at the poles creating high pressure. This polar high-pressure air flows toward lower latitudes (deflected westward by Coriolis, creating the polar easterlies), and at ~60° latitude, it meets the warm air from the Ferrel cell at the polar front. This creates the jet stream—a narrow, fast-moving ribbon of air at the tropopause where temperature and pressure gradients are steepest. The polar front jet stream (near 50–60°N/S) is the most important to mid-latitude weather; the subtropical jet stream (near 30°N/S) is associated with the Hadley/Ferrel boundary.

#### Jet Streams and Upper-Level Winds
Jet streams are rivers of fast-moving air (typically 100–200 knots) at the tropopause where temperature gradients are strongest. The zonal (east-west) jet components are strongest; meridional (north-south) deviations create troughs (southward dips, bringing cold air) and ridges (northward bulges, bringing warm air). The Arctic Oscillation and North Atlantic Oscillation describe the wobbling of the polar jet—when the jet is far north (positive phase), mid-latitudes are warm and block patterns are rare; when it dips south (negative phase), cold air surges equatorward, causing extreme cold outbreaks. Recent research suggests climate change is reducing the temperature gradient between tropics and poles, potentially weakening the jet stream and allowing more persistent weather patterns.
