#### Why Rocks Melt
Rock melts when temperature exceeds the **solidus** — the temperature at which melt first appears at a given pressure and composition. Three mechanisms achieve melting in different tectonic settings:

1. **Decompression melting:** At mid-ocean ridges, upwelling asthenosphere rises without significant cooling. As pressure decreases, the solidus temperature drops faster than the rock's actual temperature, crossing into the melt field. No extra heat is needed — just reduced pressure. Produces **tholeiitic basalt** (mafic, low silica, MOR typical).

2. **Flux melting:** At subduction zones, the descending slab releases water and other volatiles (CO₂, fluorine) that lower the melting point of the overlying mantle wedge. Water acts as a "flux" — it breaks Si-O bonds in silicate minerals. Produces water-rich, silica-enriched (andesitic) magmas.

3. **Heat transfer melting:** At continental hotspots (Yellowstone), mantle plume heat conducts into overlying continental crust, partially melting it. Produces silica-rich rhyolitic magmas capable of supervolcanic eruptions.

#### Bowen's Reaction Series
As a basaltic magma cools, minerals crystallize in a predictable order described by **Bowen's Reaction Series** (Norman Bowen, 1920s). High-temperature minerals (olivine, pyroxene, Ca-plagioclase) crystallize first, removing Mg, Fe, and Ca from the melt. The remaining melt becomes progressively enriched in Si, Al, Na, K — evolving from mafic (basalt) toward intermediate (andesite) and ultimately felsic (rhyolite) composition through **fractional crystallization**. This is why subduction zone magmas are more silica-rich than mid-ocean ridge magmas, even when both start from mantle material.
