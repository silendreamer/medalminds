#### Transform Faults and Their Seismicity
At **transform boundaries**, plates slide past each other horizontally with no creation or destruction of crust. The **San Andreas Fault** in California is a transform boundary between the Pacific Plate (moving northwest) and the North American Plate (moving southeast) — relative motion ~46 mm/yr. Transform faults produce shallow, often powerful earthquakes but no volcanism. The 1906 San Francisco earthquake (M~7.9) and 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake resulted from San Andreas system faults. Most oceanic transform faults connect offset segments of mid-ocean ridges — they form because ridges don't always grow in straight lines.

#### Hotspots: Mantle Plumes Beneath Plates
**Hotspots** are volcanic centers fed by **mantle plumes** — narrow upwellings of hot mantle material from deep within the Earth (possibly from the core-mantle boundary). Unlike plate boundary volcanoes, hotspots are stationary relative to the deep mantle while the tectonic plate above moves over them, creating a **chain of progressively older volcanic islands** in the direction of plate motion. The **Hawaiian-Emperor seamount chain** records the Pacific Plate moving over the Hawaiian hotspot: Hawaii (youngest, active) → Maui → Oahu (older, eroded) → extinct seamounts extending to the Aleutian Trench. The bend in the chain at ~43 Ma records a change in Pacific Plate motion direction.

#### Yellowstone Hotspot
The **Yellowstone hotspot** is a continental hotspot that has produced a series of supervolcanic calderas across the Snake River Plain. The hotspot has been stationary while the North American Plate moves southwest over it, leaving a track of caldera complexes. Yellowstone's magma system today feeds geysers (Old Faithful), hot springs, and ongoing seismicity. Its last supereruption was ~640,000 years ago.
