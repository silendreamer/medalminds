#### Formation and Types of Fossil Fuels
Coal forms from accumulated plant debris (peat) compressed and heated over millions of years. The rank progression: peat (50% C) → lignite (70% C) → subbituminous (77% C) → bituminous (80% C) → anthracite (93% C) reflects increasing carbon content and energy density. Anthracite, formed at highest pressure/temperature, has the highest energy content (~30 MJ/kg) and lowest sulfur (burns cleanest). Petroleum and natural gas form from marine plankton and algae buried in sediments. Source rocks must be: (1) organic-rich (marine shales), (2) buried deeply (thermal maturity window 50–150°C), (3) isolated from oxygen (anoxic). Oil generation occurs at temperatures 60–120°C; temperatures >120°C produce mainly gas. The maturation timescale is geological—millions of years. Generated hydrocarbons migrate upward into reservoir rocks (porous sandstones, carbonates) and are trapped by impermeable seals (shale, evaporites). Without a trap, hydrocarbons continue migrating to surface, lost. This trap-seal-source-migration system is the petroleum system—all must be present for economic accumulation.

#### Global Reserves and Production
Proven coal reserves are ~1 trillion tonnes, lasting ~130 years at current consumption (25 billion tonnes/year). Proven petroleum reserves are ~1.7 trillion barrels (~250 billion tonnes), lasting ~50 years at current production (90 million barrels/day). Proven natural gas reserves are ~180 trillion m³, lasting ~52 years. These "reserve lifetimes" assume static production—increasing production shortens them, while price increases might expand reserves (costlier extraction becomes economic at higher prices). Actual depletion timescales are hard to predict because: (1) new discoveries extend reserves, (2) technology (enhanced recovery, unconventional resources like shale) accesses previously uneconomic resources, (3) price-driven economics determine whether resources are "proven." The concept of "peak oil" (maximum production rate reached, then declining) is still debated—some predict peak oil has occurred, others forecast it in the 2030s, while others argue unconventional oil extends production.

#### Energy Density and Comparison
Energy content of fuels varies: coal ~24 MJ/kg, crude oil ~46 MJ/kg, natural gas ~55 MJ/kg (by mass). Natural gas has higher energy density than coal but lower than oil. However, practical energy yields depend on thermodynamic efficiency (power plant efficiency ~35% for coal, ~45% for natural gas, ~60% for combined cycle gas plants). CO2 emissions per unit energy vary: coal ~95 g CO2/MJ, oil ~75 g CO2/MJ, natural gas ~50 g CO2/MJ. So natural gas is the cleanest fossil fuel, coal the dirtiest. Switching from coal to natural gas reduces CO2 emissions by ~50%, a significant mitigation step (though not sufficient to meet climate targets without renewable transition).

#### Unconventional Fossil Fuels
Shale oil and shale gas (hydrocarbons in low-permeability source rocks) were historically uneconomic but hydraulic fracturing (fracking) made them viable. Fracking injects pressurized fluid to crack rock and prop open fractures, allowing hydrocarbon flow. Shale gas production in the US surged from <1% of gas supply (2000) to ~50% (2020), dramatically extending US gas resources. Similarly, shale oil contributes to US oil production. Environmental concerns include: (1) water contamination (fracturing fluids, produced water), (2) methane leakage (greenhouse gas), (3) induced seismicity (earthquakes from wastewater injection). Oil sands (viscous bitumen mixed with sand in Alberta, Canada; Venezuela; Russia) require thermal processing (heating) to extract, producing 1 tonne of CO2 per barrel (vs. ~0.5 tonne for conventional oil). Heavy oil, tar sands, and methane hydrates are difficult/costly to extract and have high environmental impacts, making them poor choices for climate-constrained future.
