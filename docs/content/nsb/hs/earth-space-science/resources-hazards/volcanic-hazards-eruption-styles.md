#### Magma Composition and Eruption Style
Magma composition (silica content, water content) determines eruption type. Low-silica basaltic magma (~50% SiO₂, low viscosity) erupts as effusive flows (Hawaii eruptions). High-silica rhyolitic magma (~70% SiO₂, high viscosity) erupts explosively, forming pyroclastic flows (Philippines 1991 Pinatubo). The relationship: higher silica = higher viscosity = higher gas pressure required to exsolve bubbles = more explosive. Dissolved water content also matters: water lowers magma melting point and, when exsolved, creates gas bubbles driving eruptions. A magma at depth might be quiescent (no bubbles because pressure keeps water dissolved), but rapid ascent causes decompression, gas exsolution, and explosive behavior. This explains how eruptions can begin suddenly.

#### Eruption Classification
The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) classifies eruptions 0–8: VEI 0–1 (Hawaiian, gentle effusion), VEI 2 (Strombolian, small explosions), VEI 3–4 (Vulcanian/Plinian, major explosive), VEI 5+ (rare, catastrophic). Mount Pinatubo (1991) was VEI 6, one of the largest of the 20th century. VEI 7–8 (Yellowstone supereruptions) haven't occurred in recorded history but are known from geological record. VEI correlates with magma composition: basaltic shield volcanoes are VEI 1–2, andesitic stratovolcanoes are VEI 3–5, rhyolitic calderas are VEI 6–8. This composition variation reflects tectonic setting: subduction zones (where water-enriched oceanic crust descends) produce arc magmas (intermediate composition, VEI 3–5), while hot spots (Hawaiian islands) produce basalts (VEI 1–2).

#### Pyroclastic Flows and Lahars
Pyroclastic flows are mixtures of hot gas and fragmented rock traveling >100 km/h down slopes. They cannot be outrun and have temperatures >500°C. The 1902 Mount Pelée (Martinique) pyroclastic flow killed 29,000 people in seconds. Pyroclastic flows deposit pyroclastic density currents (PDCs) that can travel far from volcano (100+ km for large eruptions) and destroy everything in their path. Lahars are mudflows of volcanic sediment, triggered by heavy rainfall on fresh ash deposits or melting of summit ice/snow. The 1985 Nevado Ruiz (Colombia) lahars killed 23,000 when pyroclastic flows melted glaciers, creating lahars that filled valleys downslope. Lahars move faster than lava (60+ km/h), travel farther, and are one of the most dangerous volcanic hazards. Lahars can remobilize years after eruption if heavy rainfall occurs on steep ash deposits.

#### Volcanic Ash and Gas
Volcanic ash (fine particles <63 micrometers) is abrasive and causes respiratory irritation. Ash falls from eruption columns can darken the sky at midday, closing schools/businesses. The 1991 Pinatubo ash fell across the Indian Ocean, turning day to darkness in Tagaytay (80 km away). More seriously, ash clogs machinery, contaminates water supplies, and causes respiratory problems (silicosis from chronic exposure). Volcanic gases (SO₂, CO₂, HCl) can be lethal in high concentrations. The 1986 Lake Nyos (Cameroon) CO₂ release (from a volcanic lake) asphyxiated 1,700 people in surrounding villages—the heavier CO₂ hugged the ground, displacing breathable air. Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) forms sulfate aerosols that cool the climate (as discussed in the climate section above) but cause acid rain and respiratory problems. SO₂ from volcanoes contributes to air pollution in downwind regions.
