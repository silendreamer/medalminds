#### Ore Deposits and Mineral Concentrations
Ore is rock with economically recoverable mineral(s). A "ore deposit" requires: (1) elevated concentration of desired metal, (2) adequate volume, (3) economic viability. Background crustal abundance vs. ore concentration:
- Copper: 50 ppm crustal, 5,000 ppm ore (~100× enrichment)
- Gold: 0.004 ppm crustal, 10 ppm ore (~2,500× enrichment)
- Iron: 5% crustal, 50–70% ore (~10× enrichment)

Achieving this enrichment requires geological processes: hydrothermal alteration (hot water dissolving and reprecipitating metals), weathering (surface concentration via leaching), evaporation (salt, potash deposits), biomineralization. Ore deposit types:
- **Porphyry Cu**: magma crystallizes, cooling fluids deposit Cu—typical grade 0.5–2% Cu
- **Placer deposits**: mechanical weathering concentrates dense minerals (gold, diamonds) in stream gravels
- **Laterite**: tropical weathering concentrates Fe, Al, Ni in residual soils
- **Evaporites**: seawater evaporation concentrates salt, gypsum, potash

#### Mining Methods and Impacts
**Surface mining** (open pit) removes overburden to access ore; efficient for low-grade deposits but creates massive disturbance. Pits 1+ km deep have been excavated (Bingham Canyon copper mine, Utah). Environmental impacts: landscape destruction, acid mine drainage (exposed sulfides oxidize, creating acidic water that mobilizes metals), habitat loss, dust. **Underground mining** for deep deposits uses shafts and tunnels; less surface disturbance but higher cost and safety risks. Methane/gas hazards and collapse risks are common. **Placer mining** (panning, sluicing) disturbs stream sediments; cyanide leaching for gold dissolves ore but is toxic to aquatic life if not contained.

#### Water Resources from Mining
Mining is water-intensive: copper extraction ~100,000 L/tonne ore, and concentrating ore requires flotation (water-intensive separation). In water-scarce regions (Chile, Peru for copper; South Africa for gold), mining competes with agriculture and municipal use. The Atacama Desert (driest non-polar place) has massive copper mines that deplete freshwater aquifers, threatening indigenous communities. Climate change exacerbates water stress—seasonal snowmelt (which mines traditionally rely on) declines, forcing deeper groundwater extraction. Peru's water conflicts between mining and agriculture are escalating.

#### Sustainability and Circular Economy
Ore depletion is inevitable at current consumption. High-grade deposits are preferentially mined first (lower-cost extraction). As grades decline, extraction costs rise and environmental impacts worsen (more waste rock per tonne metal). Some metals (like phosphorus, a critical agricultural nutrient) have no substitutes; once depleted, civilization is constrained. Recycling reduces primary mining demand: aluminum recycling is ~50% global supply (reduces energy use by 95%), copper recycling ~30%, gold recycling ~30%. A circular economy (products designed for recycling, high recovery rates) could extend resource lifetimes. However, current recycling is limited by economics and technology—rare earth elements (essential for renewable energy, electronics) are < 2% recycled globally. Future supply security is a major concern for energy transition (wind turbines need rare earths, neodymium for generators).

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate the ore concentrate ratio needed to produce 1 tonne of copper from 0.5% grade ore.
2. Explain why ore grades decline over time and what this implies for environmental impacts.
3. How does recycling reduce environmental impacts of mining?
