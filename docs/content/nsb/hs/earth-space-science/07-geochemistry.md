## Subtopic: Rock Cycle (Igneous, Sedimentary, Metamorphic)

### The Rock Cycle: An Overview
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** rock-cycle-overview
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** igneous · sedimentary · metamorphic · lithification · uplift · weathering
**Summary:** The rock cycle describes the continuous transformation of rocks among igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic types through processes driven by plate tectonics, the water cycle, and Earth's internal heat.

#### Igneous Rocks: From Melt to Solid
Igneous rocks form when magma (molten rock underground) or lava (at the surface) cools and solidifies. Intrusive (plutonic) igneous rocks cool slowly underground, allowing large crystals to grow: granite (coarse-grained, felsic, rich in quartz and feldspar), gabbro (coarse-grained, mafic, rich in pyroxene and olivine), diorite, peridotite (mantle rock). Extrusive (volcanic) igneous rocks cool quickly at the surface, producing fine-grained or glassy texture: basalt (fine-grained, mafic — oceanic crust and lava flows), rhyolite (fine-grained, felsic), obsidian (volcanic glass), pumice (vesicular glass). Crystal size is the key textural indicator: large crystals = slow cooling = intrusive; small or no crystals = fast cooling = extrusive.

#### Sedimentary Rocks: From Particles to Stone
Sedimentary rocks form from compaction and cementation (lithification) of sediments, or from precipitation of minerals from solution. Clastic sedimentary rocks form from fragments of pre-existing rocks: conglomerate (rounded gravel), sandstone (sand-sized grains), siltstone, shale (clay-sized — finest grained). Chemical/biochemical sedimentary rocks precipitate from water: limestone (CaCO₃ — may be biogenic from shells or chemical precipitation), rock salt (NaCl — evaporite), chert (microcrystalline silica). Sedimentary rocks form in horizontal layers (strata), contain fossils, and cover ~75% of Earth's surface (but only ~5% by volume of the crust).

#### Metamorphic Rocks: Changed by Heat and Pressure
Metamorphic rocks form from pre-existing rocks (of any type) subjected to heat, pressure, or chemically active fluids — without melting. Regional metamorphism (large scale, convergent margins): slate → phyllite → schist → gneiss (increasing metamorphic grade). Contact metamorphism (heat from nearby intrusion, local): marble (from limestone), hornfels (from shale). Key texture: foliation — parallel alignment of minerals (mica, hornblende) due to directed pressure. Foliated rocks: slate, phyllite, schist, gneiss. Non-foliated: marble (from limestone), quartzite (from sandstone). Mineral assemblages define metamorphic facies and pressure-temperature conditions.

#### The Rock Cycle Pathways
Every rock type can transform to any other: igneous rock → weathering/erosion → sediments → lithification → sedimentary rock → burial/heat/pressure → metamorphic rock → melting → magma → igneous rock. Or: metamorphic rock → uplift → weathering → sedimentary. Plate tectonics drives the cycle: subduction brings rocks to depth (metamorphism/melting); uplift exposes deep rocks (weathering/erosion); volcanism adds igneous material at surface. The rock cycle is not a simple circle — multiple pathways and feedback loops operate simultaneously.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the primary difference in texture between intrusive and extrusive igneous rocks, and why?
2. What process converts loose sediments into sedimentary rock?
3. What is foliation in metamorphic rocks, and what conditions produce it?

### Weathering, Erosion, and Soil Formation
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** weathering-erosion-soil-formation
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** mechanical weathering · chemical weathering · hydrolysis · oxidation · soil profile · A/B/C horizons
**Summary:** Weathering breaks rocks into smaller particles through physical and chemical processes; erosion transports them; soil forms from the accumulated weathered material plus organic matter.

#### Mechanical Weathering
Mechanical (physical) weathering breaks rocks into smaller pieces without changing their chemical composition. Frost wedging: water expands 9% upon freezing, wedging cracks apart — most effective in freeze-thaw cycles (alpine, polar). Exfoliation (unloading): as overlying rock is eroded, pressure decreases — outer rock expands and peels in sheets (granitic domes like Half Dome). Abrasion: wind, water, glaciers physically grind rocks. Root wedging: plant roots grow into cracks. The result is smaller fragments with increased surface area, accelerating chemical weathering.

#### Chemical Weathering
Chemical weathering changes mineral composition through chemical reactions. Hydrolysis: water reacts with silicate minerals — feldspar → kaolinite clay + silica + potassium ions (the dominant weathering process for most crustal rocks). Oxidation: iron-bearing minerals react with O₂ → iron oxides (hematite, limonite — the source of red/orange/brown coloring in soils; "rusting" of rocks). Carbonation: CO₂ dissolves in water → carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) → dissolves limestone/marble (karst topography: caves, sinkholes). Dissolution: soluble minerals (halite, calcite) dissolve directly in water. Warm, wet climates accelerate chemical weathering; cold, dry climates favor mechanical.

#### Soil Formation and Profiles
Soil is the product of weathered rock (regolith) plus organic matter (humus), water, air, and living organisms. Soil forms in distinct horizontal layers (horizons): O horizon (organic litter/humus, surface); A horizon (topsoil — humus + minerals, biologically active zone); B horizon (subsoil — leached minerals from A accumulate; clay, iron oxides); C horizon (partially weathered parent rock); R horizon (unweathered bedrock). Climate (especially precipitation and temperature), parent material, organisms, topography, and time control soil development. Tropical soils (oxisols) are deeply weathered and leached; arid soils (aridisols) are shallow with mineral accumulations near the surface.

#### Review Questions
1. Differentiate mechanical and chemical weathering, giving one example of each.
2. What chemical reaction is primarily responsible for the breakdown of feldspar in granite?
3. Describe the A, B, and C horizons of a soil profile and what each contains.

### Reading Rocks: Applied Rock Identification
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** applied-rock-identification
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** grain size · composition · texture · Bowen's reaction series · rock identification
**Summary:** Identifying rocks from their physical properties (texture, mineral composition, grain size, foliation) requires integrating knowledge of how each rock type forms.

#### Bowen's Reaction Series
When magma cools, minerals crystallize in a predictable sequence. The discontinuous series: olivine → pyroxene → amphibole → biotite mica → potassium feldspar → muscovite → quartz. The continuous series: calcium-rich plagioclase → sodium-rich plagioclase (simultaneously). Early-crystallizing minerals (olivine, pyroxene) are mafic (Mg/Fe-rich, dark, high melting point). Late-crystallizing minerals (quartz, K-feldspar) are felsic (Si/Al-rich, light, low melting point). The sequence explains why olivine is found in mantle rocks and basalt but not granite, and why granite contains quartz while basalt doesn't.

#### Practical Rock Identification
Granite: coarse-grained, interlocking crystals, light-colored (feldspar + quartz + mica) — slow cooling in magma chambers. Basalt: fine-grained, dark (pyroxene + plagioclase) — rapid volcanic cooling. Sandstone: sand-grain sized, may show cross-bedding. Limestone: often contains fossil shells, fizzes with HCl (carbonate reaction). Shale: fine-grained, fissile (splits in sheets), often grey/black. Quartzite: hard, interlocking quartz crystals, often white. Marble: crystalline calcite, often white or colored with veins. Schist: foliated, visible mica flakes. Gneiss: alternating light and dark bands (compositional banding from high-grade metamorphism).

#### Review Questions
1. A rock is coarse-grained, contains quartz and pink feldspar, and interlocking crystals. What rock type is it, and where did it form?
2. Using Bowen's Reaction Series, explain why basalt and granite have different mineral compositions even though both are igneous.
3. A student finds a rock that splits into thin sheets and contains tiny fish fossils. What rock type is it?

### Rock Types and Earth History
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** rock-types-earth-history
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** stratigraphic principles · unconformity · index fossils · metamorphic grade · tectonic setting
**Summary:** The rock record encodes Earth history — sedimentary sequences reveal past environments, metamorphic rocks reveal tectonic episodes, and igneous rocks mark magmatic events.

#### Stratigraphy and Sedimentary Records
Principle of original horizontality: sedimentary strata were deposited horizontally. Principle of superposition: in an undisturbed sequence, older layers are below younger ones. Principle of cross-cutting relationships: a feature that cuts across other features is younger. Unconformities — gaps in the sedimentary record — represent periods of erosion or non-deposition: angular unconformity (tilted layers below horizontal layers), disconformity (both horizontal, but missing time), nonconformity (sedimentary over igneous/metamorphic). Index fossils (from short-lived but geographically widespread species) are used for correlation of strata across distances.

#### Metamorphic Rocks as Tectonic Records
The grade of metamorphism (low → high) records burial depth and geothermal gradient. Blueschist facies (high pressure, low temperature): formed in cold subducting slabs. Eclogite facies (very high pressure): subducted oceanic crust at depth. Granulite facies (high T, moderate P): lower continental crust. The sequence of mineral assemblages (index minerals: chlorite → biotite → garnet → staurolite → kyanite → sillimanite in pelitic rocks) records the P-T path a rock followed — key for reconstructing ancient mountain belts.

#### Review Questions
1. State the principle of superposition and explain one situation where it might be violated.
2. What is an angular unconformity, and what geological history does it record?
3. What does the presence of blueschist facies metamorphic rocks on the surface tell us about ancient plate tectonics?

## Subtopic: Mineralogy & Crystal Formation

### Mineral Identification and Properties
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** mineral-identification-properties
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** mineral definition · crystal structure · cleavage vs fracture · hardness · Mohs scale · luster
**Summary:** Minerals are naturally occurring, inorganic solids with a definite chemical composition and ordered crystal structure, identified by physical properties including hardness, cleavage, luster, color, streak, and specific gravity.

#### Defining Minerals
A mineral must be: (1) naturally occurring, (2) inorganic, (3) solid at room temperature, (4) with a definite chemical composition (fixed or within a range), and (5) an ordered internal crystal structure. Ice is a mineral; glass is not (amorphous). Quartz (SiO₂), calcite (CaCO₃), halite (NaCl), feldspar (KAlSi₃O₈ and variants), olivine ((Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄) are key minerals. A rock is an aggregate of minerals (or mineraloids). There are ~5,500 known mineral species; ~30 are geologically important.

#### Physical Properties for Identification
Hardness: resistance to scratching; Mohs scale: 1 (talc) to 10 (diamond). Useful reference: fingernail ~2.5, copper penny ~3, glass plate ~5.5, steel file ~6.5. Quartz = 7; feldspar = 6; calcite = 3; gypsum = 2. Cleavage: tendency to break along flat planes parallel to crystal structure (weak chemical bonds). Described by number of directions and angles. Halite: 3 perfect cleavages at 90° → cubic pieces. Mica (muscovite, biotite): 1 perfect cleavage → thin sheets. Calcite: 3 cleavages NOT at 90° → rhombohedral pieces. Fracture: irregular breaking (not along planes); conchoidal (glass-like, quartz), irregular. Luster: metallic (galena, pyrite) or non-metallic (vitreous/glassy in quartz; pearly in talc; silky in asbestos; resinous in sulfur). Streak: color of mineral in powdered form (more diagnostic than surface color; hematite has red-brown streak even when silvery).

#### Crystal Systems
Crystals form in 7 systems based on symmetry: cubic (isometric): halite, galena, pyrite, fluorite, diamond; tetragonal: zircon; hexagonal/trigonal: quartz, calcite, ice; orthorhombic: olivine, sulfur; monoclinic: gypsum, augite; triclinic: plagioclase feldspar. Crystal form reflects the internal arrangement of atoms. Euhedral crystals (well-formed, perfectly shaped faces) grow in unconstrained spaces (geodes, vugs). Anhedral crystals (no crystal faces visible) grow where space is limited.

#### Key Mineral Groups
Silicates (most common in crust): framework silicates (quartz, feldspar); sheet silicates (mica, clay minerals); chain silicates (pyroxene, amphibole); olivine (isolated tetrahedra). Carbonates: calcite, dolomite — fizz with HCl. Oxides: magnetite (Fe₃O₄, magnetic), hematite (Fe₂O₃). Sulfides: pyrite (FeS₂, "fool's gold"), galena (PbS). Sulfates: gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O). Halides: halite (NaCl), fluorite (CaF₂).

#### Review Questions
1. What is the hardness of quartz on the Mohs scale, and what does hardness measure?
2. Differentiate cleavage from fracture in minerals. Give one example of each.
3. What mineral group makes up the majority of the Earth's crust, and what structural unit do they share?

### How Crystals Form: From Solution and Melt
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** crystal-formation-solution-melt
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** nucleation · crystal growth · saturation · hydrothermal veins · pegmatite · geode
**Summary:** Crystals form when atoms organize into ordered structures from melts, solutions, or vapor — the conditions (temperature, pressure, cooling rate, chemical environment) determine crystal size, shape, and mineral identity.

#### Crystallization from Magma
Magmatic crystallization follows Bowen's Reaction Series (see rock cycle). Slow cooling in a pluton → large crystals (granite, gabbro). Fast cooling in a lava flow → tiny crystals (basalt). Pegmatites form in the final stage of magmatic crystallization when volatile-rich water remains — these melts are highly fluid and allow unusually large crystals to grow: giant quartz, feldspar, mica, and gem minerals (tourmaline, beryl, topaz) — some crystals exceed 10 meters. The Etta Mine spodumene crystal (lithium silicate) in South Dakota reached 14 m.

#### Crystallization from Solution
Minerals precipitate from aqueous solutions when the solution becomes supersaturated — either by cooling (less solute dissolves at low T) or by evaporation (concentration increases). Evaporite deposits: sea or lake water evaporates, precipitating minerals in order of decreasing solubility — calcite and dolomite first, then gypsum, then halite, then sylvite (KCl). Hydrothermal veins: hot water circulating through cracks in rock deposits minerals as it cools — quartz veins, calcite veins, ore deposits of sulfides (galena, sphalerite, chalcopyrite = copper ore). Geodes: hollow spheres lined with crystals (often quartz or amethyst) that grew into a gas cavity in volcanic rock from circulating solutions.

#### Review Questions
1. Why do pegmatites contain unusually large crystals compared to typical granite?
2. In what order do minerals precipitate as seawater evaporates, and what deposits does this produce?
3. What is a geode, and how do the crystals inside it form?

## Subtopic: Isotope Geochemistry & Radiometric Tracers

### Radiometric Dating: Clocks in Rocks
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** radiometric-dating-clocks-in-rocks
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** radioactive decay · half-life · parent-daughter ratio · U-Pb dating · C-14 · isochron
**Summary:** Radioactive isotopes decay at known rates, allowing geologists to calculate the age of rocks and organic material by measuring the ratio of parent to daughter isotopes — the basis for all absolute geochronology.

#### Radioactive Decay and Half-Life
Radioactive isotopes (unstable nuclei) decay by emitting particles or energy, converting parent isotopes to daughter isotopes at a constant rate. The half-life (t₁/₂) is the time for half the parent atoms to decay — a fixed property of each isotope independent of temperature, pressure, or chemistry. After n half-lives, the fraction of parent remaining = (1/2)ⁿ. Example: if t₁/₂ = 10,000 years and a sample has 25% of the original parent left, 2 half-lives have passed → age = 20,000 years. The decay equation: N(t) = N₀e^(–λt), where λ = ln2/t₁/₂ is the decay constant.

#### Key Isotope Systems
Uranium-Lead (U-Pb): U-238 → Pb-206 (t₁/₂ = 4.47 billion years); U-235 → Pb-207 (t₁/₂ = 703 million years). Used for zircon crystals (retain U, exclude initial Pb when crystallized). Oldest known zircons: 4.4 billion years (Jack Hills, Australia). The age of Earth (~4.54 billion years) is determined from meteorite U-Pb dating + lead isotope ratios. Potassium-Argon (K-Ar): K-40 → Ar-40 (t₁/₂ = 1.25 billion years); Ar is a gas that escapes from lava when it erupts but is trapped when it cools → dates volcanic rocks. Used to date the KT boundary (66 Ma), Lucy (Australopithecus, ~3.2 Ma), etc. Rubidium-Strontium (Rb-Sr): t₁/₂ = 47 billion years; useful for very old rocks. Samarium-Neodymium (Sm-Nd): traces mantle vs. crustal processes.

#### Radiocarbon Dating (C-14)
Carbon-14 is produced in the upper atmosphere when cosmic rays hit nitrogen-14. Living organisms continuously incorporate C-14 from the atmosphere/food chain. At death, C-14 intake stops and decay begins. t₁/₂ = 5,730 years. Useful range: ~50,000 years before present (beyond ~10 half-lives, too little parent remains to measure accurately). Used for organic material: wood, charcoal, bone, shell, textiles. NOT useful for minerals or rocks (no carbon). Accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) has extended sensitivity and reduced sample size needed.

#### Isochron Method
A single parent-daughter ratio gives age only if initial daughter abundance is known (often uncertain). The isochron method plots multiple minerals from the same rock with different initial parent/daughter ratios — all points should define a line (isochron) whose slope gives age, independent of initial isotopic composition. Self-consistent check against initial ratio uncertainty. The Rb-Sr and Sm-Nd systems commonly use isochrons.

#### Review Questions
1. A sample originally contained 100 g of a radioactive parent. After 3 half-lives, how many grams of parent remain?
2. Why is C-14 dating not useful for minerals or rocks (like granite or limestone)?
3. What makes zircon an ideal mineral for U-Pb dating?

### Stable Isotopes as Geochemical Tracers
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** stable-isotopes-geochemical-tracers
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** δ¹⁸O · δ¹³C · isotope fractionation · paleoclimate reconstruction · ocean temperature proxies
**Summary:** Stable isotopes (especially oxygen-18/16, carbon-13/12, and hydrogen-2/1) fractionate between phases based on temperature and biological processes — making them powerful tracers of past climate, water cycles, and biological activity.

#### Oxygen Isotope Fractionation
Oxygen has two main stable isotopes: ¹⁶O (light, abundant) and ¹⁸O (heavy, ~0.2% of natural oxygen). During evaporation, lighter ¹⁶O preferentially enters the vapor → precipitation is isotopically lighter than seawater. During glacial periods, vast amounts of ¹⁶O-enriched water are locked in ice sheets, leaving the ocean enriched in ¹⁸O. Foraminifera (marine microfossils) incorporate oxygen from seawater into their shells (CaCO₃) — their δ¹⁸O values record past seawater temperature and ice volume. The δ¹⁸O record from deep-sea cores is the primary proxy for reconstructing Quaternary glacial-interglacial cycles (Milankovitch cycles).

#### Carbon Isotope Ratios
¹³C/¹²C (δ¹³C) tracks biological and geochemical processes. Photosynthesis preferentially incorporates ¹²C (kinetic isotope fractionation), making organic matter isotopically light. Carbonate rocks precipitating from seawater track the dissolved inorganic carbon δ¹³C. A large negative excursion in the δ¹³C record signals a major release of isotopically light carbon (organic or biogenic) — such excursions mark the end-Permian extinction (~252 Ma) and the Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM, ~56 Ma). The PETM δ¹³C excursion is used to understand the climate response to rapid carbon input — an analog for current CO₂ rise.

#### Review Questions
1. Why do glacial periods cause marine carbonate ¹⁸O to increase (more positive δ¹⁸O)?
2. Why is organic matter (like wood or fossil fuel) isotopically lighter (more negative δ¹³C) than marine carbonates?
3. What does a large negative δ¹³C excursion in the geological record typically indicate about past environments?

## Subtopic: Biogeochemical Cycles

### The Carbon Cycle
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** carbon-cycle
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** photosynthesis · respiration · carbon reservoirs · ocean-atmosphere exchange · fossil fuel emissions · climate feedback
**Summary:** The carbon cycle describes carbon's movement through the atmosphere, oceans, land, and lithosphere — a system where human fossil fuel emissions are adding carbon far faster than natural processes can remove it.

#### Carbon Reservoirs
The major carbon reservoirs (in gigatons of carbon, GtC): lithosphere (rocks, including fossil fuels): ~100,000,000 GtC; ocean (dissolved inorganic carbon, DIC): ~38,000 GtC; terrestrial vegetation and soils: ~2,000 GtC; atmosphere (as CO₂): ~860 GtC (pre-industrial ~590 GtC). The atmosphere is small but highly connected to other reservoirs. Residence time: carbon in the atmosphere has a mean residence time of ~4–5 years (though perturbed CO₂ takes centuries to millennia to fully re-equilibrate because of slow ocean and rock weathering).

#### The Fast Carbon Cycle (Biological)
Photosynthesis: CO₂ + H₂O + light → glucose + O₂ — removes ~123 GtC/year from the atmosphere. Respiration (all organisms): glucose + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O — returns ~120 GtC/year. Net terrestrial uptake ~ 3 GtC/year currently. Ocean air-sea exchange: ~90 GtC/year absorbed, ~88 GtC/year outgassed — oceans absorb ~2.5 GtC/year net. Marine photosynthesis (phytoplankton) fixes carbon in surface waters; the biological pump exports some as sinking organic matter to the deep ocean (sequestration on timescale of centuries to millennia).

#### The Slow Carbon Cycle (Geological)
On timescales of millions of years: volcanic degassing adds CO₂; silicate weathering (Ca/Mg silicates + CO₂ → carbonates + silica) removes CO₂. The Urey reactions: CaSiO₃ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + SiO₂. This geological thermostat — the Berner-Caldeira feedback — has regulated Earth's climate over geologic time: high CO₂ → warmer → more rain → more weathering → more CO₂ drawdown → cooling. Subduction of carbonate rocks and organic matter releases CO₂ back through volcanism. Over 300 million years, burial of dead organisms produced coal and oil — concentrating carbon underground. Human burning of fossil fuels releases ~10 GtC/year — geological instantaneous but geologically unprecedented in rate.

#### Review Questions
1. List the four major carbon reservoirs from largest to smallest.
2. How do the fast and slow carbon cycles differ in timescale and dominant processes?
3. What is the geological carbon thermostat (Urey reactions / silicate weathering feedback), and how does it regulate long-term climate?

### The Nitrogen and Phosphorus Cycles
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** nitrogen-phosphorus-cycles
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** nitrogen fixation · nitrification · denitrification · phosphorus cycle · limiting nutrients · eutrophication
**Summary:** Nitrogen and phosphorus are essential nutrient elements whose cycles regulate ecosystem productivity — nitrogen through the atmosphere-soil-biosphere exchange, phosphorus through a slower geological cycle with no atmospheric reservoir.

#### The Nitrogen Cycle
Atmosphere is 78% N₂ (dinitrogen gas), which is chemically inert — most organisms cannot use it directly. Nitrogen fixation: conversion of N₂ to biologically usable NH₃ (ammonia) by: nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium in legume root nodules; free-living Azotobacter); lightning; industrial Haber-Bosch process (> half of nitrogen in human bodies comes from Haber-Bosch). Nitrification: NH₃ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ by Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter bacteria (aerobic). Assimilation: plants absorb NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻. Ammonification: dead organic matter → NH₃ (decomposers). Denitrification: NO₃⁻ → N₂O → N₂ by anaerobic bacteria (returns N to atmosphere). Human activities double natural nitrogen fixation — excess fertilizer runs off into water bodies causing eutrophication (algal blooms → oxygen depletion → dead zones; e.g., Gulf of Mexico dead zone from Mississippi River runoff).

#### The Phosphorus Cycle
Phosphorus has no significant atmospheric reservoir — it cycles only through land, water, and organisms. P is released by weathering of phosphate-bearing rocks (apatite, Ca₅(PO₄)₃(OH/F/Cl)); leaches into soil and water; assimilated by plants and phytoplankton; returned to soil/water through decomposition; eventually buried in marine sediments (uplifted over millions of years). The "missing" phosphorus is the major long-term sink. Phosphorus is often the limiting nutrient in freshwater ecosystems (excess → eutrophication). In the ocean, nitrogen and/or iron often limit productivity. Liebig's Law of the Minimum: growth is controlled by the scarcest essential resource.

### The Water Cycle
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** water-cycle-earth-systems
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** evapotranspiration · precipitation · groundwater recharge · runoff · water table · aquifer · residence time
**Summary:** The water cycle drives erosion, transports nutrients, regulates climate, and provides freshwater — with dramatically different residence times for different reservoirs from days (atmosphere) to millions of years (deep groundwater).

#### Water Cycle Components
Evaporation (ocean surface → atmosphere): solar energy drives ~500,000 km³/year. Transpiration (plants release water vapor): roughly equal to evaporation from land surfaces. Precipitation: water returns as rain, snow, sleet — ~505,000 km³/year globally (more falls on ocean than comes from it on land). Runoff → rivers → ocean. Infiltration → soil moisture → groundwater recharge → aquifers. Global water distribution: ~96.5% in oceans, ~1.7% in glaciers and ice caps, ~0.76% groundwater, ~0.001% atmosphere, ~0.013% surface freshwater (lakes, rivers).

#### Residence Times
Atmosphere: ~10 days. Rivers: ~16 days. Shallow groundwater: months to years. Deep groundwater: thousands to millions of years (fossil water). Glaciers: thousands to hundreds of thousands of years. Ocean: ~3,200 years. Residence time = reservoir volume / flux. Long residence times in groundwater aquifers mean they are effectively non-renewable on human timescales — the Ogallala Aquifer (High Plains, US) is being depleted far faster than it recharges.

### Earth System Feedbacks and Interactions
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** earth-system-feedbacks
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** positive and negative feedback · ice-albedo feedback · carbon-climate feedback · Gaia hypothesis · coupled Earth systems
**Summary:** Earth's climate and geochemical systems are coupled through feedbacks — some stabilizing (negative), some amplifying (positive) — that control the planet's long-term habitability.

#### Feedback Mechanisms
Negative feedback (stabilizing, self-correcting): silicate weathering thermostat — warming increases weathering → more CO₂ removed → cooling; Stefan-Boltzmann cooling — warmer planet radiates more IR → cools. Positive feedback (amplifying, destabilizing): ice-albedo feedback — melting ice exposes dark ocean/land → more solar absorption → more warming → more melting; water vapor feedback — warming increases atmospheric H₂O (a greenhouse gas) → more warming; methane release from permafrost — warming thaws permafrost → releases stored CH₄ → more warming. Climate sensitivity (the equilibrium temperature increase per CO₂ doubling) is ~3°C largely because positive feedbacks amplify the initial ~1°C direct forcing.

#### Earth System Coupling
The carbon cycle couples to climate (CO₂ as greenhouse gas), biology (photosynthesis, respiration), and tectonics (volcanic degassing, subduction of carbon). The water cycle couples to carbon (weathering requires water), biology (transpiration), and climate (water vapor feedback, cloud feedbacks). The rock cycle couples to biogeochemical cycles (weathering delivers nutrients to oceans; burial of organic matter sequesters carbon). The evolution of life itself modified Earth's geochemistry: the Great Oxidation Event (~2.4 Ga) fundamentally changed atmospheric chemistry when cyanobacterial photosynthesis began producing free O₂.

3. What was the Great Oxidation Event, and why was it geochemically significant?

#### Key Facts
- Nitrogen cycle key steps: fixation (N₂ → NH₃) → nitrification (NH₃ → NO₃⁻) → assimilation → ammonification → denitrification (NO₃⁻ → N₂)
- Haber-Bosch process: industrial N₂ fixation using Fe catalyst, high T and P; responsible for feeding ~half of humanity

- Phosphorus: no atmospheric reservoir; limiting nutrient in most freshwater systems
- Eutrophication: excess nutrients (N or P) → algal blooms → O₂ depletion → dead zones
- Carbon largest reservoir: lithosphere (~100 million GtC); smallest exchangeable reservoir: atmosphere (~860 GtC)
- Ice-albedo feedback: positive (amplifying); melting ice → lower albedo → more warming
- Silicate weathering: negative (stabilizing) feedback on long-term CO₂/climate
