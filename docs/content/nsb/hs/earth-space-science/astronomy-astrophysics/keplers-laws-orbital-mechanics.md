#### Ellipses and the First Law
A planet orbits the Sun in an ellipse with the Sun at one focus — not the center. This means orbital distance varies: the point closest to the Sun is perihelion, the farthest is aphelion. Earth's perihelion (~147 million km) occurs in early January; aphelion (~152 million km) in July. The eccentricity of an orbit measures its "ovalness": a circle has e = 0, Earth's orbit e ≈ 0.017 (nearly circular), while Pluto's e ≈ 0.25 (noticeably elliptical).

#### The Second Law: Equal Areas
Kepler's second law states that a line from the Sun to a planet sweeps equal areas in equal time intervals. This means planets move faster when closer to the Sun (near perihelion) and slower when farther away (near aphelion). This is a direct consequence of conservation of angular momentum — as the planet moves inward, it speeds up to keep L = mvr constant. Earth moves ~30.3 km/s at perihelion and ~29.3 km/s at aphelion.

#### The Third Law: Period-Distance Relationship
Kepler's third law states T² ∝ a³, where T is the orbital period (in years) and a is the semi-major axis (in AU). For any two planets: (T₁/T₂)² = (a₁/a₂)³. Jupiter at 5.2 AU has a period of √(5.2³) ≈ 11.9 years. Newton later derived the full form: T² = 4π²a³/(GM), linking the law to gravity. This lets astronomers calculate a planet's mass from a moon's orbital data.

#### Applying Kepler to Real Systems
Kepler's laws apply to any bound two-body system — moons around planets, exoplanets around stars, binary stars around each other. The Galilean moons of Jupiter follow the third law: Io (1.77 days, 0.0028 AU from Jupiter), Europa (3.55 days), Ganymede (7.16 days). Astronomers used deviations from Keplerian orbits to predict the existence of Neptune (1846) and to discover exoplanets via transit timing variations.

#### Historical Context and Modern Use
Tycho Brahe's precise naked-eye measurements (pre-telescope, ~1580s) gave Kepler the data to derive his laws by 1619. Kepler's laws remained empirical until Newton showed they follow from F = GMm/r². Today, mission planners use Keplerian mechanics for satellite trajectories, and the Parker Solar Probe uses gravitational assists to achieve record-close perihelion passes to the Sun.
