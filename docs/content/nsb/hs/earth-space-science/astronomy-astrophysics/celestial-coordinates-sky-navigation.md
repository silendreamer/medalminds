#### The Celestial Sphere
Astronomers project the sky onto an imaginary celestial sphere centered on Earth. Key reference points: the celestial equator (projection of Earth's equator), the celestial poles (directly above Earth's poles — Polaris sits ~0.7° from the North Celestial Pole), and the ecliptic (the Sun's apparent annual path, tilted 23.5° from the equator because of Earth's axial tilt).

#### Equatorial Coordinates: RA and Dec
Right ascension (RA) is the celestial analog of longitude, measured eastward in hours (0h to 24h, or equivalently 0° to 360°). Declination (Dec) is the celestial analog of latitude, measured in degrees from the celestial equator (–90° to +90°). The vernal equinox (where the ecliptic crosses the equator in spring) defines RA = 0h. Orion's Betelgeuse: RA ≈ 5h 55m, Dec ≈ +7°. The Sun's RA changes throughout the year as Earth orbits.

#### Horizontal Coordinates: Azimuth and Altitude
For observing from a specific location, azimuth (0°–360°, measured clockwise from north) and altitude (0°–90° above horizon) place objects in the local sky. These change constantly as Earth rotates. An object transits (crosses the meridian, reaches max altitude) when it is due south for northern hemisphere observers. The meridian is the north-south great circle passing directly overhead.

#### Parallax and Distance Measurement
Stellar parallax measures the apparent shift of nearby stars against background stars as Earth orbits the Sun. Parallax angle p (in arcseconds) gives distance: d = 1/p parsecs. One parsec ≈ 3.26 light-years. Proxima Centauri has parallax 0.77 arcseconds → distance ≈ 1.3 pc ≈ 4.2 ly. The Hipparcos satellite measured parallaxes for ~100,000 stars; Gaia has extended this to ~1 billion stars with unprecedented precision.

#### Precession and the Changing Pole Star
Earth's axis wobbles like a top on a 26,000-year cycle called precession of the equinoxes. This shifts the celestial poles in a circle: Polaris is our current North Star, but 5,000 years ago the pole star was Thuban (in Draco). Precession also causes the vernal equinox to drift, so the "Age of Aquarius" refers to when the vernal equinox precesses into that constellation. Astronomers specify the epoch of coordinates (e.g., J2000.0) to account for precession.
