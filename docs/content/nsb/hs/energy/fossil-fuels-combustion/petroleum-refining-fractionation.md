#### Crude Oil Composition and Properties
Crude oil is a complex mixture of alkanes, cycloalkanes, and aromatic hydrocarbons ranging from methane (CH₄) to compounds with 60+ carbons. The density of crude oil varies (0.8–1.0 g/cm³) depending on the proportion of heavy hydrocarbons. Sulfur content ranges from 0.1% to 5%, with sulfur-rich oils called "sour" crude—these require desulfurization to meet environmental regulations. Crude oil also contains trace metals (nickel, vanadium) and water, which must be removed before refining begins.

#### Fractional Distillation Process
Fractional distillation exploits boiling point differences to separate crude oil into fractions. Crude oil is heated to ~350°C to vaporize most components, then fed into a fractionating column—a tall tower that is hottest at the bottom (~350°C) and cooler at the top (~50°C). Hydrocarbons with low boiling points rise high in the column before condensing; those with high boiling points condense near the bottom. Each tray collects a different fraction: petroleum gas (C₁–C₄, top), gasoline/petrol (C₅–C₁₂, middle-high), kerosene (C₁₂–C₁₆, middle), diesel (C₁₆–C₂₀, middle-low), and fuel oil/bitumen (C₂₀+, bottom).

#### Industrial Separations and Product Yield
A typical crude oil refining yields ~95% useful products and ~5% residue. Gasoline comprises 30–40% by volume and is the largest refinery product in developed nations; it requires octane number improvement (see cracking). Middle distillates (kerosene, diesel) account for 20–30%. Fuel oil and bitumen (asphalt) provide heating and road-paving material. The process is energy-intensive—distillation accounts for ~3% of global electricity use—and most refineries use waste heat recovery systems to improve efficiency.

#### Desulfurization and Environmental Treatment
Sulfur in crude oil forms hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) during combustion, creating acid rain. Hydrodesulfurization removes sulfur by reacting crude fractions with hydrogen at high temperature (300–400°C) and pressure (3–17 MPa) using cobalt-molybdenum catalysts: R-S-R + H₂ → R-H + R-H + H₂S. The H₂S is recovered and converted to elemental sulfur via the Claus process (2H₂S + O₂ → 2S + 2H₂O) for use in fertilizers and chemicals. Deep desulfurization reduces sulfur to <10 ppm, meeting modern fuel standards.

#### Petroleum Chemistry and Refinery Economics
Refineries operate on slim margins (~$5/barrel profit); profitability depends on crude input cost, product slate (gasoline vs diesel demand), and energy efficiency. Environmental regulations (sulfur limits, vapor pressure control, renewable fuel blending mandates) increase operational complexity. The global refining capacity is ~100 million barrels/day, with major centers in the Middle East, Europe, and North America. As electric vehicles grow, refineries face declining gasoline demand and are shifting toward jet fuel and chemical feedstocks.
