#### Complete Combustion Equation
Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon produces carbon dioxide and water. The general equation is: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + (3n+1)/2 O₂ → n CO₂ + (n+1) H₂O. For example, octane (C₈H₁₈) combusts as: C₈H₁₈ + 12.5 O₂ → 8 CO₂ + 9 H₂O (or with whole numbers: 2 C₈H₁₈ + 25 O₂ → 16 CO₂ + 18 H₂O). The stoichiometric ratio of O₂ to fuel ensures all fuel is consumed. In reality, excess oxygen is supplied to ensure complete combustion; the air-to-fuel ratio is typically 14.7:1 by mass for gasoline in internal combustion engines.

#### Enthalpy of Combustion
The enthalpy of combustion (ΔHc) is the heat released when one mole of fuel undergoes complete combustion at standard conditions (25°C, 1 atm, products in standard states). For octane, ΔHc = −5,470 kJ/mol. This is calculated using Hess's Law: ΔHc = Σ ΔHf(products) − Σ ΔHf(reactants), where ΔHf is the standard enthalpy of formation. Alkanes have ΔHc values of 45–49 MJ/kg, making them excellent energy sources. Longer-chain alkanes release slightly more energy per unit mass due to increased C-C and C-H bond content per atom.

#### Energy Density Comparisons
Energy density is the energy content per unit mass or volume. Alkanes have specific energy density ~45 MJ/kg (comparable to diesel ~46 MJ/kg and jet fuel ~46 MJ/kg). For comparison, natural gas has ~55 MJ/kg, while coal has ~24–30 MJ/kg and wood has ~16 MJ/kg. Volumetric energy density is lower for gases—compressed natural gas (250 bar) stores ~8–10 MJ/L, while gasoline stores ~32 MJ/L. This difference explains why liquid fuels dominate transportation despite gases having higher specific energy.

#### Incomplete Combustion and Pollutants
Incomplete combustion occurs when insufficient oxygen is available or mixing is poor. Products include carbon monoxide (CO), unburned hydrocarbons (UHC), and particulates. In a fuel-rich engine (high fuel-to-oxygen ratio), CO forms: 2 CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + (2n+1) O₂ → 2n CO + 2(n+1) H₂O. CO is toxic and reduces available oxygen in the bloodstream. Particulate matter (PM) forms from incomplete pyrolysis of fuel; soot (carbon) particles are 0.01–10 μm in size and are harmful if inhaled. Catalytic converters in vehicles oxidize CO and UHC to CO₂ and water over platinum-palladium catalysts.

#### Combustion Control and Efficiency
Engine efficiency depends on achieving complete combustion with minimal excess air. Spark-ignition (gasoline) engines operate at ~25–30% efficiency; compression-ignition (diesel) engines reach ~35–40% due to higher compression ratios. Gas turbines for power generation achieve ~40–45%; combined cycle plants (gas turbine + steam turbine) reach ~55–60%. Efficiency losses occur due to heat rejection (cooling system), incomplete combustion, and mechanical friction. Advanced technologies (homogeneous charge compression ignition, lean-burn engines) push efficiency toward theoretical limits while reducing emissions.

2. Why does complete combustion release more energy than incomplete combustion, and what pollutants result from incomplete combustion?
3. Compare the specific energy density of gasoline (~45 MJ/kg) to natural gas (~55 MJ/kg). Why is gasoline still preferred for transportation despite lower energy density?
