#### What Is Fission?
Nuclear fission occurs when a heavy nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into two medium-mass daughter nuclei (fission fragments), releasing 2–3 neutrons and ~200 MeV of energy. The fissile isotopes used in reactors and weapons are uranium-235 and plutonium-239 — both require only thermal (slow) neutrons to fission reliably. Uranium-238, which makes up 99.3% of natural uranium, is not fissile (it captures neutrons without fissioning at thermal energies).

#### A Typical Fission Reaction
²³⁵U + ¹n → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3¹n + ~200 MeV

The fission products (Ba-141, Kr-92) are radioactive and decay further. The three emitted neutrons can each trigger additional fissions — the basis for a chain reaction. The energy appears as kinetic energy of the fragments (~167 MeV), prompt gamma rays (~7 MeV), and neutrons (~5 MeV); the rest appears as beta/gamma from fission product decay.

#### Fissile vs. Fertile Materials
Fissile materials (U-235, Pu-239) can sustain a chain reaction. Fertile materials (U-238, Th-232) can be converted to fissile materials by neutron capture: ²³⁸U + n → ²³⁹U → ²³⁹Np → ²³⁹Pu. Breeder reactors use this to produce more fissile fuel than they consume, potentially extending nuclear fuel supplies by orders of magnitude.

#### Energy Density
1 kg of U-235 releases ~80 TJ of thermal energy when fully fissioned — equivalent to ~3,000 tons of coal or ~17 million liters of gasoline. This extraordinary energy density (a factor of ~50 million over fossil fuels) is why nuclear power plants use small fuel loads but generate vast amounts of electricity.
