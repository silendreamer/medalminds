#### The Neutron Multiplication Factor
Each fission produces 2–3 neutrons, but not all trigger further fissions — some escape the material, some are captured by non-fissile nuclei. The multiplication factor k = (neutrons in generation n+1)/(neutrons in generation n). If k < 1: subcritical — chain reaction dies out. If k = 1: critical — self-sustaining steady reaction (reactor operation). If k > 1: supercritical — exponentially growing reaction (weapon or prompt criticality accident).

#### Critical Mass
The critical mass is the minimum mass of fissile material needed to achieve k = 1 in a bare sphere. For U-235: ~52 kg (sphere ~17 cm diameter). For Pu-239: ~10 kg (~10 cm). Critical mass depends on geometry (sphere is optimal), density (compression reduces it — implosion weapon design), and the presence of a neutron reflector (beryllium or natural uranium surrounding the core reflects escaping neutrons back in, reducing critical mass by 40–60%).

#### Prompt vs. Delayed Neutrons
Most fission neutrons are prompt (emitted within 10⁻¹⁴ s). About 0.65% of neutrons from U-235 fission are delayed — emitted from fission products seconds to minutes later. These delayed neutrons are crucial for reactor control: without them, the reactor time constant would be microseconds, making control impossible. With delayed neutrons, the effective generation time is ~0.1 s — long enough for mechanical control rods to respond.

#### Chernobyl: Prompt Criticality
The Chernobyl accident (April 26, 1986) resulted from a steam explosion and prompt criticality — the reactor briefly went supercritical with k >> 1, driven by prompt neutrons alone. Control rods could not respond fast enough. Power surged to ~30,000× normal in milliseconds, vaporizing cooling water and destroying the reactor. This is why reactor designs now emphasize negative void coefficients: steam formation (voids) should reduce reactivity, not increase it.
