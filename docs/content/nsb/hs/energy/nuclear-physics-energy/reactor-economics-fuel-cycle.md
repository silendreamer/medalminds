#### Uranium Mining and Enrichment
Natural uranium is 99.3% U-238 and only 0.7% U-235. Most reactors require 3–5% U-235 enrichment; weapons require >90% (highly enriched uranium, HEU). Enrichment uses gaseous diffusion or gas centrifuges (UF₆ gas). The US stopped using diffusion in 2013; centrifuges are now universal — they're also what Iran and North Korea use in proliferation-sensitive programs.

#### Fuel Fabrication and In-Reactor Life
Enriched UF₆ is converted to UO₂ powder, pressed into pellets, and loaded into zirconium fuel rods (~4 m long). A typical PWR has ~200 fuel assemblies, each with ~300 rods. Fuel stays in the reactor ~3–4 years, with 1/3 replaced annually. Burnup — the energy extracted per unit mass — is typically 45–60 GWd/tonne for modern fuels.

#### Spent Fuel and Waste
Spent fuel is highly radioactive (from fission products) and hot. It's stored in water pools at reactor sites for 5–10 years, then potentially moved to dry cask storage. The US has ~90,000 tonnes of spent fuel with no permanent repository. Reprocessing (used in France, UK, Russia) separates usable plutonium and uranium from waste, reducing volume but raising proliferation concerns. The Yucca Mountain site in Nevada was proposed as a permanent repository but remains politically blocked.

#### Nuclear LCOE
Levelized cost of energy (LCOE) for nuclear averages ~$90/MWh for existing plants, ~$120/MWh for new plants (US, EIA 2023). High capital costs (60–70% of LCOE) dominate; fuel costs are low. Nuclear plants have capacity factors >90% — among the highest of any energy source — and lifetimes of 40–80 years. Advanced designs (SMRs, Gen IV) aim to reduce capital costs.
