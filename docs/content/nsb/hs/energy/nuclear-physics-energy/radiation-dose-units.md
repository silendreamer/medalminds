#### Absorbed Dose: The Gray
The absorbed dose measures energy deposited per unit mass of tissue. 1 gray (Gy) = 1 joule of energy deposited per kilogram of tissue. The older unit is the rad (1 Gy = 100 rad). Absorbed dose doesn't account for biological damage — alpha particles are far more damaging per Gy than gamma rays because they deposit energy more densely along their track (higher linear energy transfer, LET).

#### Dose Equivalent: The Sievert
The sievert (Sv) accounts for biological effectiveness: H = D × Q, where D is absorbed dose in grays and Q is the radiation weighting factor. For gamma and beta: Q = 1. For neutrons: Q = 5–20 (energy-dependent). For alpha particles: Q = 20. So 1 Gy of alpha radiation = 20 Sv of dose equivalent — 20× more biologically harmful than 1 Gy of gamma. Average annual background dose: ~3 mSv. CT scan: ~10 mSv. Acute radiation syndrome threshold: ~1,000 mSv (1 Sv).

#### ALARA Principle
Radiation protection follows ALARA: As Low As Reasonably Achievable. Three principles reduce exposure: time (minimize time near source), distance (dose falls as 1/r² from a point source), and shielding (alpha stopped by paper/skin; beta by plastic/glass; gamma/X-ray by lead or thick concrete; neutrons by hydrogen-rich materials like water or polyethylene).

#### Comparing Radiation Sources
Chest X-ray: ~0.1 mSv. Transatlantic flight: ~0.08 mSv. Annual limit for radiation workers: 50 mSv. Chernobyl liquidators received doses up to 16 Gy — lethal for many. Context: the average American receives more radiation from radon in their home than from all nuclear power plants combined.
