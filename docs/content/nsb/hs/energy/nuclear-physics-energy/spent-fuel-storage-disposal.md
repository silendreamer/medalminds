#### Spent Fuel Pools
Spent fuel rods are first stored in water pools at the reactor site. Water serves triple duty: shielding gamma radiation, cooling decay heat, and providing visual inspection capability. After ~5 years, decay heat drops enough for dry storage. US reactors store ~80,000 tonnes of spent fuel in pools and casks — "nuclear waste" that is actually ~95% U-238 and contains usable Pu and U.

#### Dry Cask Storage
After pool cooling, assemblies are transferred to dry casks — thick-walled steel or concrete containers filled with inert gas. Decay heat is removed passively by convection — no pumps or power required. Casks are licensed for 40+ years. In the absence of a permanent repository, dry casks continue to accumulate at reactor sites indefinitely — the US has ~4,000 dry casks at ~75 sites.

#### Deep Geological Repository Concept
The international consensus for HLW disposal: burial 300–1,000 meters underground in stable geological formations (crystalline rock, clay, salt). Multiple barriers provide isolation: the waste form (glass or ceramic), the container (corrosion-resistant alloy), the backfill (bentonite clay, which swells to seal fractures), and the geological formation itself. Finland's Onkalo repository (in crystalline bedrock) is the world's first approved deep geological repository for HLW — construction ongoing, first waste emplacement expected ~2024.

#### Yucca Mountain Controversy
Congress approved Yucca Mountain in Nevada as the US permanent repository in 1987. DOE spent ~$15 billion developing it. The Obama administration withdrew the license application in 2010, citing political opposition from Nevada. The US currently has no approved permanent repository — creating a legal and practical crisis, as utilities have sued the government for failing to take title to spent fuel as promised.
