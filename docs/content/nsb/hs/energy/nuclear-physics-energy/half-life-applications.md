#### Medical Imaging: Technetium-99m
Technetium-99m (t½ = 6 hours) is the workhorse of nuclear medicine — used in over 40 million diagnostic procedures annually. Its short half-life means the radioactive dose clears the body within a day, minimizing patient exposure. It emits gamma rays (140 keV) detectable by a gamma camera. Tc-99m is produced by molybdenum-99 generators at hospitals, which are themselves produced in nuclear reactors.

#### PET Scans: Fluorine-18
Positron emission tomography (PET) uses fluorine-18 (t½ = 110 min) attached to glucose (FDG). Cancer cells uptake glucose rapidly; PET scans reveal tumors by detecting the 511 keV gamma pairs from positron-electron annihilation. The short half-life requires an on-site cyclotron, limiting PET to major medical centers.

#### RTGs: Power for Deep Space
Radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs) use the heat from plutonium-238 decay (t½ = 87.7 years) to generate electricity via thermoelectrics. The long half-life provides steady power for decades — essential for missions beyond Jupiter where solar panels fail. Voyager 1 (launched 1977) still operates partly on RTG power. NASA's Cassini, New Horizons, and Mars Science Laboratory (Curiosity) all used RTGs.

#### Nuclear Waste: Why Half-Life Matters
High-level nuclear waste from reactors contains fission products with varying half-lives. Short-lived isotopes (cesium-137, t½ = 30 years; strontium-90, t½ = 29 years) are the most immediately hazardous. After ~300 years (10 half-lives), their activity drops by a factor of 1,000. Long-lived actinides (plutonium-239, t½ = 24,100 years) require isolation for hundreds of thousands of years — the challenge facing the proposed Yucca Mountain repository.

#### Review Questions
1. Why is a short half-life desirable for medical diagnostic isotopes but a long half-life desirable for RTGs?
2. Calculate how long it takes for cesium-137 (t½ = 30.2 years) to decay to 1/1024 of its original activity.
3. Why does nuclear waste storage require timescales of thousands of years?
