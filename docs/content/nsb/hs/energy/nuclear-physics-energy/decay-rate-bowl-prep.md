#### High-Yield Facts
- Carbon-14 half-life: 5,730 years (used for organic material up to ~50,000 years)
- Uranium-238 half-life: 4.47 billion years (age of Earth ~4.5 Gy)
- Technetium-99m: most-used medical imaging isotope; t½ = 6 hours
- Plutonium-238: RTG fuel; t½ = 87.7 years
- Alpha particle = helium-4 nucleus (Z=2, A=4)
- Beta-minus: atomic number increases by 1, mass unchanged
- Gamma: no change in Z or A
- 1 Bq = 1 decay/second; 1 Ci = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq
- Sievert = gray × quality factor; alpha Q = 20

#### Common Bowl Toss-Up Stems
- "For 10 points, this isotope of carbon with 8 neutrons is used in radiocarbon dating…" → **Carbon-14**
- "For 10 points, a nucleus emits a helium-4 particle. This type of decay is called…" → **Alpha decay**
- "For 10 points, this unit equal to one joule per kilogram measures absorbed radiation dose…" → **Gray**
- "For 10 points, after three half-lives, what fraction of a radioactive sample remains?" → **One-eighth (1/8)**

#### Speed Math Drill
After n half-lives: fraction remaining = (1/2)ⁿ. Know your powers of 2: 2¹=2, 2²=4, 2³=8, 2⁴=16, 2⁵=32, 2⁶=64, 2⁷=128, 2⁸=256, 2⁹=512, 2¹⁰=1024. Bowl questions often give elapsed time and ask for remaining fraction — divide elapsed time by half-life, then apply (1/2)ⁿ.

#### Review Questions
1. A radioactive sample decreases to 6.25% of its original amount. How many half-lives have passed?
2. Which type of radioactive decay increases the atomic number by 1?
3. What is the quality factor for alpha particles, and why is it different from gamma rays?
