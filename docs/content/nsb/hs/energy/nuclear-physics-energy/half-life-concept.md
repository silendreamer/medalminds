#### The Half-Life Law
The half-life (t½) of a radioactive isotope is the time required for half the nuclei in a sample to decay. After one half-life, 50% remains. After two, 25%. After three, 12.5%. This follows the equation N = N₀(½)^(t/t½), where N₀ is the initial number of atoms and t is elapsed time. Equivalently: N = N₀e^(−λt), where λ = ln2/t½ ≈ 0.693/t½.

#### Half-Lives Across the Periodic Table
Half-lives span an enormous range: polonium-214 (t½ = 164 μs) to uranium-238 (t½ = 4.47 billion years). Carbon-14 has t½ = 5,730 years — perfect for dating organic materials up to ~50,000 years old. Iodine-131 (t½ = 8 days) is used in thyroid cancer treatment. Cesium-137 (t½ = 30.2 years) is a major long-term nuclear waste concern.

#### Worked Calculation
A 80 g sample of iodine-131 (t½ = 8 days) is produced. How much remains after 24 days? t/t½ = 24/8 = 3 half-lives. N = 80 × (½)³ = 80 × 1/8 = 10 g. After 24 days, 10 g of I-131 remains (the rest has decayed to xenon-131).

#### Carbon Dating
Living organisms continuously absorb carbon-14 from the atmosphere, maintaining a fixed ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio. At death, intake stops and ¹⁴C decays. By measuring the ratio in a sample versus the atmospheric standard, scientists calculate elapsed time. A sample with 25% of its original ¹⁴C has undergone two half-lives: 2 × 5,730 = 11,460 years old.

#### Limitations of Half-Life Calculations
Half-life describes the average behavior of large populations of atoms. For very small samples, statistical fluctuations become significant. Additionally, some decay series produce radioactive daughters — the sample's total radioactivity may not follow simple half-life math if daughter products accumulate.

#### Review Questions
1. A 100 g sample of a radioisotope has a half-life of 20 years. How much remains after 60 years?
2. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years. A wooden artifact has 12.5% of its original ¹⁴C. How old is it?
3. Why can't carbon dating be used for fossils millions of years old?
