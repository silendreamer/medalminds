## Subtopic: Radioactive Decay & Half-Life

### What Is Radioactive Decay?
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** radioactive-decay-intro
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** radioactive decay · unstable nuclei · spontaneous emission · activity
**Summary:** Radioactive decay is the spontaneous disintegration of an unstable atomic nucleus, releasing energy and particles in the process.

#### Why Nuclei Decay
Some nuclei have too many neutrons, too many protons, or too much internal energy to remain stable. These unstable nuclei lower their energy by emitting particles or radiation — a process called radioactive decay. Decay is spontaneous and random at the level of individual atoms; you cannot predict when any single nucleus will decay. However, for large collections of atoms, decay follows a precise statistical law.

#### Types of Radioactive Decay
Alpha decay ejects a helium-4 nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons), reducing the atomic number by 2 and mass number by 4. Beta-minus decay converts a neutron to a proton, emitting an electron and antineutrino — the atomic number increases by 1. Beta-plus decay converts a proton to a neutron, emitting a positron. Gamma decay releases high-energy photons without changing the nucleus's composition — it often follows alpha or beta decay.

#### Activity and the Becquerel
The activity of a radioactive sample is the number of decays per second, measured in becquerels (Bq): 1 Bq = 1 decay/s. The curie (Ci), an older unit, equals 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq. Activity depends on both the number of atoms present and the decay constant (λ): A = λN. As atoms decay, N decreases, so activity drops over time.

#### Decay Equations
A nuclear decay equation must conserve both mass number (A) and atomic number (Z). For uranium-238 alpha decay: ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He. For carbon-14 beta-minus decay: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e + antineutrino. Always check that the top numbers (mass) and bottom numbers (charge) balance on both sides.

#### Real-World Context
Natural radioactivity is everywhere: potassium-40 in bananas, radon-222 in basements, carbon-14 in all living things. Medical PET scans use positron-emitting isotopes (fluorine-18, t½ = 110 min). Smoke detectors use americium-241. Understanding decay types and rates is fundamental to nuclear medicine, power generation, and waste management.

#### Review Questions
1. What happens to the atomic number and mass number of a nucleus undergoing alpha decay?
2. A sample has an activity of 4,000 Bq. After two half-lives, what is its activity?
3. Why is gamma radiation the most penetrating but least ionizing form of radioactive emission?

### Half-Life: The Clock Inside Every Atom
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** half-life-concept
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** half-life · exponential decay · decay constant · N = N₀(½)^(t/t½)
**Summary:** Half-life is the time for half a radioactive sample to decay, and it enables everything from carbon dating to nuclear waste planning.

#### The Half-Life Law
The half-life (t½) of a radioactive isotope is the time required for half the nuclei in a sample to decay. After one half-life, 50% remains. After two, 25%. After three, 12.5%. This follows the equation N = N₀(½)^(t/t½), where N₀ is the initial number of atoms and t is elapsed time. Equivalently: N = N₀e^(−λt), where λ = ln2/t½ ≈ 0.693/t½.

#### Half-Lives Across the Periodic Table
Half-lives span an enormous range: polonium-214 (t½ = 164 μs) to uranium-238 (t½ = 4.47 billion years). Carbon-14 has t½ = 5,730 years — perfect for dating organic materials up to ~50,000 years old. Iodine-131 (t½ = 8 days) is used in thyroid cancer treatment. Cesium-137 (t½ = 30.2 years) is a major long-term nuclear waste concern.

#### Worked Calculation
A 80 g sample of iodine-131 (t½ = 8 days) is produced. How much remains after 24 days? t/t½ = 24/8 = 3 half-lives. N = 80 × (½)³ = 80 × 1/8 = 10 g. After 24 days, 10 g of I-131 remains (the rest has decayed to xenon-131).

#### Carbon Dating
Living organisms continuously absorb carbon-14 from the atmosphere, maintaining a fixed ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio. At death, intake stops and ¹⁴C decays. By measuring the ratio in a sample versus the atmospheric standard, scientists calculate elapsed time. A sample with 25% of its original ¹⁴C has undergone two half-lives: 2 × 5,730 = 11,460 years old.

#### Limitations of Half-Life Calculations
Half-life describes the average behavior of large populations of atoms. For very small samples, statistical fluctuations become significant. Additionally, some decay series produce radioactive daughters — the sample's total radioactivity may not follow simple half-life math if daughter products accumulate.

#### Review Questions
1. A 100 g sample of a radioisotope has a half-life of 20 years. How much remains after 60 years?
2. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years. A wooden artifact has 12.5% of its original ¹⁴C. How old is it?
3. Why can't carbon dating be used for fossils millions of years old?

### Decay Series and Secular Equilibrium
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** decay-series-equilibrium
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** decay chain · daughter nucleus · secular equilibrium · uranium series
**Summary:** Heavy radioactive nuclei decay through a chain of daughter products, eventually reaching a stable end product like lead.

#### What Is a Decay Series?
A single radioactive decay rarely produces a stable nucleus directly. Instead, heavy nuclei like uranium-238 undergo a sequence of alpha and beta decays — a decay series or decay chain — passing through many intermediate radioactive daughters before reaching stable lead-206. The uranium-238 series has 14 steps; the thorium-232 series has 10.

#### The Uranium-238 Series
U-238 (t½ = 4.47 Gy) → Th-234 → Pa-234 → U-234 → Th-230 → Ra-226 → Rn-222 → … → Pb-206 (stable). Each step releases energy. Radon-222 (t½ = 3.8 days) is a gas that can accumulate in buildings — it's the second leading cause of lung cancer in the US after smoking.

#### Secular Equilibrium
When a long-lived parent (very slow decay) produces a short-lived daughter, the daughter's activity eventually equals the parent's — this is secular equilibrium. At equilibrium, the activity of parent = activity of each daughter in the chain. This is why old uranium ores contain measurable amounts of all intermediate daughters despite their short half-lives.

#### Natural Background Radiation
Most natural background radiation comes from decay series: uranium and thorium in rocks produce radon gas; potassium-40 in soil and food; cosmic rays produce carbon-14 in the atmosphere. Average US background dose is ~3 mSv/year; radon contributes ~2 mSv. Understanding decay series is essential for assessing radiation exposure.

#### Review Questions
1. What stable element is the final product of the uranium-238 decay series?
2. Why is radon gas a health hazard even though it has a relatively short half-life?
3. Define secular equilibrium and explain when it occurs in a decay chain.

### Half-Life Applications in Energy and Medicine
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** half-life-applications
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** medical isotopes · PET scan · nuclear waste timeline · radioisotope thermoelectric generator
**Summary:** Half-life governs isotope selection for medical imaging, cancer therapy, space power systems, and nuclear waste storage decisions.

#### Medical Imaging: Technetium-99m
Technetium-99m (t½ = 6 hours) is the workhorse of nuclear medicine — used in over 40 million diagnostic procedures annually. Its short half-life means the radioactive dose clears the body within a day, minimizing patient exposure. It emits gamma rays (140 keV) detectable by a gamma camera. Tc-99m is produced by molybdenum-99 generators at hospitals, which are themselves produced in nuclear reactors.

#### PET Scans: Fluorine-18
Positron emission tomography (PET) uses fluorine-18 (t½ = 110 min) attached to glucose (FDG). Cancer cells uptake glucose rapidly; PET scans reveal tumors by detecting the 511 keV gamma pairs from positron-electron annihilation. The short half-life requires an on-site cyclotron, limiting PET to major medical centers.

#### RTGs: Power for Deep Space
Radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs) use the heat from plutonium-238 decay (t½ = 87.7 years) to generate electricity via thermoelectrics. The long half-life provides steady power for decades — essential for missions beyond Jupiter where solar panels fail. Voyager 1 (launched 1977) still operates partly on RTG power. NASA's Cassini, New Horizons, and Mars Science Laboratory (Curiosity) all used RTGs.

#### Nuclear Waste: Why Half-Life Matters
High-level nuclear waste from reactors contains fission products with varying half-lives. Short-lived isotopes (cesium-137, t½ = 30 years; strontium-90, t½ = 29 years) are the most immediately hazardous. After ~300 years (10 half-lives), their activity drops by a factor of 1,000. Long-lived actinides (plutonium-239, t½ = 24,100 years) require isolation for hundreds of thousands of years — the challenge facing the proposed Yucca Mountain repository.

#### Review Questions
1. Why is a short half-life desirable for medical diagnostic isotopes but a long half-life desirable for RTGs?
2. Calculate how long it takes for cesium-137 (t½ = 30.2 years) to decay to 1/1024 of its original activity.
3. Why does nuclear waste storage require timescales of thousands of years?

### Radiation Dose: From Grays to Sieverts
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** radiation-dose-units
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** gray · sievert · dose equivalent · radiation weighting factor · ALARA
**Summary:** Understanding radiation dose units — grays and sieverts — is essential for evaluating health risks from nuclear energy and medical procedures.

#### Absorbed Dose: The Gray
The absorbed dose measures energy deposited per unit mass of tissue. 1 gray (Gy) = 1 joule of energy deposited per kilogram of tissue. The older unit is the rad (1 Gy = 100 rad). Absorbed dose doesn't account for biological damage — alpha particles are far more damaging per Gy than gamma rays because they deposit energy more densely along their track (higher linear energy transfer, LET).

#### Dose Equivalent: The Sievert
The sievert (Sv) accounts for biological effectiveness: H = D × Q, where D is absorbed dose in grays and Q is the radiation weighting factor. For gamma and beta: Q = 1. For neutrons: Q = 5–20 (energy-dependent). For alpha particles: Q = 20. So 1 Gy of alpha radiation = 20 Sv of dose equivalent — 20× more biologically harmful than 1 Gy of gamma. Average annual background dose: ~3 mSv. CT scan: ~10 mSv. Acute radiation syndrome threshold: ~1,000 mSv (1 Sv).

#### ALARA Principle
Radiation protection follows ALARA: As Low As Reasonably Achievable. Three principles reduce exposure: time (minimize time near source), distance (dose falls as 1/r² from a point source), and shielding (alpha stopped by paper/skin; beta by plastic/glass; gamma/X-ray by lead or thick concrete; neutrons by hydrogen-rich materials like water or polyethylene).

#### Comparing Radiation Sources
Chest X-ray: ~0.1 mSv. Transatlantic flight: ~0.08 mSv. Annual limit for radiation workers: 50 mSv. Chernobyl liquidators received doses up to 16 Gy — lethal for many. Context: the average American receives more radiation from radon in their home than from all nuclear power plants combined.

#### Review Questions
1. A radiation worker receives 2 Gy of alpha radiation. What is their dose equivalent in sieverts?
2. List the three ALARA principles for reducing radiation exposure and explain the physics behind each.
3. Why is alpha radiation highly dangerous when inhaled but harmless when it strikes the outside of the skin?

## Subtopic: Nuclear Structure & Binding Energy

### Inside the Nucleus: Protons, Neutrons, and the Strong Force
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** nuclear-structure-strong-force
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** nucleon · strong nuclear force · nuclear radius · neutron-to-proton ratio
**Summary:** The strong nuclear force binds protons and neutrons together against electrostatic repulsion, and its short range governs nuclear stability.

#### Nuclear Composition
A nucleus contains protons (charge +1e) and neutrons (charge 0), collectively called nucleons. The atomic number Z gives the number of protons; the mass number A gives the total nucleons (A = Z + N, where N is neutrons). Isotopes share the same Z but differ in N. The nuclear radius scales as r = r₀A^(1/3), where r₀ ≈ 1.2 fm (femtometers, 10⁻¹⁵ m) — the nucleus is ~10⁵ times smaller than the atom.

#### The Strong Nuclear Force
Protons repel each other via the Coulomb force. The nucleus would instantly fly apart if not for the strong nuclear force — one of the four fundamental forces. The strong force is: (1) attractive between nucleons; (2) charge-independent (acts equally on proton-proton, proton-neutron, neutron-neutron); (3) short-range (effective only within ~2 fm); (4) much stronger than electrostatic repulsion at short distances. Beyond ~2 fm, the strong force vanishes and electrostatic repulsion dominates.

#### The N/Z Ratio and Stability
For light nuclei (Z < 20), stable nuclei have roughly equal numbers of protons and neutrons (N ≈ Z). As Z increases, more neutrons are needed to dilute proton-proton repulsion — the N/Z ratio rises above 1. Above Z = 82 (lead), no stable nuclei exist; all heavier nuclei are radioactive. The "valley of stability" on a nuclide chart maps all stable and unstable isotopes.

#### Nuclear Density
Nuclear density is roughly constant across all nuclei: ~2 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³. This extraordinary density — a teaspoon of nuclear matter would weigh 2 billion tons — arises because nucleons pack together at nuclear distances. This near-incompressibility is one reason the strong force saturates (each nucleon interacts only with its nearest neighbors).

#### Review Questions

2. What is the range of the strong nuclear force, and why does this limit the size of stable nuclei?
3. Calculate the nuclear radius of uranium-238 using r = r₀A^(1/3) with r₀ = 1.2 fm.

### Mass Defect and Binding Energy
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** mass-defect-binding-energy
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** mass defect · binding energy · binding energy per nucleon · E = mc²
**Summary:** The mass of a nucleus is always less than the sum of its parts — this mass defect, converted via E = mc², is the binding energy holding the nucleus together.

#### The Mass Defect
When protons and neutrons combine to form a nucleus, the total mass decreases. This missing mass is the mass defect (Δm). For helium-4: 2 protons (2 × 1.00728 u) + 2 neutrons (2 × 1.00867 u) = 4.03190 u, but the measured He-4 mass is 4.00150 u. Mass defect: Δm = 4.03190 − 4.00150 = 0.03040 u.

#### Binding Energy via E = mc²
Einstein's mass-energy equivalence: E = mc². The binding energy equals the mass defect converted to energy: Eb = Δm × 931.5 MeV/u. For He-4: Eb = 0.03040 × 931.5 = 28.3 MeV. This is the energy required to completely separate He-4 into 2 protons and 2 neutrons. It's also the energy released when those nucleons fuse.

#### Binding Energy Per Nucleon
Dividing binding energy by the number of nucleons gives binding energy per nucleon (BE/A) — a measure of nuclear stability. He-4: 28.3/4 = 7.07 MeV/nucleon. Iron-56: 8.79 MeV/nucleon — the highest of any nucleus, making it the most stable. BE/A peaks around A = 56 then slowly decreases. This graph explains why fission releases energy (splitting heavy nuclei) and fusion releases energy (combining light nuclei): both move nuclei toward iron's peak stability.

#### The Iron Peak
Iron-56 and nickel-62 sit at the peak of the binding energy per nucleon curve. Stars generate energy by fusing light elements up to iron; when an iron core forms in a massive star, fusion becomes endothermic — the star can no longer support itself against gravity, triggering a supernova. The elements heavier than iron are forged in supernovae and neutron star mergers.

#### Review Questions
1. Uranium-235 has a measured mass of 235.0439 u. The sum of its constituent protons and neutrons is 236.9590 u. Calculate the mass defect and binding energy in MeV.
2. Why does the binding energy per nucleon peak at iron-56?
3. Explain why both fission and fusion can release energy using the binding energy per nucleon curve.

### Magic Numbers and Nuclear Shell Model
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** magic-numbers-shell-model
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** magic number · nuclear shell model · doubly magic nucleus · extra stability
**Summary:** Certain nuclei with "magic numbers" of protons or neutrons are exceptionally stable — evidence for quantized energy levels inside the nucleus.

#### Magic Numbers
Nuclei with certain numbers of protons or neutrons (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, or 126) are unusually stable — they have higher binding energy, more stable isotopes, and lower neutron-capture cross sections. These are magic numbers. Helium-4 (Z=2, N=2), oxygen-16 (Z=8, N=8), calcium-40 (Z=20, N=20), and lead-208 (Z=82, N=126) are "doubly magic" — all have especially high stability.

#### Nuclear Shell Model
Analogous to the electron shell model in chemistry, nucleons fill quantized energy levels (shells) in the nucleus. A completely filled shell creates extra stability — exactly as a noble gas configuration creates chemically inert atoms. Magic numbers correspond to closed nuclear shells. Maria Goeppert Mayer and J. Hans D. Jensen developed the nuclear shell model in 1949, earning the Nobel Prize in Physics.

#### Evidence for Magic Numbers
Tin (Z=50) has 10 stable isotopes — more than any other element. Lead (Z=82) is the heaviest element with a stable isotope (Pb-208). Calcium-48 has an unusually high N/Z ratio for a stable light nucleus (N=28, another magic number). These patterns emerge naturally from the shell model.

#### Review Questions
1. List four magic numbers and give one example of a doubly magic nucleus.
2. Why are doubly magic nuclei especially stable?
3. How is the nuclear shell model analogous to the electron shell model in chemistry?

### Binding Energy Calculations in Energy Systems
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** binding-energy-calculations
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** Q-value · fission energy · fusion energy · mass table lookup
**Summary:** Calculating Q-values from mass tables quantifies the energy released or absorbed in nuclear reactions, connecting theory to reactor and weapon design.

#### Q-Value of a Nuclear Reaction
The Q-value is the energy released by a nuclear reaction: Q = (Δm_reactants − Δm_products) × 931.5 MeV/u. If Q > 0, the reaction is exothermic (releases energy). If Q < 0, the reaction is endothermic (requires energy input). Q-values from binding energy tables let engineers calculate fuel efficiency without running the reaction.

#### Fission Q-Value: Uranium-235
A typical U-235 fission: ²³⁵U + n → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3n. Approximate Q ≈ 200 MeV per fission event. Compare: burning one carbon atom releases ~4 eV. Nuclear fission releases ~50 million times more energy per atom than coal combustion — the fundamental reason nuclear fuel is so energy-dense. One kilogram of U-235 produces ~80 TJ of thermal energy.

#### Fusion Q-Value: Deuterium-Tritium
The most promising fusion reaction: ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n. Q ≈ 17.6 MeV. Deuterium is abundant (1 in 6,400 water molecules); tritium must be bred from lithium. The energy density of D-T fusion fuel is even greater than fission. To reach Q > 1 (more energy out than in), plasma must reach ~100 million °C — 6× hotter than the sun's core, where gravitational confinement replaces magnetic confinement.

#### Review Questions
1. A nuclear reaction releases 180 MeV. Convert this to joules (1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J).
2. Why does fusion require such extreme temperatures despite releasing more energy per nucleon than fission?
3. Calculate the mass defect in atomic mass units for a reaction releasing 17.6 MeV.

## Subtopic: Fission: Chain Reactions & Reactor Design

### Nuclear Fission: Splitting the Atom
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** nuclear-fission-basics
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** fission · fissile material · neutron-induced fission · fission products · energy release
**Summary:** Nuclear fission splits heavy nuclei into smaller fragments, releasing ~200 MeV per event and 3 neutrons that can trigger further fissions.

#### What Is Fission?
Nuclear fission occurs when a heavy nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into two medium-mass daughter nuclei (fission fragments), releasing 2–3 neutrons and ~200 MeV of energy. The fissile isotopes used in reactors and weapons are uranium-235 and plutonium-239 — both require only thermal (slow) neutrons to fission reliably. Uranium-238, which makes up 99.3% of natural uranium, is not fissile (it captures neutrons without fissioning at thermal energies).

#### A Typical Fission Reaction
²³⁵U + ¹n → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3¹n + ~200 MeV

The fission products (Ba-141, Kr-92) are radioactive and decay further. The three emitted neutrons can each trigger additional fissions — the basis for a chain reaction. The energy appears as kinetic energy of the fragments (~167 MeV), prompt gamma rays (~7 MeV), and neutrons (~5 MeV); the rest appears as beta/gamma from fission product decay.

#### Fissile vs. Fertile Materials
Fissile materials (U-235, Pu-239) can sustain a chain reaction. Fertile materials (U-238, Th-232) can be converted to fissile materials by neutron capture: ²³⁸U + n → ²³⁹U → ²³⁹Np → ²³⁹Pu. Breeder reactors use this to produce more fissile fuel than they consume, potentially extending nuclear fuel supplies by orders of magnitude.

#### Energy Density
1 kg of U-235 releases ~80 TJ of thermal energy when fully fissioned — equivalent to ~3,000 tons of coal or ~17 million liters of gasoline. This extraordinary energy density (a factor of ~50 million over fossil fuels) is why nuclear power plants use small fuel loads but generate vast amounts of electricity.

#### Review Questions
1. Write a balanced nuclear equation for the fission of U-235 by a thermal neutron, producing Cs-137 and Rb-96.
2. Why must uranium be enriched before use in most reactors?
3. How does U-238 become Pu-239 in a reactor, and why is this significant?

### Chain Reactions: Critical Mass and Criticality
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** chain-reaction-criticality
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** chain reaction · multiplication factor k · critical mass · subcritical · supercritical
**Summary:** A self-sustaining chain reaction requires at least one neutron from each fission to trigger another fission — described by the neutron multiplication factor k.

#### The Neutron Multiplication Factor
Each fission produces 2–3 neutrons, but not all trigger further fissions — some escape the material, some are captured by non-fissile nuclei. The multiplication factor k = (neutrons in generation n+1)/(neutrons in generation n). If k < 1: subcritical — chain reaction dies out. If k = 1: critical — self-sustaining steady reaction (reactor operation). If k > 1: supercritical — exponentially growing reaction (weapon or prompt criticality accident).

#### Critical Mass
The critical mass is the minimum mass of fissile material needed to achieve k = 1 in a bare sphere. For U-235: ~52 kg (sphere ~17 cm diameter). For Pu-239: ~10 kg (~10 cm). Critical mass depends on geometry (sphere is optimal), density (compression reduces it — implosion weapon design), and the presence of a neutron reflector (beryllium or natural uranium surrounding the core reflects escaping neutrons back in, reducing critical mass by 40–60%).

#### Prompt vs. Delayed Neutrons
Most fission neutrons are prompt (emitted within 10⁻¹⁴ s). About 0.65% of neutrons from U-235 fission are delayed — emitted from fission products seconds to minutes later. These delayed neutrons are crucial for reactor control: without them, the reactor time constant would be microseconds, making control impossible. With delayed neutrons, the effective generation time is ~0.1 s — long enough for mechanical control rods to respond.

#### Chernobyl: Prompt Criticality
The Chernobyl accident (April 26, 1986) resulted from a steam explosion and prompt criticality — the reactor briefly went supercritical with k >> 1, driven by prompt neutrons alone. Control rods could not respond fast enough. Power surged to ~30,000× normal in milliseconds, vaporizing cooling water and destroying the reactor. This is why reactor designs now emphasize negative void coefficients: steam formation (voids) should reduce reactivity, not increase it.

### Nuclear Reactor Design and Operation
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** reactor-design-operation
**Estimated time:** 15 min
**Key concepts:** moderator · control rod · coolant · pressurized water reactor · reactor generations
**Summary:** A nuclear reactor maintains a controlled chain reaction by balancing fission neutron production with neutron moderation, absorption, and removal.

#### Reactor Components
Every thermal reactor has four key systems: (1) **Fuel** — enriched UO₂ pellets in zirconium alloy cladding; (2) **Moderator** — slows fast neutrons to thermal energies for efficient U-235 fission; (3) **Control rods** — absorb neutrons (boron, hafnium, or silver-indium-cadmium alloys) to regulate power; (4) **Coolant** — removes heat for electricity generation (also often serves as moderator).

#### Pressurized Water Reactor (PWR)
PWRs are the most common reactor type worldwide (~300 of ~440 operating reactors). Light water (H₂O) serves as both moderator and coolant. Primary loop: pressurized water (~155 bar, ~325°C) circulates through the reactor core, absorbing heat without boiling. Secondary loop: steam generator transfers heat to a second water loop that produces steam to drive turbines. The pressure boundary prevents radioactive primary coolant from reaching the turbines.

#### Boiling Water Reactor (BWR)
In a BWR, water boils directly in the reactor vessel, producing steam that drives turbines. Simpler than PWR (no steam generator) but the turbines become slightly radioactive. Both PWR and BWR use light water as moderator — a negative void coefficient safety feature: if coolant boils away, moderation decreases and the reaction slows automatically.

#### CANDU Reactor: Heavy Water
Canada's CANDU (CANada Deuterium Uranium) reactor uses heavy water (D₂O) as moderator. D₂O absorbs far fewer neutrons than H₂O, allowing natural (unenriched) uranium as fuel — eliminating the need for expensive enrichment. CANDU reactors can also be refueled online without shutdown.

### Reactor Economics and the Nuclear Fuel Cycle
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** reactor-economics-fuel-cycle
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** enrichment · fuel fabrication · spent fuel · reprocessing · LCOE
**Summary:** The nuclear fuel cycle from uranium mining to waste disposal shapes the economics and proliferation risks of nuclear power.

#### Uranium Mining and Enrichment
Natural uranium is 99.3% U-238 and only 0.7% U-235. Most reactors require 3–5% U-235 enrichment; weapons require >90% (highly enriched uranium, HEU). Enrichment uses gaseous diffusion or gas centrifuges (UF₆ gas). The US stopped using diffusion in 2013; centrifuges are now universal — they're also what Iran and North Korea use in proliferation-sensitive programs.

#### Fuel Fabrication and In-Reactor Life
Enriched UF₆ is converted to UO₂ powder, pressed into pellets, and loaded into zirconium fuel rods (~4 m long). A typical PWR has ~200 fuel assemblies, each with ~300 rods. Fuel stays in the reactor ~3–4 years, with 1/3 replaced annually. Burnup — the energy extracted per unit mass — is typically 45–60 GWd/tonne for modern fuels.

#### Spent Fuel and Waste
Spent fuel is highly radioactive (from fission products) and hot. It's stored in water pools at reactor sites for 5–10 years, then potentially moved to dry cask storage. The US has ~90,000 tonnes of spent fuel with no permanent repository. Reprocessing (used in France, UK, Russia) separates usable plutonium and uranium from waste, reducing volume but raising proliferation concerns. The Yucca Mountain site in Nevada was proposed as a permanent repository but remains politically blocked.

#### Nuclear LCOE
Levelized cost of energy (LCOE) for nuclear averages ~$90/MWh for existing plants, ~$120/MWh for new plants (US, EIA 2023). High capital costs (60–70% of LCOE) dominate; fuel costs are low. Nuclear plants have capacity factors >90% — among the highest of any energy source — and lifetimes of 40–80 years. Advanced designs (SMRs, Gen IV) aim to reduce capital costs.

## Subtopic: Fusion: Plasma Physics & Confinement

### Nuclear Fusion: Power of the Stars
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** nuclear-fusion-basics
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** fusion · Coulomb barrier · plasma · deuterium-tritium reaction · Q = 17.6 MeV
**Summary:** Nuclear fusion joins light nuclei to release enormous energy but requires temperatures of ~100 million °C to overcome the Coulomb barrier.

#### Why Fusion Releases Energy
Fusion of light nuclei (below iron on the binding energy curve) releases energy because the products have higher binding energy per nucleon. The D-T reaction: ²H + ³H → ⁴He (3.5 MeV) + n (14.1 MeV), releasing 17.6 MeV total. Per kilogram of fuel, D-T fusion releases ~4× more energy than U-235 fission and ~340 million times more than coal combustion.

#### The Coulomb Barrier
Two positively charged nuclei repel each other electrostatically. To fuse, they must approach within ~1 fm (nuclear force range). At room temperature, thermal kinetic energies (~0.025 eV) are vastly insufficient to overcome the ~MeV Coulomb barrier. Fusion requires temperatures of ~100 million K (~10 keV) — where quantum tunneling through the barrier becomes significant. At these temperatures, electrons strip from atoms and matter exists as plasma.

#### The Sun's Fusion: Proton-Proton Chain
The sun fuses hydrogen to helium via the proton-proton chain: net result 4¹H → ⁴He + 2e⁺ + 2νₑ + 26.7 MeV. The sun's core temperature is ~15 million K — lower than needed for Earth-based fusion because the sun has 10²⁵ times more fuel and gravitational confinement. Even at 15 million K, each proton takes ~10 billion years on average to fuse — fusion in the sun is very slow.

#### Why D-T, Not Hydrogen?
The D-T reaction has the highest cross section (probability) at achievable temperatures (~100 million K), making it the most practical terrestrial fusion fuel. Deuterium is abundant in seawater (33 mg/L); tritium (t½ = 12.3 years) must be bred from lithium-6: ⁶Li + n → ³H + ⁴He. The world's lithium supply could fuel D-T fusion for millions of years.

### Magnetic Confinement: Tokamaks and ITER
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** tokamak-iter-confinement
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** tokamak · magnetic confinement · plasma · ITER · Q factor · ignition
**Summary:** Tokamaks use powerful magnetic fields to confine plasma at fusion temperatures, and ITER will be the first device to achieve Q > 1.

#### The Tokamak Design
A tokamak (from Russian: toroidal chamber with magnetic coils) confines plasma in a donut-shaped (toroidal) magnetic field. Two magnetic field components: (1) toroidal field from external coils; (2) poloidal field from plasma current induced by a central solenoid. The combined helical field keeps plasma from drifting out. Plasma temperatures exceed 100 million K — hotter than the sun's core — contained entirely by magnetic fields, never touching the walls.

#### ITER: The International Experiment
ITER (International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor) is under construction in Cadarache, France — the world's largest fusion experiment. Cost: ~€20 billion. Partners: EU, US, Russia, China, Japan, South Korea, India. ITER aims to produce Q = 10 (10× more fusion power than input heating power) and sustain a 500 MW plasma for 400 seconds. First plasma is expected in the late 2020s. ITER will not generate electricity — it's a proof-of-concept experiment for DEMO, the planned demonstration power plant.

#### The Lawson Criterion
For fusion to be self-sustaining (ignition), the plasma must satisfy the Lawson criterion: nτT must exceed a minimum value (~10²¹ m⁻³ · s · keV for D-T). n is plasma density, τ is energy confinement time, T is temperature. Triple product: difficult to maximize all three simultaneously. Record: JET tokamak (UK) achieved ~1.8 × 10²¹ m⁻³ · s · keV in 2022, producing 59 MJ of fusion energy — a world record.

#### JET's 2022 Record
In February 2022, the Joint European Torus (JET) in Culham, UK, produced 59 MJ of fusion energy in 5 seconds — the world record. Previous record: 21.7 MJ (JET, 1997). NIF (National Ignition Facility, Lawrence Livermore) achieved ignition (Q > 1) in December 2022 using laser-driven inertial confinement. These milestones demonstrate fusion's scientific feasibility.

### Inertial Confinement: NIF and Laser Fusion
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** inertial-confinement-nif
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** inertial confinement · NIF · laser compression · ignition · ICF
**Summary:** Inertial confinement fusion uses powerful lasers to compress and heat a tiny fuel pellet to fusion conditions, achieving ignition at NIF in 2022.

#### Inertial Confinement Fusion (ICF)
In ICF, a tiny pellet (~2 mm diameter) of frozen D-T fuel is bombarded symmetrically by powerful laser pulses. The outer layer ablates (vaporizes) explosively, driving the core inward via Newton's third law — a tiny implosion that compresses and heats the fuel to fusion conditions in nanoseconds. If symmetry is perfect, the compressed core reaches 100× the density of lead and ~100 million K, enabling fusion before the pellet flies apart (confinement by inertia).

#### The National Ignition Facility (NIF)
NIF at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory uses 192 laser beams delivering 2.05 MJ of UV laser energy — the world's largest laser. The beams enter a gold cylinder (hohlraum) that converts them to X-rays, providing more symmetric compression. In December 2022, NIF produced 3.15 MJ of fusion energy from 2.05 MJ of laser input — Q_fusion > 1, achieving scientific ignition for the first time. Note: overall efficiency (wall-plug to laser to fusion) is still far below 1 for power generation.

#### Path to ICF Power Plants
Commercial ICF power would require firing ~10–15 pellets per second (current rate: ~1 per day at NIF), much more efficient lasers (NIF lasers are only ~1% efficient), and affordable pellet fabrication. Companies like Xcimer Energy, Marvel Fusion, and Focused Energy are pursuing commercial ICF. The timeline remains decades away, but ignition at NIF was a crucial scientific proof.

## Subtopic: Radiation Types, Shielding & Safety

### Three Types of Radiation: Alpha, Beta, Gamma
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** radiation-types-properties
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** alpha particle · beta particle · gamma ray · ionizing radiation · penetration vs. ionization
**Summary:** Alpha, beta, and gamma radiation have very different penetrating powers and biological effects, driving all shielding and safety decisions.

#### Alpha Radiation
Alpha particles (⁴₂He, 2 protons + 2 neutrons) are the largest and most ionizing radiation type. They travel only 2–10 cm in air and are stopped by a sheet of paper or the outer layer of skin (~70 μm of dead cells). Despite low penetration, alpha emitters are extremely dangerous if inhaled or ingested: polonium-210 (used to assassinate Alexander Litvinenko in 2006) delivers massive doses to lung tissue. Alpha particles produce ~20 ion pairs per μm of tissue — far denser ionization than beta or gamma.

#### Beta Radiation
Beta particles (fast electrons or positrons) penetrate several meters in air and ~1 cm of tissue. Stopped by ~3 mm of aluminum or thick plastic. Beta emitters on skin can cause radiation burns; if ingested or inhaled, they irradiate internal organs. Strontium-90 (a major fission product) concentrates in bone marrow, causing leukemia. Yttrium-90 (a beta emitter) is used in targeted cancer therapy.

#### Gamma Radiation and X-Rays
Gamma rays are high-energy photons (>100 keV). They penetrate deeply — several centimeters of lead or ~1 meter of concrete reduces intensity by a factor of 10. Gamma attenuation follows I = I₀e^(−μx), where μ is the linear attenuation coefficient (material-dependent) and x is shield thickness. The half-value layer (HVL) is the thickness that halves intensity: HVL = ln2/μ. For 1 MeV gammas: HVL in lead ≈ 1 cm, in water ≈ 10 cm, in concrete ≈ 6 cm.

#### Neutron Radiation
Neutrons are uncharged and highly penetrating. They don't ionize directly but scatter off nuclei, producing recoiling protons in hydrogen-rich materials. Best shielding: hydrogen-rich materials (water, polyethylene, paraffin) that thermalize neutrons via elastic scattering, combined with a neutron absorber (boron, cadmium). Neutrons can activate non-radioactive materials, making reactor vessels and components radioactive over time.

### Shielding Design and Attenuation
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** shielding-design-attenuation
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** half-value layer · attenuation coefficient · ALARA · shielding calculation
**Summary:** Radiation shielding is designed using exponential attenuation — the half-value layer concept lets engineers calculate required shield thickness.

#### Exponential Attenuation
Gamma rays and X-rays attenuate exponentially through matter: I = I₀e^(−μx). The linear attenuation coefficient μ depends on photon energy and material. The half-value layer (HVL) is the thickness halving intensity: HVL = ln2/μ ≈ 0.693/μ. After n HVLs, intensity = I₀ × (1/2)ⁿ. For 1 MeV gammas: HVL in lead ≈ 1 cm; in concrete ≈ 6 cm; in water ≈ 10 cm.

#### Worked Shielding Problem
A Co-60 source (1.25 MeV gammas, HVL in lead = 1.1 cm) must be reduced to 1/64 of its original intensity. How thick must the lead shield be? n HVLs needed: (1/2)ⁿ = 1/64 = (1/2)⁶, so n = 6. Thickness = 6 × 1.1 cm = 6.6 cm of lead.

#### Layered Shielding
Different radiation types require different shielding materials. A mixed gamma-neutron field (as in a reactor) requires: (1) water or polyethylene to moderate and absorb neutrons; (2) boron to absorb thermal neutrons (⁶Li or ¹⁰B capture); (3) lead or steel to attenuate gamma rays. Layering order matters — gammas from neutron capture in shields must also be attenuated.

#### ALARA in Practice
At DOE facilities, ALARA is not just a principle but a regulatory requirement. Workers wear dosimeters. Shielding is designed to keep collective dose below 100 mSv/year (occupational limit). Time-distance-shielding are balanced economically: reducing worker time in high-dose areas, increasing distance from sources, adding shielding to reduce background. Remote handling tools and robots are used for high-activity operations.

## Subtopic: Nuclear Waste Management

### Categories of Nuclear Waste
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** nuclear-waste-categories
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** high-level waste · low-level waste · transuranic waste · spent fuel · waste classification
**Summary:** Nuclear waste is classified by radioactivity level and half-life, determining storage and disposal strategy for timescales from decades to millennia.

#### Low-Level Waste (LLW)
LLW includes contaminated tools, protective clothing, resins, and medical waste with relatively low radioactivity. Activity decays to safe levels within 100–300 years. LLW is compacted, packaged in drums, and buried in near-surface repositories. The US generates ~3 million cubic feet of LLW annually from reactors, hospitals, and research. LLW makes up ~90% of the volume but only ~1% of total radioactivity of all nuclear waste.

#### Intermediate-Level Waste (ILW)
ILW includes reactor components, filters, and sludges with higher radioactivity than LLW but lower than HLW. Requires shielding during handling. Typically stabilized in concrete or bitumen and buried in medium-depth repositories (tens of meters).

#### High-Level Waste (HLW)
HLW consists primarily of spent nuclear fuel and the separated liquid waste from reprocessing. It contains concentrated fission products (Cs-137, Sr-90 — short-lived but intensely radioactive) and actinides (plutonium, americium, neptunium — long-lived). HLW makes up ~3% of volume but ~95% of total radioactivity. It requires shielding, cooling, and isolation for thousands of years. Currently stored in water pools and dry casks at reactor sites.

#### Transuranic Waste (TRU)
TRU waste contains elements heavier than uranium (actinides: Pu, Am, Np, Cm) produced in reactors by neutron capture. TRU has long half-lives requiring deep geological disposal. The Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP) near Carlsbad, New Mexico, is the only operating deep geological repository in the US for TRU waste, located in a stable salt formation 655 meters underground.

**Summary:** Spent nuclear fuel moves from cooling pools to dry casks, ultimately requiring permanent geological disposal for isolation over thousands of years.

#### Spent Fuel Pools
Spent fuel rods are first stored in water pools at the reactor site. Water serves triple duty: shielding gamma radiation, cooling decay heat, and providing visual inspection capability. After ~5 years, decay heat drops enough for dry storage. US reactors store ~80,000 tonnes of spent fuel in pools and casks — "nuclear waste" that is actually ~95% U-238 and contains usable Pu and U.

#### Dry Cask Storage
After pool cooling, assemblies are transferred to dry casks — thick-walled steel or concrete containers filled with inert gas. Decay heat is removed passively by convection — no pumps or power required. Casks are licensed for 40+ years. In the absence of a permanent repository, dry casks continue to accumulate at reactor sites indefinitely — the US has ~4,000 dry casks at ~75 sites.

#### Deep Geological Repository Concept
The international consensus for HLW disposal: burial 300–1,000 meters underground in stable geological formations (crystalline rock, clay, salt). Multiple barriers provide isolation: the waste form (glass or ceramic), the container (corrosion-resistant alloy), the backfill (bentonite clay, which swells to seal fractures), and the geological formation itself. Finland's Onkalo repository (in crystalline bedrock) is the world's first approved deep geological repository for HLW — construction ongoing, first waste emplacement expected ~2024.

#### Yucca Mountain Controversy
Congress approved Yucca Mountain in Nevada as the US permanent repository in 1987. DOE spent ~$15 billion developing it. The Obama administration withdrew the license application in 2010, citing political opposition from Nevada. The US currently has no approved permanent repository — creating a legal and practical crisis, as utilities have sued the government for failing to take title to spent fuel as promised.

### Reprocessing: Closing the Fuel Cycle
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** nuclear-reprocessing
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** PUREX · reprocessing · plutonium separation · mixed oxide fuel · proliferation risk
**Summary:** Reprocessing separates usable plutonium and uranium from spent fuel, reducing waste volume but raising proliferation concerns.

#### What Is Reprocessing?
Reprocessing chemically separates spent fuel into: (1) usable materials — uranium and plutonium; (2) fission product waste. The dominant technology is PUREX (Plutonium-Uranium Reduction Extraction), using tributyl phosphate solvent in nitric acid. France reprocesses ~1,700 tonnes of spent fuel per year at La Hague; Russia and UK also reprocess. The US banned civilian reprocessing in 1977 (Carter administration, proliferation concerns); the ban was lifted in 1981 but economics prevent it.

#### Mixed Oxide (MOX) Fuel
Separated plutonium is mixed with uranium oxide to make MOX fuel, usable in standard LWRs. France uses MOX in ~20 reactors, reducing fresh uranium demand by ~15%. Japan planned widespread MOX use (Rokkasho reprocessing plant) but the program has faced repeated delays since 1997.

#### Proliferation Concerns
Separated civilian plutonium is weapons-usable — less than 10 kg can make a nuclear weapon. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) safeguards monitor civilian plutonium inventories, but perfect accounting is difficult. The US opposes reprocessing in many countries precisely because of proliferation risk. Advanced reprocessing concepts (UREX+, pyroprocessing) aim to keep plutonium mixed with other actinides — weapons-unusable without further separation.

#### Waste Volume Reduction
Reprocessing reduces the volume of HLW requiring geological disposal by ~80% (fission products are a much smaller volume than spent fuel assemblies). The remaining HLW is vitrified — immobilized in borosilicate glass — which is chemically stable for millions of years. France's HLW from 50 years of nuclear power would fit under the Eiffel Tower.

2. Why did the US ban civilian reprocessing in 1977, and what is the proliferation concern?
3. What is vitrification, and why is it used for high-level waste?

### Waste Management Bowl Prep
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** waste-management-bowl-prep
**Estimated time:** 7 min
**Key concepts:** waste categories · WIPP · Yucca Mountain · Onkalo · reprocessing
**Summary:** High-yield nuclear waste facts for Science Bowl competition.

#### High-Yield Facts
- HLW: ~3% of volume, ~95% of radioactivity; requires geological disposal
- LLW: ~90% of volume, ~1% of radioactivity; near-surface burial
- TRU waste: actinides heavier than uranium from reactor neutron capture
- WIPP: Waste Isolation Pilot Plant, Carlsbad NM; TRU waste; 655 m deep salt
- Yucca Mountain: proposed US HLW repository; politically blocked since 2010
- Onkalo (Finland): first approved deep geological repository for HLW
- Reprocessing: PUREX process; France reprocesses ~1,700 t/year
- MOX fuel: mixed plutonium-uranium oxide; used in French reactors
- Vitrification: immobilizing HLW in borosilicate glass
- Decay heat: fission products still generate heat after reactor shutdown

#### Bowl Toss-Up Stems
- "For 10 points, this New Mexico facility is the only operating deep geological repository for transuranic waste in the US…" → **WIPP (Waste Isolation Pilot Plant)**
- "For 10 points, this process immobilizes high-level radioactive waste by incorporating it into borosilicate glass…" → **Vitrification**
- "For 10 points, this chemical separation process recovers plutonium and uranium from spent nuclear fuel…" → **PUREX (reprocessing)**

#### Review Questions
1. Why does spent fuel require geological disposal timescales of thousands of years?
2. What is the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant, and what type of waste does it accept?
3. Compare the advantages and disadvantages of reprocessing spent nuclear fuel.
