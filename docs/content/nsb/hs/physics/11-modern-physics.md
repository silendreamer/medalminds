## Subtopic: Special Relativity

### Einstein's Postulates & Time Dilation
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** einstein-postulates-time-dilation
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** postulates of relativity · inertial frames · speed of light · time dilation · proper time
**Summary:** Special relativity rests on two postulates: the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames, and the speed of light is constant. This leads to time dilation and other surprising effects.

#### Postulates of Special Relativity
(1) The laws of physics are identical in all inertial (non-accelerating) frames of reference. (2) The speed of light in vacuum is the same in all inertial frames (c ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s), independent of the motion of the light source or observer. These seemingly innocent postulates lead to profound consequences: time and space are not absolute; simultaneity is relative; and mass and energy are interchangeable.

#### Relativity of Simultaneity
Two events simultaneous in one frame are not simultaneous in another frame moving relative to the first. This challenges our intuition that "now" has universal meaning. The relativity of simultaneity arises because observers in different frames must synchronize clocks differently, accounting for light travel time.

#### Time Dilation
A moving clock runs slower than a stationary clock. If a moving observer measures a time interval Δt (proper time, in their frame), a stationary observer measures Δt' = γΔt, where γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) is the Lorentz factor. As v approaches c, γ increases, and the time dilation becomes extreme. At v = 0.9c, γ ≈ 2.3, so moving clocks run about 2.3 times slower. At v = c (light), γ → ∞, and time effectively stops.

#### Worked Example: Muon Decay
Muons (subatomic particles) decay with a proper lifetime of about 2 microseconds. Cosmic-ray muons travel at v ≈ 0.99c (γ ≈ 7). In the lab frame, they live about 7 × 2 = 14 microseconds. This extended lifetime allows muons produced high in the atmosphere to reach Earth's surface before decaying—direct evidence of time dilation.

#### Review Questions
1. What are the two postulates of special relativity?
2. Explain time dilation: why does a moving clock run slow?
3. A muon has proper lifetime 2 μs and travels at 0.95c. How long does it live in the lab frame?

### Length Contraction & Relativistic Mass
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** length-contraction-relativistic-mass
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** length contraction · proper length · relativistic mass · rest mass · mass-energy
**Summary:** A moving object's length contracts in the direction of motion; its inertial mass increases with velocity.

#### Length Contraction
An object's length measured in its rest frame (proper length L₀) appears contracted in a frame where the object moves. The measured length is L = L₀/γ = L₀ √(1 − v²/c²). Contraction occurs only along the direction of motion; perpendicular dimensions are unaffected. At v = 0.95c, √(1 − v²/c²) ≈ 0.31, so lengths contract to about 31% of rest length.

#### Invariant Proper Length
While length contracts in different frames, all observers agree on the proper length L₀ (length in the object's rest frame). Similarly, all observers agree on proper time intervals (time in an object's rest frame). These invariant quantities are the true physical properties of an object.

#### Relativistic Momentum & Mass
A moving object's momentum p = γmv increases beyond the classical p = mv as v approaches c. The relativistic mass is m_rel = γm, where m is the rest mass. This increase in inertia means it takes ever-greater force to accelerate an object as it approaches c. At v = c, γ → ∞, so infinite force would be required—no massive particle can reach light speed.

#### Conservation of Energy & Momentum
In special relativity, energy and momentum are both conserved. The total energy of a particle is E = γmc². At rest (v = 0), E = mc² (rest energy). The kinetic energy is KE = (γ − 1)mc² = E − mc². As v → c, KE → ∞, confirming that no massive particle can reach light speed with finite energy.

#### Review Questions
1. A spaceship 100 m long travels at 0.9c. What length does a stationary observer measure?
2. What is the rest energy of an electron (m_e ≈ 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg)?
3. Why does the relativistic mass increase with velocity?

### E = mc² & Mass-Energy Equivalence
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** e-equals-mc-squared
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** mass-energy equivalence · rest energy · energy-mass conversion · nuclear reactions
**Summary:** Mass and energy are interchangeable according to E = mc²; a small amount of mass represents enormous energy.

#### The Mass-Energy Relation
Einstein's famous equation E = mc² states that a mass m at rest has energy equivalent of mc². The speed of light c ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s, so c² ≈ 9 × 10¹⁶ m²/s². A mere 1 kg of matter has rest energy E = 1 × (9 × 10¹⁶) ≈ 9 × 10¹⁶ joules—equivalent to the energy released by burning about 3 billion tons of coal. This enormous energy underlies nuclear reactions.

#### Nuclear Reactions & Binding Energy
In nuclear fusion (combining light nuclei) and fission (splitting heavy nuclei), a small fraction of mass converts to energy. For example, in deuteron-tritium fusion (d + t → ⁴He + n), about 0.3% of the reactant mass converts to kinetic energy of products and neutrons. Over timescales of billions of years, this mass-to-energy conversion powers the Sun. In a nuclear bomb, this energy release is catastrophically fast.

#### Antimatter-Matter Annihilation
When a particle meets its antiparticle (e.g., electron and positron), they annihilate, converting their entire rest masses to energy: e⁻ + e⁺ → 2γ (gamma rays). The released energy is E = (m_e + m_e)c² ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻¹³ joules per pair—a large amount for such tiny masses. Antimatter would be an ideal fuel if it could be contained and controlled.

#### Worked Example: Nuclear Binding Energy
The helium-4 nucleus (⁴He) has a mass slightly less than the sum of its components (2 protons + 2 neutrons). The mass defect is Δm ≈ 0.0305 u (atomic mass units; 1 u ≈ 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg). The binding energy is BE = Δm c² ≈ (0.0305 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷) × (9 × 10¹⁶) ≈ 4.6 × 10⁻¹² joules, or about 28.3 MeV (million electron volts).

#### Review Questions
1. What is the rest energy of a 1 gram mass?
2. Explain how fusion releases energy.
3. What is the energy released in e⁻ + e⁺ annihilation?

### Relativistic Kinematics & Dynamics
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** relativistic-kinematics-dynamics
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** relativistic equations of motion · force & acceleration · energy-momentum relation · four-vectors
**Summary:** Classical mechanics must be modified at high speeds; relativistic dynamics accounts for the dependence of mass on velocity.

#### Relativistic Force & Acceleration
Force is still F = dp/dt, but now p = γmv. As v approaches c, dp/dv increases, so the acceleration a = dv/dt for a given force decreases. A constant force no longer produces constant acceleration; instead, acceleration asymptotically approaches zero as v → c. This is consistent with the impossibility of reaching light speed: any finite force produces continuously smaller accelerations at high speeds.

#### Energy-Momentum Relation
A powerful relation connects energy and momentum: E² = (pc)² + (mc²)². At rest (p = 0), E = mc². For a highly relativistic particle (v ≈ c), pc ≈ E, so E ≈ pc. This relation is the same for all observers, even if they measure different E and p values (in their respective frames).

#### Doppler Effect for Light
The relativistic Doppler effect for light is f' = f√[(1 + β)/(1 − β)], where β = v/c and v is the relative velocity (positive for approach, negative for recession). This differs from the classical sound wave Doppler formula and accounts for time dilation. It predicts: (1) approach (v > 0) increases frequency (blueshift), (2) recession (v < 0) decreases frequency (redshift), and (3) the shift depends only on relative velocity, not on the medium.

#### Lorentz Transformations
The Lorentz transformations relate spatial and temporal coordinates between inertial frames. A spacetime event (x, t) in one frame relates to (x', t') in another by: x' = γ(x − vt), t' = γ(t − vx/c²). These transformations preserve the speed of light invariant: if light travels c in one frame, it travels c in all frames. They reduce to Galilean transformations (x' = x − vt, t' = t) in the limit v << c.

#### Review Questions
1. Why does acceleration decrease at high speeds for a constant force?
2. Derive the energy-momentum relation E² = (pc)² + (mc²)².
3. A light source approaches at 0.9c. By what factor is frequency shifted?

### Relativistic Paradoxes & Applications
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** relativistic-paradoxes-applications
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** twin paradox · Lorentz contraction paradox · time machines · GPS relativity
**Summary:** Special relativity resolves apparent paradoxes through careful analysis of reference frames and proper understanding of relativity principles.

#### The Twin Paradox
One twin stays on Earth; the other travels in a spaceship at high speed and returns. The traveling twin experiences less proper time (time dilation) and ages less than the Earth-bound twin. This seems paradoxical because relativity treats all inertial frames equally—why doesn't the Earth-bound twin age less from the traveling twin's perspective? The resolution: the traveling twin's frame is not inertial (it accelerates to turn around), breaking the symmetry. When accounting for acceleration, the traveling twin indeed ages less. Time dilation is real and has been measured for high-speed particles and orbiting clocks (atomic clocks on airplanes age slightly less).

#### Length Contraction Paradox
A contracted moving rod seems to fit into a shorter stationary garage that is normally shorter than the rod. The paradox asks: does the rod fit or not? Resolution: "fitting" requires simultaneity. In the rod's frame, the rod is full length and the garage is contracted—the rod does not fit. In the garage's frame, the rod is contracted and does fit. Both viewpoints are correct in their respective frames; simultaneity of the rod's ends entering and exiting the garage is frame-dependent.

#### GPS & Relativity
GPS satellites move at high speed and orbit at high altitude (weaker gravity). Special relativity (satellite speed → time dilation, running slow) and general relativity (weak gravity → time dilation, running fast) affect satellite clock rates by about 38 microseconds per day. Without relativistic corrections, GPS position errors would accumulate at ~10 meters per day—rendering the system useless. Modern GPS relies crucially on relativity.

#### High-Energy Particle Colliders
In particle accelerators, particles reach speeds with γ > 1000. Time dilation dramatically extends particle lifetimes. A muon with 2 μs proper lifetime, traveling at γ = 1000, lives 2000 microseconds (2 ms) in the lab frame—long enough to traverse kilometers of detector. Similarly, length contraction affects how detectors observe fast particles.

#### Review Questions
1. For 10 points: Explain the twin paradox and its resolution.
2. How do relativistic effects affect GPS satellites?
3. Why do high-energy particles live longer in the lab frame?

## Subtopic: Photoelectric Effect & Wave-Particle Duality

### The Photoelectric Effect
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** photoelectric-effect
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** photon energy · work function · stopping potential · Einstein's photoelectric equation
**Summary:** When light strikes a metal, it can eject electrons; the kinetic energy of ejected electrons depends on the light's frequency, not its intensity.

#### Classical Problem & Quantum Solution
Classical physics predicted that increasing light intensity (more energy) should increase the kinetic energy of ejected electrons and lower the frequency threshold for ejection. Experiments contradicted this: increasing intensity increases ejection rate but not electron kinetic energy; the frequency threshold depends on the metal and is independent of intensity. Einstein's explanation: light consists of particles (photons) with energy E = hf, where h ≈ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is Planck's constant. A single photon ejects a single electron; more intense light means more photons, not higher photon energy.

#### Einstein's Photoelectric Equation
The energy balance for photoelectric ejection is: hf = W + KE_max, where W is the work function (energy to remove an electron) and KE_max is the maximum kinetic energy of ejected electrons. Rearranging: KE_max = hf − W = h(f − f₀), where f₀ = W/h is the threshold frequency. For frequencies below f₀, no electrons are ejected regardless of intensity. For frequencies above, KE_max increases linearly with frequency (not intensity).

#### Stopping Potential
A retarding electric potential (stopping potential V_s) can stop even the fastest ejected electrons: eV_s = KE_max = hf − W. Measuring V_s for different frequencies determines both h and W. Plotting V_s vs. f yields a straight line with slope h/e (allowing Planck's constant to be measured) and x-intercept f₀ (the threshold frequency).

#### Applications: Photomultipliers & Image Sensors
Photomultipliers use the photoelectric effect to detect light: photons eject electrons, which are accelerated and strike a dynode, ejecting more electrons (cascade amplification). Image sensors in cameras use the photoelectric effect: photons eject electrons from pixels, creating electrical signals that form images.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the stopping potential for electrons ejected by 400 nm light from a metal with work function 2.3 eV?
2. Explain why the photoelectric effect contradicts classical physics.
3. What is the threshold frequency for a metal with work function 3 eV?

### Wave-Particle Duality of Light
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** wave-particle-duality-light
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** photon · de Broglie wavelength · wave-particle behavior · double-slit experiment · complementarity
**Summary:** Light exhibits both wave and particle properties; the de Broglie relation connects wavelength to momentum.

#### Photon Properties
A photon (particle of light) has: energy E = hf = ℏω (ℏ = h/(2π)), momentum p = E/c = hf/c = h/λ, and travels at speed c. The de Broglie wavelength is λ = h/p. Unlike massive particles, photons are massless; their momentum is purely relativistic. A photon can be thought of as a packet of electromagnetic waves or as a quantum of light.

#### Double-Slit Experiment with Light
In the double-slit experiment, light passes through two slits and interferes at a screen, creating an interference pattern (many bright and dark fringes). This demonstrates wave behavior. However, if detectors are placed at the slits to determine which slit each photon passes through, the interference pattern disappears and a simple two-slit diffraction pattern appears. The act of measurement forces the photon to choose a slit, destroying interference—demonstrating particle-like behavior.

#### Complementarity
Niels Bohr's principle of complementarity states that particle and wave aspects of quantum objects are mutually exclusive—you cannot simultaneously measure both. If you measure which slit a photon passes through (particle aspect), you destroy the interference pattern (wave aspect). If you allow interference (wave aspect), you cannot determine which slit the photon used (particle aspect). This is a fundamental feature of quantum mechanics, not a limitation of measurement technology.

#### Practical Consequences
The wave-particle duality limits optical resolution. Diffraction effects (wave behavior) prevent focusing light into arbitrarily small spots. The best resolution achievable is roughly the wavelength: δx ~ λ/2. This is why X-ray and electron microscopes are needed for atomic-scale imaging (shorter wavelength = better resolution).

#### Review Questions
1. A photon has frequency 5 × 10¹⁴ Hz. What is its energy and momentum?
2. What is the de Broglie wavelength of an electron moving at 10⁷ m/s?
3. Explain why the double-slit experiment demonstrates wave-particle duality.

### Compton Scattering & X-Rays
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** compton-scattering-xrays
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** Compton effect · photon scattering · X-ray production · photon momentum transfer
**Summary:** When a photon scatters off an electron, both energy and momentum are conserved; the scattered photon has lower energy (longer wavelength).

#### Compton Scattering
When a high-energy photon (typically X-ray) scatters off an electron at rest, the photon transfers momentum to the electron, recoils to lower energy (longer wavelength), and the electron recoils (gains kinetic energy). Energy and momentum are both conserved. The Compton scattering formula relates the initial wavelength λ, final wavelength λ', and scattering angle θ: λ' − λ = (h/(m_e c))(1 − cos θ). The quantity h/(m_e c) ≈ 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m is the Compton wavelength of the electron.

#### Physical Interpretation
Compton scattering provides direct evidence that photons carry momentum (p = h/λ). Classical wave theory predicts that scattered light should have the same wavelength as incident light (resonant scattering by oscillating charges). The observed wavelength shift uniquely confirms photon momentum. The shift is maximum at 180° (backscattering), where λ' − λ = 2h/(m_e c) ≈ 4.86 × 10⁻¹² m.

#### X-Ray Production
X-rays are produced by accelerating electrons and letting them decelerate rapidly (bremsstrahlung, or "braking radiation"). When a high-speed electron decelerates, it radiates electromagnetic energy as X-rays. A more efficient method is to accelerate electrons into a target metal; inner-shell electrons are knocked out, and higher-shell electrons fall down, emitting characteristic X-rays (specific wavelengths for each metal).

#### Applications & Medical Use
X-rays penetrate soft tissue but are absorbed by dense materials (bone, metals). This allows medical imaging: X-ray radiography creates shadow images of internal structures. CT (computed tomography) scans use multiple X-ray angles and computer reconstruction. The photoelectric effect and Compton scattering are the primary X-ray absorption mechanisms in biological tissue.

#### Review Questions
1. A 0.5 Å X-ray scatters at 45°. What is the final wavelength?
2. Explain the Compton effect and why it proves photon momentum.
3. How are X-rays produced in medical imaging equipment?

### Quantum Nature of Light Competition
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** quantum-light-competition
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** high-yield photon facts · energy-momentum · practical scenarios · elegant solutions
**Summary:** Competition problems on light often test deep understanding of E=hf, p=h/λ, and the photoelectric effect.

#### Classic Photoelectric Problem
"Light of wavelength 300 nm shines on a metal with work function 3.6 eV. What is the maximum kinetic energy of ejected electrons?" Solution: Photon energy E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3 × 10⁸)/(300 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ 6.63 × 10⁻¹⁹ J ≈ 4.14 eV. KE_max = 4.14 − 3.6 = 0.54 eV.

#### Compton Scattering Calculation
"A 1 MeV photon scatters 180° (backscatters) off an electron. What is the electron's kinetic energy?" Solution: The Compton shift at 180° is Δλ = 2h/(m_e c) ≈ 4.85 × 10⁻¹² m. For the initial photon, λ = hc/E = (1240 eV·nm)/(1 × 10⁶ eV) ≈ 1.24 × 10⁻³ nm. After scattering, λ' = λ + Δλ ≈ 1.24 × 10⁻³ nm (shift is tiny, but significant). The scattered photon's energy is E' = hc/λ', and the electron receives KE = E − E'.

#### Photon Momentum in Radiation Pressure
"A 1000 W laser (1 μm wavelength) shines on a perfectly absorbing surface. What is the radiation pressure?" Solution: Photon momentum p = h/λ. Number of photons per second: N = (Power)/(Energy per photon) = P/(hf) = Pλ/(hc). Force on surface: F = (dp/dt) = N × p = [Pλ/(hc)] × (h/λ) = P/c. Pressure P_rad = F/Area = I/c, where I is intensity. For I = Power/Area = 1000/A, pressure depends on beam size.

#### Threshold Problems
"For what wavelength does a given metal's photoelectric threshold occur?" Solution: At threshold, f = f₀, so λ₀ = c/f₀ = c/(W/h) = hc/W. If W = 2 eV, then λ₀ = (1240 eV·nm)/(2 eV) = 620 nm (red light).

#### Review Questions
1. For 10 points: Calculate the kinetic energy of electrons ejected by 250 nm light from a 2 eV work function metal.
2. What is the pressure from a 500 W laser on a perfectly absorbing surface?
3. Why is the photoelectric effect a "quantum" phenomenon rather than "classical"?

## Subtopic: Atomic Models & Quantum Theory

### Bohr Model of the Hydrogen Atom
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** bohr-model-hydrogen
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** quantized energy levels · Bohr radius · orbital angular momentum · spectral lines · transitions
**Summary:** Bohr's model of hydrogen atom proposes quantized electron orbits and energy levels; transitions between levels emit or absorb photons.

#### Postulates of Bohr Model
(1) Electrons orbit the nucleus in circular orbits with quantized angular momentum: L = nℏ = n(h/2π), where n = 1, 2, 3, ... (principal quantum number). (2) Electrons do not continuously radiate energy despite acceleration, defying classical electromagnetism. (3) Energy is released or absorbed when electrons transition between allowed orbits. Despite its conceptual inconsistencies, Bohr's model correctly predicts hydrogen's spectrum and energy levels.

#### Quantization Conditions
For a hydrogen atom with electron mass m_e, electron charge e, and nucleus charge Ze (Z = 1 for hydrogen), Bohr's quantization conditions yield the Bohr radius: a₀ = 4πε₀ℏ²/(m_e e²) ≈ 0.53 Å (5.3 × 10⁻¹¹ m). The energy levels are E_n = −Z²(13.6 eV)/n². For n = 1 (ground state), E₁ = −13.6 eV. For n = 2, E₂ = −3.4 eV. The energy difference for a transition from n = 2 to n = 1 is ΔE = −3.4 − (−13.6) = 10.2 eV, corresponding to a photon of frequency f = 10.2 eV / h ≈ 2.5 × 10¹⁵ Hz.

#### Spectral Lines
When an electron transitions from level n_i to n_f, the emitted photon's frequency is f = (Z²13.6 eV)(1/n_f² − 1/n_i²)/h. The Lyman series (n_f = 1) occurs in the ultraviolet; the Balmer series (n_f = 2) occurs in the visible; the Paschen series (n_f = 3) occurs in the infrared. These series are seen in hydrogen's emission spectrum and absorption spectrum. Hydrogen's line spectrum provided early evidence for quantization.

#### Limitations of Bohr Model
The Bohr model works only for hydrogen (single electron). For helium (two electrons) and heavier atoms, electron-electron repulsion and quantum effects make classical orbits invalid. The model does not explain why certain transitions are allowed and others forbidden. Modern quantum mechanics (using wave functions and the Schrödinger equation) supersedes Bohr's model but keeps its central insight: energy levels are quantized.

#### Review Questions
1. What are the main postulates of Bohr's model?
2. What is the Bohr radius, and what does it represent?
3. Calculate the wavelength of light emitted in the n = 3 to n = 2 transition of hydrogen.

### Quantum Mechanics & Schrödinger Equation
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** quantum-mechanics-schrodinger
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** wave function · probability · Schrödinger equation · uncertainty principle · quantum states
**Summary:** Quantum mechanics describes particles as wave functions; the Schrödinger equation governs how they evolve.

#### Wave Function & Probability
In quantum mechanics, an electron's state is described by a wave function ψ(x, t). The wave function is not directly observable; instead, |ψ(x)|² gives the probability density of finding the electron at position x. The electron doesn't have a definite position until measured; the wave function represents all possible positions weighted by probability. The wave function must normalize: ∫|ψ|² dx = 1 (probability of finding it somewhere is 1).

#### Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation
For a particle in a potential V(x), the time-independent Schrödinger equation is: −(ℏ²/2m)(d²ψ/dx²) + V(x)ψ = Eψ. This is an eigenvalue equation: solutions ψ and E exist only for specific (quantized) values of E. These allowed energies are the system's quantum levels. Solving Schrödinger's equation for a hydrogen atom reproduces Bohr's energy levels without assuming classical orbits.

#### Quantum Numbers & Orbitals
Hydrogen's wave functions are labeled by quantum numbers (n, l, m_l): n (principal, determines energy), l (orbital angular momentum quantum number, l = 0, 1, ..., n − 1), and m_l (magnetic quantum number, m_l = −l, ..., 0, ..., l). Each set of quantum numbers defines an orbital (a spatial probability distribution for the electron). The s orbital (l = 0) is spherical; p orbitals (l = 1) are dumbbell-shaped; d orbitals (l = 2) are cloverleaf-shaped.

#### Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle
Heisenberg's uncertainty principle states that Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2, where Δx is the uncertainty in position and Δp is the uncertainty in momentum. The more precisely you know position, the less precisely you know momentum, and vice versa. This is not a measurement limitation but a fundamental quantum property: particles don't have well-defined positions and momenta simultaneously. For an electron confined to atomic dimensions (Δx ~ 10⁻¹⁰ m), the uncertainty in momentum is huge (Δp ~ 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s), meaning high uncertainty in velocity.

#### Review Questions
1. What is a wave function, and how does it relate to probability?
2. State Heisenberg's uncertainty principle and explain its significance.
3. What are the quantum numbers for hydrogen's 2p orbital?

### Atomic Structure & the Periodic Table
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** atomic-structure-periodic-table
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** electron configuration · Pauli exclusion principle · shielding · effective nuclear charge · periodic trends
**Summary:** The periodic table arises from quantum rules for electron configuration; similar chemistry occurs for elements in the same group.

#### Pauli Exclusion Principle
No two electrons in an atom can have identical quantum numbers. Each orbital (defined by n, l, m_l) can hold at most two electrons (differing in spin m_s = ±½). This principle determines how electrons fill orbitals, explaining the structure of the periodic table. Helium (two electrons) fills the 1s orbital; lithium (three electrons) starts the second shell; and so on.

#### Electron Configuration & Aufbau Principle
Electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy according to the aufbau principle: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 3d, 4s, ... The periodic table's structure reflects this filling: the s-block (Groups 1–2) fills s orbitals; the p-block (Groups 13–18) fills p orbitals; the d-block (transition metals) fills d orbitals. Valence electrons (outermost shell) determine chemical properties, explaining why elements in the same group (same valence configuration) have similar chemistry.

#### Shielding & Effective Nuclear Charge
Inner electrons shield outer electrons from the full nuclear charge. An outer electron experiences an effective nuclear charge Z_eff < Z. The degree of shielding depends on how close the inner electrons are and their number. Electrons in the same shell shield each other less effectively than inner electrons. Across a period, Z_eff increases as nuclear charge increases while shielding remains roughly constant—causing atomic radius to decrease.

#### Periodic Trends
Atomic radius decreases left to right across a period (increasing Z_eff) and increases down a group (more electron shells). Ionization energy (energy to remove an electron) increases left to right and increases up a group. Electronegativity (tendency to attract electrons in a bond) increases left to right and increases up a group. These trends follow from Z_eff and electron configuration.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the Pauli exclusion principle, and how does it explain the periodic table?
2. Why is atomic radius larger for atoms farther down a group?
3. Explain shielding and effective nuclear charge.

### Spectroscopy & Atomic Transitions
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** spectroscopy-atomic-transitions
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** emission spectra · absorption spectra · selection rules · fine structure · hyperfine structure
**Summary:** Spectral lines arise from electronic transitions between quantum states; line patterns reveal atomic structure.

#### Selection Rules
Not all transitions are allowed. Electric dipole transitions must obey selection rules: Δl = ±1 and Δs = 0 (spin doesn't change). These rules arise from the interaction of the electric field (from the photon) with the electron's orbital and spin angular momenta. A transition between two s orbitals (Δl = 0) is forbidden and has extremely low probability. Selection rules explain why some spectral lines are absent or very dim.

#### Fine Structure
Higher-resolution spectroscopy reveals that spectral lines split into closely spaced components. This fine structure arises from spin-orbit coupling: the electron's spin magnetic moment interacts with the magnetic field produced by its orbital motion. This coupling causes small energy shifts depending on the relative orientations of spin and orbital angular momentum. Spectral lines split into multiplets reflecting these different couplings.

#### Hyperfine Structure
Even finer structure results from the nuclear spin's interaction with the electron's magnetic field (hyperfine coupling). This produces small energy splittings that can be resolved only with high-precision spectroscopy. The 21 cm line of hydrogen (used in radio astronomy) results from a hyperfine transition between parallel and antiparallel spins of the electron and nucleus.

#### Doppler Broadening & Natural Line Width
Thermal motion of atoms causes Doppler shifts, broadening spectral lines. Hotter gases have broader lines due to larger velocity distributions. Additionally, excited states have finite lifetimes (due to spontaneous emission), which according to the uncertainty principle limits the precision to which energy (and hence frequency) can be defined, causing an intrinsic broadening called natural line width.

#### Review Questions
1. State the selection rules for electric dipole transitions.
2. Explain fine structure in atomic spectra.
3. What causes Doppler broadening of spectral lines?

## Subtopic: Nuclear Physics

### Nuclear Structure & Radioactivity
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** nuclear-structure-radioactivity
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** nucleons · binding energy · mass defect · radioactive decay · activity
**Summary:** The nucleus consists of protons and neutrons held together by the strong force; unstable nuclei decay through radioactive processes.

#### Nuclear Composition & Stability
A nucleus contains Z protons and N neutrons (total mass number A = Z + N). The nuclear diameter is roughly 10⁻¹⁵ m (1 femtometer). Despite the electrostatic repulsion between protons, nuclei are stable due to the strong nuclear force, which is stronger than electromagnetism at nuclear distances but falls off rapidly. Nuclei with the "right" neutron-to-proton ratio are stable; those with too many or too few neutrons are unstable and decay.

#### Binding Energy & Mass Defect
The mass of a nucleus is less than the sum of its nucleon masses—the mass defect Δm = Z·m_p + N·m_n − M_nucleus. This difference represents the binding energy: BE = Δm · c². Nucleons gain binding energy when assembled; conversely, breaking apart nuclei requires energy. The binding energy per nucleon (BE/A) is largest for iron-56 (~8.8 MeV/nucleon), making it the most stable nucleus. Fusion of light nuclei or fission of heavy nuclei release energy because the products have higher BE/A.

#### Radioactive Decay Modes
(1) Alpha decay: nucleus emits helium-4 (α particle), decreasing A by 4, Z by 2. (2) Beta-minus (β⁻) decay: neutron converts to proton, emitting electron (e⁻) and antineutrino (ν̄_e), increasing Z by 1. (3) Beta-plus (β⁺) decay: proton converts to neutron, emitting positron (e⁺) and neutrino (ν_e), decreasing Z by 1. (4) Gamma decay: nucleus emits high-energy photon (γ ray), decreasing energy but not changing A or Z. Different nuclei decay preferentially by different modes depending on their structure.

#### Activity & Half-Life
The activity (A) is the decay rate: A = λN, where λ is the decay constant and N is the number of nuclei. Activity has units of becquerels (Bq = decays/second) or curies (Ci ≈ 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq). The number of nuclei decays exponentially: N(t) = N₀ e^(−λt). The half-life (t₁/₂) is the time for half the nuclei to decay: t₁/₂ = ln(2)/λ ≈ 0.693/λ.

### Fission & Fusion
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** fission-fusion
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** nuclear fission · chain reaction · critical mass · nuclear fusion · stellar nucleosynthesis
**Summary:** Fission splits heavy nuclei (releasing energy); fusion combines light nuclei (releasing energy). Both are mechanisms for nuclear energy generation.

#### Fission Basics
When a heavy nucleus (e.g., uranium-235) absorbs a neutron, it can fission into two smaller nuclei plus 2–3 free neutrons and energy (~200 MeV per fission). The products have higher binding energy per nucleon than the original nucleus, releasing energy. Fission is used in nuclear reactors and bombs. Unlike radioactive decay (spontaneous), fission requires an incoming neutron trigger.

#### Chain Reaction & Critical Mass
The free neutrons from one fission can trigger additional fissions, creating an exponential chain reaction if the reaction conditions are right. In a reactor, control rods absorb neutrons, maintaining a steady reaction rate. In a bomb, the reaction rate is uncontrolled, releasing enormous energy suddenly. The critical mass is the minimum amount of fissile material needed to sustain a chain reaction; it depends on the material's properties and geometry.

#### Fusion Basics
Fusion occurs when two light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy. The Sun's energy comes from fusion of hydrogen into helium via the proton-proton chain (pp-chain). A deuterium-tritium fusion (d + t → ⁴He + n) releases 17.6 MeV. Fusion requires extremely high temperatures (millions of kelvins) to overcome the Coulomb barrier (electrostatic repulsion between nuclei). Fusion is the basis for hydrogen bombs and is being pursued for controlled, clean energy generation (ITER tokamak project).

#### Stellar Nucleosynthesis
Fusion in stellar cores creates heavier nuclei: hydrogen fuses to helium, helium to carbon, and so on up to iron. Iron fusion is endothermic (requires energy), so massive stars end their lives in supernovae when iron accumulates. Supernovae create even heavier elements and spread them through space, enriching the interstellar medium. All nuclei heavier than lithium were created in stars or supernovae.

#### Worked Example: Energy from Fission
U-235 + n → Ba-141 + Kr-92 + 3n (rough fission). Using E = Δm · c² with typical mass defect Δm ≈ 0.1 u ≈ 1.66 × 10⁻²⁸ kg: E ≈ (1.66 × 10⁻²⁸) × (3 × 10⁸)² ≈ 1.5 × 10⁻¹¹ J ≈ 93 MeV (in the right ballpark for 200 MeV per fission after accounting for multiple fragments).

### Nuclear Decay & Radiation Hazards
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** nuclear-decay-radiation
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** activity · dose · radiation types · shielding · biological effects
**Summary:** Radioactive materials emit harmful radiation; understanding decay rates and radiation properties is crucial for safety.

#### Measuring Radioactive Activity
Activity (A = λN) is measured in becquerels (Bq) or curies (Ci). For radiometric dating, the fraction of a radioisotope remaining after time t is N(t)/N₀ = e^(−λt) = (½)^(t/t₁/₂). Carbon-14 dating: ¹⁴C has t₁/₂ = 5730 years; living organisms maintain constant ¹⁴C through food chains, but once dead, ¹⁴C decays. Measuring the ¹⁴C activity in ancient artifacts determines their age (valid up to ~50,000 years).

#### Radiation Types & Shielding
- Alpha particles (⁴He): stopped by paper or skin, but dangerous if inhaled or ingested (internal exposure).
- Beta particles (electrons): stopped by aluminum foil or plastic; more penetrating than alpha.
- Gamma rays (high-energy photons): penetrate deeply, stopped by lead or thick concrete. Shielding thickness increases exponentially: each "half-value layer" reduces intensity by half. No shielding is 100% effective; reducing exposure relies on distance (intensity ∝ 1/r²) and time (less time = less dose).

#### Biological Damage
Ionizing radiation damages cells by creating ions and free radicals. At low doses, cells repair damage and survive; at high doses, cells die (useful for killing cancer, but harmful to normal tissue). Very high doses cause acute radiation syndrome (nausea, vomiting, hair loss, death). Long-term effects include increased cancer risk and genetic damage. Dose is measured in gray (Gy) or rem (roentgen equivalent man), accounting for radiation type and biological effect.

#### Natural Background Radiation
Everyone receives ~2–3 mSv/year from cosmic rays, radon, and natural radioisotopes (potassium-40, radium). Higher altitudes have more cosmic rays; radon-rich regions have higher internal exposure. Human tolerance for radiation is roughly 500 mSv acute dose (lethal) or 100 mSv annual dose (safe limit). Medical X-rays and CT scans deliver ~0.01–10 mSv per procedure, well below dangerous levels for occasional procedures.

#### Nuclear Reaction Balancing
"Plutonium-239 alpha decays. Write the reaction and identify the product." Solution: ²³⁹Pu → ⁴He + X. Conservation of mass number: 239 = 4 + A_X, so A_X = 235. Conservation of atomic number: 94 = 2 + Z_X, so Z_X = 92. Product is ²³⁵U. Reaction: ²³⁹Pu → ⁴He + ²³⁵U.

#### Half-Life Calculation
"A sample of iodine-131 (t₁/₂ = 8 days) starts with 1000 Bq activity. After 24 days (three half-lives), what is the activity?" Solution: After 24 days = 3 × t₁/₂, the activity is (½)³ × 1000 = 125 Bq.

#### Binding Energy Problem
"Calculate the binding energy of carbon-12 (mass = 12.000 u, 6 protons, 6 neutrons)." Solution: Mass defect = 6 × m_p + 6 × m_n − 12.000 u = 6 × 1.00728 + 6 × 1.00866 − 12.000 ≈ 6.04368 + 6.05196 − 12.000 = 0.09564 u. BE = 0.09564 u × 931.5 MeV/u ≈ 89.1 MeV. BE/A ≈ 7.4 MeV/nucleon (reasonable for C-12).

#### Decay Chain Problem
"Uranium-238 decays via alpha emission to thorium-234, which decays via beta emission to protactinium-234. Write both reactions." Solution: ²³⁸U → ⁴He + ²³⁴Th and ²³⁴Th → e⁻ + ν̄_e + ²³⁴Pa.
