#### What Inertia Really Means
Inertia is the resistance of any object to a change in its state of motion. It is not a force — it is a property of mass. A bowling ball has more inertia than a tennis ball because it has more mass, so it requires more force to accelerate it and more force to stop it. Galileo first recognized this principle by imagining frictionless surfaces; Newton formalized it as his First Law: an object remains in its current state of motion (at rest or moving at constant velocity) unless a *net* external force acts on it.

#### Net Force and Equilibrium
When all forces acting on an object sum to zero — ΣF = 0 — the object is in translational equilibrium. Equilibrium does NOT mean no forces act; it means forces cancel. A book resting on a table has gravity pulling it down (mg) and the normal force pushing it up (N = mg), so ΣF = 0 and it stays put. A car cruising at constant velocity on a highway is also in equilibrium: engine thrust forward equals air drag and rolling friction backward.

#### Dynamic vs. Static Situations
First Law applies in two scenarios: (1) *static equilibrium* — object is at rest; (2) *dynamic equilibrium* — object moves at constant velocity. Both require ΣF = 0. The critical question when analyzing any situation is: "Is there a net force, or do forces balance?" If they balance, use First Law reasoning. If there is a net force, you need Second Law (F = ma).

#### Inertia in Real Life
Seat belts work because of inertia. When a car decelerates suddenly, your body tends to continue moving forward (First Law). The seat belt provides the net backward force needed to decelerate you along with the car. Similarly, when a magician pulls a tablecloth quickly, dishes stay approximately in place because the brief friction force doesn't significantly change their velocity — inertia keeps them nearly stationary.

#### Frame of Reference
Newton's Laws hold in *inertial reference frames* — frames that are not accelerating. A rotating carousel is non-inertial; standing on it, you feel a "centrifugal" push outward that has no force source. In a non-inertial frame, fictitious forces appear. For Science Bowl purposes, assume inertial frames unless told otherwise.

#### Review Questions
1. A 10 kg box sits on a table. What is the net force on the box, and what does this tell you about its motion?
2. A hockey puck slides across frictionless ice at 5 m/s. What will happen to its speed if no net force acts on it?
3. Why is it harder to stop a loaded freight train than an empty one traveling at the same speed?
