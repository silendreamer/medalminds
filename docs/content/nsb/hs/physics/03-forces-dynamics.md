## Subtopic: Newton's Laws of Motion

### Newton's First Law: Inertia and Equilibrium
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** newtons-first-law-inertia
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** inertia · net force · equilibrium
**Summary:** Newton's First Law explains why objects at rest stay at rest and objects in motion stay in motion unless acted on by a net external force.

#### What Inertia Really Means
Inertia is the resistance of any object to a change in its state of motion. It is not a force — it is a property of mass. A bowling ball has more inertia than a tennis ball because it has more mass, so it requires more force to accelerate it and more force to stop it. Galileo first recognized this principle by imagining frictionless surfaces; Newton formalized it as his First Law: an object remains in its current state of motion (at rest or moving at constant velocity) unless a *net* external force acts on it.

#### Net Force and Equilibrium
When all forces acting on an object sum to zero — ΣF = 0 — the object is in translational equilibrium. Equilibrium does NOT mean no forces act; it means forces cancel. A book resting on a table has gravity pulling it down (mg) and the normal force pushing it up (N = mg), so ΣF = 0 and it stays put. A car cruising at constant velocity on a highway is also in equilibrium: engine thrust forward equals air drag and rolling friction backward.

#### Dynamic vs. Static Situations
First Law applies in two scenarios: (1) *static equilibrium* — object is at rest; (2) *dynamic equilibrium* — object moves at constant velocity. Both require ΣF = 0. The critical question when analyzing any situation is: "Is there a net force, or do forces balance?" If they balance, use First Law reasoning. If there is a net force, you need Second Law (F = ma).

#### Inertia in Real Life
Seat belts work because of inertia. When a car decelerates suddenly, your body tends to continue moving forward (First Law). The seat belt provides the net backward force needed to decelerate you along with the car. Similarly, when a magician pulls a tablecloth quickly, dishes stay approximately in place because the brief friction force doesn't significantly change their velocity — inertia keeps them nearly stationary.

#### Frame of Reference
Newton's Laws hold in *inertial reference frames* — frames that are not accelerating. A rotating carousel is non-inertial; standing on it, you feel a "centrifugal" push outward that has no force source. In a non-inertial frame, fictitious forces appear. For Science Bowl purposes, assume inertial frames unless told otherwise.

#### Review Questions
1. A 10 kg box sits on a table. What is the net force on the box, and what does this tell you about its motion?
2. A hockey puck slides across frictionless ice at 5 m/s. What will happen to its speed if no net force acts on it?
3. Why is it harder to stop a loaded freight train than an empty one traveling at the same speed?

### Newton's Second Law: Force, Mass, and Acceleration
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** newtons-second-law-fma
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** net force · mass · acceleration · ΣF = ma
**Summary:** Newton's Second Law quantifies how a net force changes an object's motion: the acceleration produced is proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass.

#### The Core Equation
**ΣF = ma** — the net force (vector sum of all forces) equals mass times acceleration. Acceleration is in the *same direction* as the net force. Key implications: (1) double the net force → double the acceleration; (2) double the mass → half the acceleration for the same force. Units: force in Newtons (N), where 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

#### Applying the Law: Strategy
Standard approach — (1) identify all forces on the object; (2) choose a coordinate system; (3) write ΣFx = max and ΣFy = may separately; (4) solve. Forces must be treated as vectors — they have direction. A 10 N force right and a 6 N force left give ΣF = 4 N right, not 16 N.

#### The Weight Equation
Weight is the gravitational force on a mass: **W = mg**, where g = 9.8 m/s² near Earth's surface. A 5 kg object weighs 5 × 9.8 = 49 N. Weight and mass are different: mass is intrinsic (doesn't change), weight depends on gravitational field. On the Moon (g = 1.6 m/s²), a 5 kg object still has mass 5 kg but weighs only 8 N.

#### Multi-Force Example
A 3 kg box is pushed right with 20 N of applied force. Friction acts left with 8 N. Find acceleration: ΣF = 20 − 8 = 12 N right. a = ΣF/m = 12/3 = 4 m/s² right.

#### Superposition of Forces
When multiple forces act simultaneously, they add as vectors. ΣF is the vector sum. Components: ΣFx = F1x + F2x + …, ΣFy = F1y + F2y + … Treat x and y directions independently. Only after finding ΣFx and ΣFy do you compute the magnitude: |ΣF| = √(ΣFx² + ΣFy²).

#### Review Questions
1. A 2 kg object has forces 15 N east and 9 N west acting on it. Find its acceleration (magnitude and direction).
2. What net force is required to accelerate a 1200 kg car from rest to 20 m/s in 5 seconds?
3. On the Moon (g = 1.6 m/s²), what is the weight of a 60 kg astronaut? What is the astronaut's mass?

### Newton's Third Law: Action-Reaction Pairs
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** newtons-third-law-action-reaction
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** action-reaction pair · equal and opposite forces · force pairs on different objects
**Summary:** Newton's Third Law states that every force has an equal and opposite reaction force acting on a *different* object.

#### The Precise Statement
"For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction." More precisely: if object A exerts force F on object B, then object B exerts force −F on object A. The two forces are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and act on *different objects*. This is crucial — the two forces in a Third Law pair can never cancel each other because they act on different objects.

#### Why Third Law Pairs Don't Cancel
Students often ask: "If the floor pushes up on me with the same force I push down, why do I accelerate?" Answer: they act on *different* bodies. Your weight pulls Earth up toward you (yes — you exert a gravitational pull on Earth), and Earth's gravity pulls you down. The pair acts on you and Earth, not on you alone. When analyzing YOUR motion, you only include forces ON you.

#### Identifying Third Law Pairs
A Third Law pair: same type of force, same magnitude, opposite direction, one force on each of two objects. Example: You push a wall right (30 N). Wall pushes you left (30 N). That's the pair. The wall's normal force on you and the floor's friction on you are NOT a Third Law pair — they're different types and act on the same object (you).

#### Propulsion Applications
Rocket engines work by Newton's Third Law. Hot exhaust gases are expelled backward; the rocket is pushed forward. A swimmer pushes water backward with their hand; water pushes the swimmer forward. Walking: your foot pushes backward on the ground; the ground pushes your foot forward. In every propulsion scenario, ask: what are you pushing against, and what pushes you?

#### Review Questions
1. A 60 kg skater pushes off a wall with 180 N of force. What force does the wall exert on the skater, and in which direction?
2. Earth pulls a falling apple downward with force F. What is the reaction force, and on what object does it act?
3. A horse pulls a cart forward with 500 N. The cart pulls the horse backward with 500 N. Why does the system accelerate forward?

### Applying Newton's Laws: Block-and-Pulley Systems
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** newtons-laws-block-pulley
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** tension · Atwood machine · system of equations · constraint equations
**Summary:** Block-and-pulley problems use Newton's Second Law for each object in a system, with tension linking connected objects.

#### Solving Connected-Object Problems
When blocks are connected by a string over a pulley: (1) define positive direction consistently for each block; (2) write ΣF = ma for each block separately; (3) recognize that string tension T is the same throughout (massless string, frictionless pulley); (4) both blocks have the same magnitude of acceleration (inextensible string). This gives two equations with two unknowns (T and a).

#### Worked Example: Atwood Machine
Two blocks hang from a massless pulley: m₁ = 3 kg, m₂ = 5 kg. Find acceleration and tension.
- For m₂ (heavier, goes down positive): m₂g − T = m₂a → 49 − T = 5a
- For m₁ (lighter, goes up positive): T − m₁g = m₁a → T − 29.4 = 3a
- Add equations: 49 − 29.4 = 8a → 19.6 = 8a → a = 2.45 m/s²
- Tension: T = 29.4 + 3(2.45) = 36.75 N

#### Horizontal + Vertical System
Block A (4 kg) on a frictionless table, connected by string over edge to block B (2 kg) hanging. Positive: A moves right, B moves down.
- Block A: T = 4a
- Block B: 2(9.8) − T = 2a → 19.6 − T = 2a
- Adding: 19.6 = 6a → a = 3.27 m/s²; T = 4(3.27) = 13.1 N

#### What Tension Means Physically
Tension is the pull force transmitted through the string. It is NOT the weight of the hanging block — in an accelerating system, T < m_hanging × g. Only in equilibrium (a = 0) does T = mg for a hanging mass. A common exam trap: assuming T equals weight even when the system accelerates.

#### Review Questions
1. In an Atwood machine, m₁ = 6 kg and m₂ = 4 kg. Calculate the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string.
2. A 5 kg block on a frictionless table is connected to a 3 kg hanging block. What is the acceleration of the system?
3. If the pulley in an Atwood machine has friction, how would that affect the system's acceleration compared to the frictionless case?

### Newton's Laws Bowl Blitz
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** newtons-laws-bowl-blitz
**Estimated time:** 7 min
**Key concepts:** toss-up recognition · formula recall · common traps
**Summary:** High-frequency Newton's Laws patterns that appear in Science Bowl toss-ups.

#### Toss-Up Pattern Recognition
Bowl questions often open with: "This law states that…" or "For 10 points, what quantity equals mass times acceleration?" Practice instantaneous recognition of: First Law → inertia/equilibrium; Second Law → ΣF = ma; Third Law → action-reaction on *different* objects. When you hear "equal and opposite," lock in Third Law.

#### High-Yield Facts
- 1 Newton = 1 kg·m/s² (unit definition)
- Weight = mg (g = 9.8 m/s² or 10 m/s² for quick estimates)
- In equilibrium: ΣF = 0, NOT that no forces act
- Third Law pairs: same type, same magnitude, opposite direction, on different objects
- Atwood machine: a = (m₂−m₁)g/(m₁+m₂); T = 2m₁m₂g/(m₁+m₂)

#### Common Traps
- "Net force is zero, so no forces act" — WRONG. Zero net force means forces balance.
- "The reaction force to gravity is the normal force" — WRONG. Normal is a contact force (different type). Reaction to Earth's gravity on you is your gravity on Earth.
- Forgetting that in an accelerating system, string tension ≠ hanging weight.

#### Sample Toss-Up Stems
- "For 10 points, what law states that an object at rest remains at rest unless acted on by a net external force?"
- "For 10 points, name the property of matter that describes its resistance to changes in motion."
- "For 10 points, two blocks connected by a string over a pulley — what name is given to this system?"

#### Review Questions
1. State Newton's Three Laws in one sentence each.
2. In an Atwood machine with m₁ = 2 kg and m₂ = 2 kg, what is the acceleration of the system?
3. A 70 kg person stands in an elevator accelerating upward at 2 m/s². What does the scale read?

### Elevator Problems and Apparent Weight
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** elevator-apparent-weight
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** apparent weight · normal force · accelerating reference frame
**Summary:** Elevator problems apply Newton's Second Law to find normal force, which equals what a scale reads — called apparent weight.

#### Apparent Weight Defined
A scale reads the normal force N it exerts on you, not your true weight mg. In an accelerating system, N ≠ mg. Applying ΣF = ma vertically (positive up): N − mg = ma → **N = m(g + a)**. Positive a (accelerating up) → N > mg → you feel heavier. Negative a (accelerating down) → N < mg → you feel lighter.

#### Four Elevator Scenarios
1. **At rest or constant velocity**: a = 0 → N = mg. Scale reads true weight.
2. **Accelerating upward**: a > 0 → N = m(g + a) > mg. Feel heavier.
3. **Decelerating going up (or accelerating down)**: a < 0 → N = m(g − a) < mg. Feel lighter.
4. **Free fall (a = −g)**: N = m(g − g) = 0. Weightlessness — not zero gravity, zero contact force.

#### Worked Problem
A 60 kg person in an elevator. Scale reads 720 N. Find acceleration.
N − mg = ma → 720 − 60(9.8) = 60a → 720 − 588 = 60a → 132 = 60a → a = 2.2 m/s² upward.

#### Synthesis: Newton's Laws Integration
This problem uses all three laws: First Law tells us the person moves with the elevator (same acceleration); Second Law gives ΣF = ma; Third Law explains why the scale pushes up on the person with the same force the person pushes down on the scale.

#### Review Questions
1. A 50 kg person stands in an elevator accelerating downward at 3 m/s². What does the scale read?
2. The elevator cable breaks. What does the scale read, and why?
3. In which scenario is apparent weight greater than true weight: accelerating up, moving at constant velocity upward, or decelerating while moving downward?

### Third Law Deep Dive: Tension and Normal Force Pairs
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** third-law-tension-normal-pairs
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** Third Law pairs · tension pair · normal force pair · contact forces
**Summary:** Practice correctly identifying Third Law pairs for tension, normal force, and gravity across multi-object systems.

#### Identifying Pairs Systematically
For every force on object A from object B, there is a Third Law partner: the force on B from A. Method: state the force as "A pulls/pushes B with force F." The pair is "B pulls/pushes A with force F." Type, magnitude, and direction are dictated by the original force.

#### Three-Body System
Boxes A, B, C sit in a line on a frictionless surface. A hand pushes A, which pushes B, which pushes C.
- Hand on A (right, F_hand) → A on hand (left, F_hand)
- A on B (contact, right) → B on A (contact, left)
- B on C (contact, right) → C on B (contact, left)
Each interface has a Third Law pair. The forces on each box are not Third Law pairs with each other.

#### Common Confusion: Tension
For a string connecting two blocks: the string pulls Block 1 right (tension T) and pulls Block 2 left (tension T). Are these a Third Law pair? **No** — they act on different ends of the *string* (not the blocks), and only if the string is massless are both T. The actual Third Law pairs involve block-string interfaces: Block 1 pulls string left with T, string pulls Block 1 right with T.

#### Review Questions
1. A book sits on a table. List all Third Law pairs involving the book, table, and Earth.
2. Two ice skaters push off each other. Skater A (50 kg) accelerates at 3 m/s². Skater B has mass 75 kg. Find B's acceleration.
3. In the three-box problem above, if the applied force is 30 N and each box is 2 kg, find the contact force between B and C.

### Solving Multi-Force Problems with Components
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** multi-force-component-problems
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** vector components · simultaneous equations · two-dimensional force analysis
**Summary:** Multi-force problems in 2D require resolving all forces into x and y components, then applying ΣFx = max and ΣFy = may separately.

#### Why Components Work
Forces are vectors. The x-components and y-components are independent — a horizontal force doesn't affect vertical acceleration and vice versa (absent coupling through the geometry). Decompose every force: Fx = F·cosθ, Fy = F·sinθ. Then ΣFx = max and ΣFy = may are two independent equations.

#### Worked Example: Two Ropes at Angles
A 20 kg sign hangs from two ropes. Rope 1 makes 30° with horizontal, Rope 2 makes 60° with horizontal. Sign is in equilibrium (a = 0).
- x: T₁cos30° − T₂cos60° = 0 → T₁(0.866) = T₂(0.5)
- y: T₁sin30° + T₂sin60° − 196 = 0 → 0.5T₁ + 0.866T₂ = 196
- From x equation: T₁ = 0.577T₂. Substituting: 0.5(0.577T₂) + 0.866T₂ = 196 → 1.155T₂ = 196 → T₂ = 169.7 N; T₁ = 97.97 N.

#### Setting Up the Strategy
(1) Draw free body diagram. (2) Choose coordinate axes (align one axis with acceleration if possible). (3) Resolve all forces. (4) Write ΣF = ma in each direction. (5) Solve the system of equations. (6) Check signs and units.

#### Review Questions
1. A traffic light (mass 15 kg) hangs from two cables, each at 45° from horizontal. Find the tension in each cable.
2. Two horizontal forces act on a 5 kg object: 30 N at 0° and 40 N at 90°. Find the magnitude and direction of the net force and the resulting acceleration.
3. A 10 kg box is pulled at 37° above horizontal with 80 N. The floor exerts a normal force and 20 N of friction. Find the acceleration of the box.

## Subtopic: Free-Body Diagrams

### Drawing Free-Body Diagrams
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** drawing-free-body-diagrams
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** free-body diagram · force identification · isolated object
**Summary:** A free-body diagram (FBD) isolates a single object and shows all external forces acting on it as labeled vectors.

#### What Belongs in an FBD
An FBD shows ONE object (drawn as a dot or simple shape) with arrows representing every external force acting ON that object. Forces the object exerts on OTHER objects are NOT included. Common forces: weight (W = mg, downward from center of mass), normal force (N, perpendicular to contact surface), friction (f, parallel to surface opposing motion), tension (T, along rope toward the rope), applied force (F_app, in direction of push/pull), air drag (opposing velocity).

#### Rules for Drawing FBDs
1. Isolate the object — mentally remove it from its surroundings.
2. Identify every surface/object in contact with your object → each gives at least one force (normal, possibly friction).
3. Include gravity on every object with mass.
4. Include any ropes, springs, or fields.
5. Draw arrows from the object (or center), scaled approximately to magnitude.
6. Label each arrow clearly (W, N, f, T, F_app, etc.).

#### Common Mistakes
- Including forces the object exerts on others (Third Law partner forces).
- Forgetting gravity (every massive object has weight).
- Forgetting normal force when the object is on a surface.
- Drawing velocity or acceleration as if they were forces (they are NOT).
- Including internal forces (for a system, you'd need to isolate each piece separately).

#### FBD for Complex Situations
For a block on an inclined plane: (1) gravity (mg, straight down); (2) normal force (perpendicular to incline surface); (3) friction (parallel to incline, opposing motion). When you tilt your coordinate system to align with the incline, mg must be resolved: mg·sinθ down the incline, mg·cosθ into the surface.

#### Review Questions
1. Draw and label the FBD for a book sitting on a table.
2. Draw the FBD for a box being pulled up a ramp by a rope (include friction), then list the forces by name.
3. A ball swings in a horizontal circle on a string. What forces act on it? What is the direction of the net force?

### Using FBDs to Solve Problems
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** fbds-problem-solving
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** FBD · equation of motion · solving for unknowns
**Summary:** FBDs are problem-solving tools: draw the diagram, translate each force into component equations, and solve for unknowns.

#### The Five-Step Method
(1) Draw FBD for every object of interest. (2) Choose coordinate axes (positive directions). (3) Write ΣFx = max and ΣFy = may from the FBD. (4) List knowns and unknowns. (5) Solve algebraically, then substitute numbers.

#### Worked Example: Box on Floor
A 10 kg box: applied force 60 N at 37° above horizontal, kinetic friction 25 N. Find acceleration.
- Fx: 60cos37° − 25 = 10a → 60(0.8) − 25 = 10a → 48 − 25 = 10a → a = 2.3 m/s²
- Fy: N + 60sin37° − mg = 0 → N = 98 − 60(0.6) = 98 − 36 = 62 N.

#### Worked Example: Hanging Sign
A 5 kg sign hangs from one vertical chain. FBD: tension T upward, weight 49 N downward. Equilibrium: T − 49 = 0 → T = 49 N. Now the sign hangs from two chains, each vertical: each T = 24.5 N by symmetry.

#### Review Questions
1. A 15 kg crate slides across a floor. Applied force is 80 N horizontal, friction is 45 N. Draw the FBD and find acceleration.
2. A stoplight (mass 12 kg) hangs from a single vertical wire. Draw the FBD and find the wire tension.
3. A 20 kg block is pushed against a wall by a horizontal force of 150 N. The wall exerts friction upward. Draw the FBD and find the minimum friction force needed to prevent the block from sliding down.

### FBD Synthesis: Systems and Constraints
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** fbd-systems-constraints
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** system FBD · constraint · connected bodies
**Summary:** For connected objects, draw separate FBDs for each and use constraint equations linking their accelerations.

#### System vs. Individual FBDs
When objects are connected (string, rod), you can draw a *system FBD* to find total acceleration (treating the whole as one object). Then draw individual FBDs to find internal forces (like tension). Both approaches must give the same acceleration.

#### Practice Problem Set
Three 2 kg blocks on a frictionless surface connected by strings, pushed by 24 N.
- System: F = (m₁+m₂+m₃)a → 24 = 6a → a = 4 m/s²
- Tension between block 1 and 2 (T₁₂): 24 − T₁₂ = m₁a → 24 − T₁₂ = 8 → T₁₂ = 16 N
- Tension between block 2 and 3 (T₂₃): T₂₃ = m₃a = 2(4) = 8 N

#### Review Questions
1. Draw FBDs for each block in the three-block system above. Verify that ΣF = ma for each.
2. Two blocks (3 kg and 5 kg) on a frictionless surface are pushed by a 32 N force. Find the contact force between them.
3. A 4 kg block rests on top of a 6 kg block. Both accelerate at 2 m/s² to the right. Find the friction force the bottom block exerts on the top block.

## Subtopic: Friction, Normal Force & Inclined Planes

### Static and Kinetic Friction
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** static-kinetic-friction
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** static friction · kinetic friction · coefficient of friction · normal force
**Summary:** Friction is a contact force parallel to surfaces; static friction prevents motion up to a maximum, while kinetic friction acts during sliding.

#### The Two Types of Friction
**Static friction (fs)**: acts when surfaces are *not* sliding relative to each other. It adjusts to match the applied force up to a maximum: fs ≤ μsN. When applied force exceeds μsN, the object starts sliding. **Kinetic friction (fk)**: acts while surfaces slide. It is constant: fk = μkN. Critically: μs > μk, so it's harder to start sliding than to keep sliding.

#### Coefficients of Friction
μs and μk are dimensionless numbers that depend on the *pair* of surfaces in contact (not on speed, area of contact, or mass). Rubber on dry pavement: μs ≈ 0.8; ice on ice: μs ≈ 0.1. The coefficients multiply the *normal force* N, not the weight (they're equal only on level surfaces).

#### Normal Force and Its Relationship to Friction
Friction = μ × N. If you push a crate harder into the floor (increasing N), friction increases. If you push the crate at an upward angle, the vertical component reduces N, which reduces friction — that's why tilting a handle upward makes it easier to push something. On a horizontal surface: N = mg. On an incline or with a tilted applied force: N ≠ mg.

#### Finding the Coefficient Experimentally
Place block on surface. Gradually increase applied force F until block just slides. At that moment, fs,max = F → μs = F/N = F/mg (on level surface). Measure sliding force to find μk.

#### Review Questions
1. A 10 kg box has μs = 0.5 and μk = 0.35. (a) What is the maximum static friction? (b) Once sliding, what is the kinetic friction?
2. You push down at 30° on a 5 kg box. Applied force is 40 N. Find the normal force and maximum static friction (μs = 0.4).
3. Why does kinetic friction remain approximately constant regardless of sliding speed (for moderate speeds)?

### Inclined Planes: Force Analysis
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** inclined-planes-force-analysis
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** inclined plane · component resolution · angle of incline
**Summary:** Inclined plane problems resolve gravity into components parallel and perpendicular to the surface, then apply Newton's Second Law along and normal to the incline.

#### Tilted Coordinate System
The key trick: align coordinate axes WITH the incline. Let x point down the slope, y point perpendicular out of the surface. Now resolve gravity:
- mg·sinθ → along the slope (down the incline, positive x direction)
- mg·cosθ → perpendicular to slope (into the surface, negative y direction)

Normal force N acts in the positive y direction. On a frictionless surface: ΣFy = 0 → N = mg·cosθ. Net force along slope: ΣFx = mg·sinθ → a = g·sinθ (frictionless incline).

#### With Friction
Friction opposes motion: if block slides down, friction acts up the incline.
- ΣFy = 0 → N = mg·cosθ
- fk = μkN = μkmg·cosθ
- ΣFx = mg·sinθ − fk = ma → a = g(sinθ − μkcosθ)

The block slides if mg·sinθ > μsmg·cosθ, i.e., tanθ > μs. The angle of incline where the block just starts to slide: θ = arctan(μs).

#### Pushed Up the Incline
If an applied force F pushes the block up the incline, friction now acts down (opposing upward motion):
- ΣFx = F − mg·sinθ − μkmg·cosθ = ma

#### Worked Example
A 5 kg block on a 30° incline, μk = 0.2. Find acceleration (sliding down).
- N = 5(9.8)cos30° = 42.4 N
- fk = 0.2(42.4) = 8.48 N (up slope)
- ΣF = 5(9.8)sin30° − 8.48 = 24.5 − 8.48 = 16.02 N
- a = 16.02/5 = 3.2 m/s² (down slope)

#### Review Questions
1. A 3 kg block slides down a frictionless 45° ramp. What is its acceleration?
2. A 6 kg block is on a 25° incline with μs = 0.3. Does the block slide? (Compare mg·sinθ with μs·mg·cosθ)
3. A 4 kg block is pushed up a 20° incline by a 40 N force along the surface. μk = 0.25. Find the acceleration.

### Friction Application: Stopping Distance and Skidding
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** friction-stopping-distance
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** stopping distance · kinematic equations · friction deceleration
**Summary:** Combine friction force with kinematics to find stopping distances, skid marks, and braking forces.

#### Deceleration from Friction
When a car brakes with locked wheels (skidding), kinetic friction decelerates the car: f = μkN = μkmg → a = −μkg. Using kinematics (v² = v₀² + 2aΔx) with final velocity 0: 0 = v₀² − 2μkgΔx → **Δx = v₀²/(2μkg)**. Doubling speed quadruples stopping distance — critical safety implication.

#### Worked Problem
A 1200 kg car traveling at 25 m/s brakes with μk = 0.7. Find stopping distance.
- a = −μkg = −0.7(9.8) = −6.86 m/s²
- Δx = v₀²/(2|a|) = 625/(2 × 6.86) = 625/13.72 = 45.5 m

#### Anti-Lock Brakes (ABS)
ABS prevents wheels from fully locking by maintaining controlled slipping. Since μs > μk, a rolling-but-decelerating wheel has more friction force than a fully skidding wheel — so ABS actually gives *shorter* stopping distances and maintains steering control.

#### Review Questions
1. A car moving at 30 m/s stops in 60 m. What is the coefficient of kinetic friction between tires and road?
2. If a car's initial speed doubles, by what factor does stopping distance increase (same friction)?
3. A 10 kg crate slides across a warehouse floor (μk = 0.3) and decelerates. It enters the frictionless section at 6 m/s. How far does it travel on the frictionless section before... (it keeps going, so explain what happens).

### Inclined Plane Competition Problems
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** inclined-plane-competition
**Estimated time:** 8 min
**Key concepts:** quick calculation · standard setups · toss-up recognition
**Summary:** Competition-ready strategies and formulas for inclined plane and friction toss-ups.

#### High-Yield Formulas
- Frictionless incline acceleration: a = g·sinθ
- With friction (sliding down): a = g(sinθ − μkcosθ)
- Normal force on incline: N = mg·cosθ
- Block slides when: tanθ > μs (angle of repose)
- Stopping distance: Δx = v²/(2μkg)

#### Common Setup Recognition
"A block on a frictionless inclined plane at angle θ" → a = g·sinθ (no calculation needed once you know the formula). Special cases: θ = 0° → a = 0 (flat surface); θ = 90° → a = g (free fall). At 30°: a = g/2 = 4.9 m/s². At 45°: a = g/√2 ≈ 6.93 m/s².

#### Bowl Toss-Up Sample
"For 10 points, a 5 kg block slides down a frictionless 30-degree incline. What is the block's acceleration?"
Answer: g·sin30° = 9.8 × 0.5 = 4.9 m/s²

2. What angle makes tanθ = 0.4? (This is the angle of repose for μs = 0.4, ≈ 21.8°)

## Subtopic: Tension, Springs & Hooke's Law

### Hooke's Law and Spring Force
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hookes-law-spring-force
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** spring constant · Hooke's Law · elastic restoring force
**Summary:** Hooke's Law states that the force exerted by a spring is proportional to its displacement from equilibrium: F = −kx.

#### Hooke's Law Stated
**F = −kx** — the spring force is proportional to displacement x from natural (equilibrium) length, and acts in the *opposite* direction to the displacement. k is the spring constant (units: N/m), a measure of stiffness. A large k means a stiff spring; small k means a soft spring. The negative sign indicates a *restoring force*: the spring always tries to return to equilibrium.

#### Calculating Extension and Force
If a 50 N/m spring is stretched 0.2 m: F = kx = 50 × 0.2 = 10 N (directed back toward equilibrium). If you want to find k: hang a mass m, the spring extends by x until equilibrium (spring force = weight): kx = mg → k = mg/x. If m = 0.5 kg and x = 0.1 m: k = 0.5(9.8)/0.1 = 49 N/m.

#### Series and Parallel Springs
**Parallel** (both attached to same points, side by side): k_eff = k₁ + k₂. Springs in parallel are stiffer — both share the load. **Series** (end to end): 1/k_eff = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂. Springs in series are softer — each extends independently. For two identical springs (k each): parallel → k_eff = 2k; series → k_eff = k/2.

#### Elastic Potential Energy
Energy stored in a compressed or stretched spring: **PE_spring = ½kx²**. This is always positive (energy stored regardless of direction of displacement). Important for energy conservation problems involving springs.

#### Review Questions
1. A spring (k = 200 N/m) is compressed 0.05 m. What force does it exert?
2. A 2 kg mass hangs from a spring and stretches it 0.04 m. Find k.
3. Two springs (k₁ = 100 N/m, k₂ = 150 N/m) are connected in series. Find the effective spring constant.

### Tension in Strings and Ropes
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** tension-strings-ropes
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** tension · massless string · magnitude throughout rope
**Summary:** Tension is a pulling force transmitted through a string or rope; in a massless string, tension has the same magnitude throughout.

#### What Tension Is
Tension is the internal pulling force within a string, rope, or cable. It acts *away* from the object along the direction of the string. Every cross-section of the string transmits this force. For a massless string (standard assumption in physics problems), tension is the same everywhere along the string. For a real rope with mass, tension varies — higher at the top when the rope hangs.

#### Tension in a Vertical Rope
A 5 kg sign hangs from a rope. The rope tension = weight of sign = 49 N (equilibrium). Now if the sign accelerates upward at 3 m/s²: T − mg = ma → T = m(g + a) = 5(9.8 + 3) = 64 N. The tension is higher than the sign's weight because the rope must not only support the weight but also provide the net upward force for acceleration.

#### Multi-Mass Hanging System
Three masses (m₁ = 1 kg, m₂ = 2 kg, m₃ = 3 kg) hang in a vertical chain. In equilibrium:
- Tension T₁ (supports m₁ only): T₁ = m₁g = 9.8 N
- Tension T₂ (supports m₁ + m₂): T₂ = (m₁+m₂)g = 29.4 N
- Tension T₃ (supports all three): T₃ = (m₁+m₂+m₃)g = 58.8 N

#### Review Questions
1. A 4 kg block hangs from a rope. Find the rope tension (a) at rest, (b) accelerating upward at 2 m/s², (c) in free fall.
2. Two blocks (2 kg and 3 kg) hang from a rope in series. Find the tension in the upper segment and the lower segment.
3. A mountaineer hangs from a rope attached to a cliff. The rope makes 15° from vertical. If the mountaineer weighs 700 N, find the tension in the rope.

### Springs and Energy: Worked Applications
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** springs-energy-applications
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** spring PE · energy conservation · launch problems
**Summary:** Combining Hooke's Law with energy conservation solves spring-launch and spring-collision problems.

#### Spring Launch Problem
A spring (k = 800 N/m) is compressed 0.15 m, then releases a 0.2 kg ball horizontally on a frictionless surface.
Using energy conservation: PE_spring = KE_final
½kx² = ½mv² → kx² = mv² → v = x√(k/m) = 0.15√(800/0.2) = 0.15 × 63.2 = 9.49 m/s

#### Spring Compressed Against a Wall
A 3 kg block compresses a spring (k = 500 N/m) by 0.1 m against a wall. Spring is released. Find block's velocity when it leaves the spring.
½kx² = ½mv² → v = x√(k/m) = 0.1√(500/3) = 0.1 × 12.9 = 1.29 m/s

#### Combined Spring and Gravity
A 0.5 kg block is placed on a vertical spring (k = 200 N/m) and compresses it 0.08 m from equilibrium, then is released. Maximum height above release point?
PE_spring + PE_gravity_initial = PE_gravity_final (at max height h above release)
½kx² + 0 = mgh (taking release point as reference)
But wait — the equilibrium point is where spring force equals weight. This needs careful reference point. Using energy: ½(200)(0.08²) = 0.5(9.8)h → 0.64 = 4.9h → h = 0.131 m.

1. A spring (k = 1000 N/m) is compressed 0.2 m. It launches a 0.5 kg projectile horizontally from a 1 m high table. Find the horizontal range.
2. Two springs in series (k₁ = 200, k₂ = 300 N/m) support a 4 kg mass. How much does the system stretch?
3. A 1 kg block collides with and compresses a spring (k = 500 N/m) by 0.3 m before stopping. What was the block's initial speed?
