## Subtopic: Scientific Method & Experimental Design

### What Scientists Actually Do: The Scientific Method in Physics
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** scientific-method-in-physics
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** hypothesis · controlled experiment · falsifiability
**Summary:** The scientific method is a structured process of observation, hypothesis, testing, and revision that underlies every physics experiment.

#### Observation and Question
Physics begins with a quantifiable observation. Before forming a hypothesis, physicists measure and record phenomena precisely — noting units, significant figures, and conditions. A good physics question is specific: not "why do things fall?" but "does a heavier object fall faster than a lighter one when air resistance is negligible?"

#### Forming a Testable Hypothesis
A hypothesis must be falsifiable — it must be possible to disprove it. "Heavier objects fall at the same rate in a vacuum" is testable because you can conduct the experiment and get a definitive result. A hypothesis that can explain any outcome ("maybe it depends") is not scientific.

#### Designing a Controlled Experiment
In a controlled experiment, only one variable changes at a time — the independent variable. All other variables (mass, temperature, surface) are held constant. The dependent variable is what you measure. Controls allow you to attribute the result to the one thing you changed, not to hidden factors.

#### Analyzing and Concluding
Data is analyzed for trends, often through graphing. Physicists look for proportional relationships (linear graphs), inverse relationships (hyperbolas), or square relationships (parabolas). A conclusion states whether the data supports or refutes the hypothesis — it does not "prove" it true, only fails to disprove it.

#### Science Bowl Clue
Bowl questions about scientific method often test the difference between independent and dependent variables, or ask what makes a hypothesis "scientific." Remember: falsifiability is the key word. If a question asks what makes a good experiment, the answer almost always involves controlling variables.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between an independent variable and a dependent variable?
2. Why must a scientific hypothesis be falsifiable?
3. A student tests whether temperature affects the period of a pendulum but also changes the pendulum's length between trials. What is wrong with this experiment?

### Variables, Controls, and the Logic of Experimental Design
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** variables-controls-experimental-design
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** independent variable · dependent variable · control group
**Summary:** Understanding variable types and control groups is essential for designing valid physics experiments and interpreting published results.

#### Types of Variables
Every experiment has three kinds of variables: independent (what you change), dependent (what you measure), and controlled (what you keep constant). In an experiment testing how angle affects projectile range, the launch angle is independent, horizontal distance is dependent, and initial speed, projectile mass, and air conditions are controlled.

#### The Role of the Control Group
A control group receives no treatment — it represents the baseline. In a physics experiment testing whether a magnetic field affects current, the control setup runs the circuit without the magnet. The control lets you compare treated vs. untreated conditions with confidence.

#### Replication and Repeatability
A single trial proves nothing. Physicists repeat experiments multiple times and report the mean and standard deviation. If three trials give 9.78, 9.81, and 9.80 m/s² for gravitational acceleration, the mean is more reliable than any single measurement. Repeatability (same experimenter) and reproducibility (different labs) both matter.

#### Identifying Errors
Systematic errors shift all measurements in the same direction (e.g., a miscalibrated balance always reads 2 g too high). Random errors scatter measurements around the true value (e.g., reaction time variability). Systematic errors are more dangerous because averaging doesn't eliminate them.

#### Review Questions
1. Name the three types of variables in a controlled experiment.
2. What is the difference between systematic error and random error?
3. A student measures the speed of sound three times and gets 340, 338, and 342 m/s. What is the mean? What type of error do these variations represent?

### Accuracy, Precision, and Uncertainty in Measurement
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** accuracy-precision-uncertainty
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** accuracy · precision · percent error
**Summary:** Accuracy and precision are distinct qualities of measurement, and every measurement in physics must include an estimate of uncertainty.

#### Accuracy vs. Precision
Accuracy describes how close a measurement is to the true value. Precision describes how close repeated measurements are to each other. A dartboard analogy: darts clustered in the bullseye = accurate and precise; darts clustered away from center = precise but not accurate; darts scattered everywhere = neither.

#### Significant Figures
Every measured quantity has limited precision. The number of significant figures (sig figs) reflects this. 12.34 cm has 4 sig figs; 0.0034 m has 2 sig figs (leading zeros don't count). When multiplying or dividing, round the answer to the fewest sig figs in the inputs.

#### Percent Error
Percent error = |experimental − theoretical| / theoretical × 100%. If you measure g = 9.4 m/s² and the accepted value is 9.8 m/s², percent error = |9.4−9.8|/9.8 × 100% = 4.1%. Always use the absolute value in the numerator.

#### Uncertainty Notation
Every measurement should be written as value ± uncertainty (e.g., 9.81 ± 0.05 m/s²). The uncertainty can come from the instrument's smallest division, from statistical spread in repeated trials, or from both. Uncertainty propagates through calculations — add absolute uncertainties when adding/subtracting; add relative (%) uncertainties when multiplying/dividing.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between accuracy and precision? Give an example of each.
2. How many significant figures are in the number 0.00500?
3. A student measures the acceleration due to gravity as 9.6 m/s². The accepted value is 9.81 m/s². Calculate the percent error.

### Applying the Scientific Method: Designing a Physics Investigation
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** applying-scientific-method-physics-investigation
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** experimental design · variable identification · error analysis
**Summary:** Walk through designing a complete physics investigation — from question to conclusion — using projectile motion as the example.

#### The Investigation: Does Launch Angle Affect Range?
You want to know: at what angle does a projectile travel the farthest horizontally? Start with theory — kinematics predicts maximum range at 45°. Your hypothesis: "A projectile launched at 45° will have the greatest horizontal range compared to other angles, given constant initial speed."

#### Setting Up the Experiment
Independent variable: launch angle (15°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 75°). Dependent variable: horizontal range (measured in meters). Controlled variables: initial speed (set by a spring launcher), projectile mass (same ball), launch height, and surface angle. Run 5 trials at each angle; record and average.

#### Collecting and Graphing Data
Plot angle (x-axis) vs. mean range (y-axis). If theory is correct, you'll see a parabolic shape peaking at 45°. Notice that 30° and 60° give the same range — complementary angles are symmetric. Draw error bars representing ± one standard deviation.

#### Evaluating Error and Conclusion
Sources of error: variability in launch speed, air currents, measurement of landing position. Conclusion: "The data supports the hypothesis. The 45° launch produced the greatest range (4.82 ± 0.08 m). The complementary angle symmetry was observed (30° ≈ 60°), consistent with kinematic theory."

#### Review Questions
1. In the projectile experiment, what would happen to your conclusion if the launch speed varied between trials?
2. Why do complementary angles (e.g., 30° and 60°) produce the same range?
3. A student plots their data and finds the peak at 40°, not 45°. Propose two possible explanations.

### Reading and Critiquing a Physics Experiment Report
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** reading-critiquing-physics-experiment-report
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** experimental validity · sources of error · peer review
**Summary:** Learn to evaluate the quality of an experimental report by identifying design flaws, uncontrolled variables, and unjustified conclusions.

#### What Makes a Report Credible?
A credible physics report states a clear hypothesis, identifies all variables, describes the procedure in enough detail to reproduce it, reports raw data and statistical analysis, and draws conclusions that follow directly from the data. It also acknowledges limitations.

#### Spotting Flaws: A Sample Report
"I tested whether mass affects falling time by dropping a feather and a bowling ball from 2 m. The bowling ball hit first. Conclusion: heavier objects fall faster." What's wrong? Air resistance is a confounding variable — the feather's large surface area causes drag, not its low mass. The experiment didn't control for drag.

#### Evaluating Conclusions
Does the conclusion go beyond the data? "Heavier objects always fall faster" goes beyond a two-object comparison. "In the presence of air resistance, objects with lower surface area-to-mass ratios fall faster" is a better, bounded conclusion.

#### Peer Review and Replication
Science becomes reliable through peer review (experts critique the methods and logic before publication) and independent replication (other labs reproduce the result). A single experiment, however well-designed, is not enough for a scientific consensus.

#### Review Questions
1. What is a confounding variable? Give an example from the feather-bowling ball experiment.
2. Why is replication important even if the original experiment was well-designed?
3. A paper claims "Exercise improves physics test scores" based on one trial of 10 students. Identify two weaknesses in this study.

### Scientific Method in Physics: Synthesis Problems
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** scientific-method-synthesis-problems
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** synthesis · experimental critique · error types
**Summary:** Work through multi-concept problems linking experimental design, error analysis, and the scientific process.

#### Problem 1: Design a Hooke's Law Experiment
You want to verify that spring force is proportional to extension (F = kx). Write: (a) a testable hypothesis, (b) independent and dependent variables, (c) two controlled variables, (d) potential sources of systematic error.
*Solution:* Hypothesis: "The extension of a spring is directly proportional to the applied force." Independent: mass added (which determines force F = mg). Dependent: spring extension (measured with a ruler). Controlled: same spring, room temperature, vertical orientation. Systematic error: ruler not perfectly aligned with spring; zero-extension mark measured incorrectly.

#### Problem 2: Analyzing a Table of Results
A student measures the period of a pendulum 5 times: 2.01, 1.98, 2.03, 2.00, 2.02 s. The accepted period is 2.00 s. Calculate: (a) the mean, (b) the percent error.
*Solution:* Mean = (2.01+1.98+2.03+2.00+2.02)/5 = 10.04/5 = 2.008 s. Percent error = |2.008−2.00|/2.00 × 100% = 0.4%.

#### Problem 3: Evaluate This Conclusion
"I dropped a marble from 1 m and 2 m. It took longer from 2 m. Therefore, distance is proportional to time in free fall." Is this conclusion valid?
*Solution:* No. The relationship between distance and time in free fall is d = ½gt² — distance is proportional to time squared, not time. Two data points are also insufficient to claim proportionality; a graph of multiple points is needed.

#### Review Questions
1. What graph would you plot to verify that d ∝ t² for free fall?
2. If all your measured values are 5% higher than the true value, what type of error is this?
3. Why can't two data points prove a proportional relationship?

### The SI System: Units That Make Physics Universal
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** si-system-units-physics-universal
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** SI base units · prefixes · unit conversion
**Summary:** The International System of Units (SI) defines the seven base units from which all physics quantities are derived.

#### The Seven Base Units
The SI system has seven base units: meter (m) for length, kilogram (kg) for mass, second (s) for time, ampere (A) for electric current, kelvin (K) for temperature, mole (mol) for amount of substance, and candela (cd) for luminous intensity. Everything else — newtons, joules, watts, pascals — is derived from these seven.

#### Derived Units
A newton (N) = kg·m/s². A joule (J) = kg·m²/s² = N·m. A watt (W) = J/s = kg·m²/s³. A pascal (Pa) = N/m² = kg/(m·s²). Understanding derivations lets you check equations: if both sides have the same units, the equation is dimensionally consistent.

#### SI Prefixes
Key prefixes: nano (n) = 10⁻⁹, micro (μ) = 10⁻⁶, milli (m) = 10⁻³, centi (c) = 10⁻², kilo (k) = 10³, mega (M) = 10⁶, giga (G) = 10⁹. Example: 500 nm = 500 × 10⁻⁹ m = 5 × 10⁻⁷ m. This is the wavelength of visible (green) light.

#### Why Units Matter
Units prevent disasters. In 1999, NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter was lost because one team used pound-force·seconds and another used newton·seconds. Always carry units through every calculation; they'll catch errors that numbers alone miss.

#### Review Questions
1. What are the SI base units for force (newton) in terms of kg, m, and s?
2. Convert 450 nm to meters in scientific notation.
3. A student calculates power as P = Fd/t. Show that the units work out to watts.

### Dimensional Analysis: The Unit Cancellation Technique
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** dimensional-analysis-unit-cancellation
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** dimensional analysis · conversion factors · unit checking
**Summary:** Dimensional analysis is a systematic method for unit conversion and equation checking using multiplication by conversion factors equal to 1.

#### The Conversion Factor Method
Every conversion factor equals exactly 1 (e.g., 1 km/1000 m = 1). Multiplying any quantity by 1 doesn't change its value — only the units change. To convert 60 mph to m/s: 60 miles/hour × 1609 m/1 mile × 1 hour/3600 s = 26.8 m/s. Line up so the units you want to cancel are on opposite sides of the fraction.

#### Multi-Step Conversions
Convert 5.0 g/cm³ to kg/m³:
5.0 g/cm³ × (1 kg/1000 g) × (100 cm/1 m)³
= 5.0 × (1/1000) × (10⁶) kg/m³
= 5000 kg/m³.
Note: cubing the length unit means cubing the conversion factor (100 cm = 1 m, so 100³ cm³ = 1 m³).

#### Dimensional Analysis to Check Equations
If an equation's units don't balance, the equation is wrong. Check kinetic energy: KE = ½mv². Units: kg × (m/s)² = kg·m²/s² = J ✓. Check: F = mv/t²? Units: kg × m/s / s² = kg·m/s³. That's not newtons (kg·m/s²). ✗ The equation is wrong.

#### Estimating with Dimensional Analysis
You can estimate unknown quantities using dimensional analysis alone. How many seconds are in a year? 365 days × 24 hr × 60 min × 60 s ≈ 3.15 × 10⁷ s. This is a famous physics approximation worth memorizing (π × 10⁷ s).

#### Review Questions
1. Convert 90 km/h to m/s using dimensional analysis. Show all conversion factors.
2. A student writes the formula a = v²/d. Check if the units work for acceleration (m/s²).
3. How many microseconds are in one day?

### Significant Figures: Rules and Applications
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** significant-figures-rules-applications
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** significant figures · rounding rules · sig fig in calculations
**Summary:** Significant figures encode the precision of measurements; the rules for counting and using them in calculations are essential for all physics work.

#### Counting Significant Figures
Rules: (1) All non-zero digits are significant. (2) Zeros between non-zeros are significant (1003 has 4 sig figs). (3) Leading zeros are never significant (0.0045 has 2 sig figs). (4) Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant (2.50 has 3 sig figs). (5) Trailing zeros without a decimal are ambiguous (200 could have 1, 2, or 3 sig figs — use scientific notation to clarify: 2.00 × 10²).

#### Sig Figs in Multiplication and Division
The result has the same number of sig figs as the input with the fewest. Example: 3.21 × 4.5 = 14.445 → round to 14 (2 sig figs, since 4.5 has only 2).

#### Sig Figs in Addition and Subtraction
The result is rounded to the same decimal place as the least precise input. Example: 12.3 + 1.45 = 13.75 → round to 13.8 (limited to tenths place by 12.3).

#### Exact Numbers
Defined values (1 km = 1000 m exactly) and counted values (5 students) have infinite significant figures and never limit your answer's sig figs.

#### Review Questions
1. How many sig figs are in 0.00307?
2. Calculate 12.4 × 0.30. Round to the correct number of sig figs.
3. A plank is 3.5 m long. A student adds two pieces: 3.5 m + 0.25 m + 1.2 m. What is the correct answer with appropriate sig figs?

### Dimensional Analysis in Real Physics Problems
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** dimensional-analysis-real-physics-problems
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** unit conversion · equation verification · physics estimation
**Summary:** Apply dimensional analysis to convert complex units, verify physics equations, and make order-of-magnitude estimates.

#### Problem Set: Unit Conversions
Convert these: (a) 55 miles/hour → m/s. (b) 1.0 atm → Pa (1 atm = 101,325 Pa). (c) 1 eV → joules (1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J). (d) 100 W for 1 hour → joules.
Solutions: (a) 55 × 1609/3600 = 24.6 m/s. (b) 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa. (c) 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. (d) 100 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁵ J.

#### Verifying Equations by Dimensions
Verify the period of a simple pendulum: T = 2π√(L/g). Units: √(m / (m/s²)) = √(s²) = s ✓. Now check T = 2π√(m/k) for a spring-mass system. Units: √(kg / (N/m)) = √(kg / (kg/s²)) = √(s²) = s ✓. Both give seconds — dimensionally consistent.

#### Order-of-Magnitude Estimates
How much energy does a car engine produce per year if it runs at 100 kW for 4 hours a day? 100 kW × 4 hr × 365 days = 146,000 kWh ≈ 1.5 × 10⁵ kWh = 5.3 × 10¹¹ J. This is called a Fermi estimate — physicists use these constantly to sanity-check calculations.

#### Review Questions
1. Convert 1 light-year to meters. (Speed of light ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s; 1 year ≈ 3.15 × 10⁷ s)
2. Verify that the equation for gravitational potential energy PE = mgh has units of joules.
3. Estimate the number of heartbeats in a human lifetime (assume 70 years, 70 bpm).

#### Synthesis Problem 1: Equation Building
A pendulum swings. Someone tells you the period depends only on length L and gravity g. Use dimensional analysis to find the form of the equation. [L] = m, [g] = m/s². We need [T] = s. Try T ∝ Lᵃgᵇ: mᵃ(m/s²)ᵇ = s → m^(a+b) s^(−2b) = s⁰ s¹. So a+b=0 and −2b=1 → b=−½, a=½. Therefore T ∝ √(L/g). ✓

#### Synthesis Problem 2: Sig Fig Chain
Calculate the density of a block: mass = 125.0 g, volume = 87.3 cm³. Density = 125.0/87.3 = 1.432… g/cm³. Sig figs: 125.0 has 4, 87.3 has 3. Round to 3 sig figs: 1.43 g/cm³. Convert to kg/m³: 1.43 g/cm³ × 1000 kg/g × 10⁶ cm³/m³... wait, that's wrong. Correctly: ×(1 kg/1000 g) × (100 cm/m)³ = 1.43 × 10³ kg/m³.

#### Synthesis Problem 3: Error Propagation
If A = 3.2 ± 0.1 m and B = 1.5 ± 0.1 m, find A+B and A×B with uncertainties. A+B = 4.7 ± 0.2 m (add absolute uncertainties). A×B = 4.8 m²; relative uncertainty = 0.1/3.2 + 0.1/1.5 = 0.031 + 0.067 = 0.098 ≈ 10%; absolute uncertainty = 4.8 × 0.098 ≈ ±0.47 m² → A×B = 4.8 ± 0.5 m².

## Subtopic: Vectors & Scalars

### Vectors vs. Scalars: Direction Changes Everything
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** vectors-vs-scalars-direction-matters
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** vector · scalar · magnitude and direction
**Summary:** Scalars have magnitude only; vectors have both magnitude and direction — and this distinction changes how you add, subtract, and use physical quantities.

#### What Makes a Quantity a Vector?
A scalar is a single number with a unit: mass (5 kg), temperature (300 K), speed (10 m/s), energy (50 J). A vector has both magnitude and direction: displacement (10 m north), velocity (10 m/s north), force (50 N at 30°). You can't fully specify displacement without saying which way you moved.

#### Representing Vectors
Vectors are drawn as arrows. The arrow's length represents magnitude; the arrowhead shows direction. In equations, vectors are written in bold (**F**) or with an arrow (→F). The magnitude of a vector **A** is written |**A**| or simply A.

#### Key Physics Vectors
Vectors: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum, electric field, magnetic field. Scalars: distance, speed, mass, energy, time, temperature, work (technically a scalar — force dotted with displacement). Note: speed is scalar, velocity is vector. Distance is scalar, displacement is vector.

#### Why Direction Matters
If you walk 3 m east and then 3 m west, your displacement is 0 m — you're back where you started. But your distance traveled is 6 m. Speed (scalar) uses distance; velocity (vector) uses displacement. This distinction is fundamental to kinematics.

### Vector Addition and Components
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** vector-addition-and-components
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** vector addition · components · resultant
**Summary:** Vectors are added using the head-to-tail method or by breaking them into x and y components — the component method is faster and exact for any angle.

#### Head-to-Tail Method
To add vectors graphically, place the tail of the second vector at the head of the first. The resultant goes from the original tail to the final head. For vectors at right angles, the resultant magnitude comes from the Pythagorean theorem: R = √(A² + B²).

#### The Component Method
Any vector **A** can be broken into: Aₓ = A cos(θ) and Aᵧ = A sin(θ), where θ is measured from the positive x-axis. To add vectors **A** and **B**: Rₓ = Aₓ + Bₓ, Rᵧ = Aᵧ + Bᵧ. Then R = √(Rₓ² + Rᵧ²) and θ_R = arctan(Rᵧ/Rₓ).

#### Example: Adding Two Forces
Force **F₁** = 40 N at 0° (east). Force **F₂** = 30 N at 90° (north). F₁ₓ = 40, F₁ᵧ = 0. F₂ₓ = 0, F₂ᵧ = 30. Rₓ = 40, Rᵧ = 30. R = √(40² + 30²) = √(1600+900) = √2500 = 50 N. θ = arctan(30/40) = 36.9° north of east.

#### Vector Subtraction
**A** − **B** = **A** + (−**B**). Negating a vector reverses its direction. This is critical for finding change in velocity: Δ**v** = **v**f − **v**i.

#### Review Questions
1. A vector has magnitude 13 N at 67.4° above the x-axis. Find its x and y components.
2. Add **A** = 5 m/s east and **B** = 12 m/s north. Find the resultant magnitude and direction.
3. What does it mean to negate a vector?

### Unit Vectors and Dot Products
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** unit-vectors-dot-products
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** unit vector · dot product · work calculation
**Summary:** Unit vectors provide a compact notation for vector directions, and the dot product lets you calculate how much of one vector acts along another — essential for work.

#### Unit Vectors: î, ĵ, k̂
Unit vectors have magnitude 1 and point along coordinate axes: î (x-direction), ĵ (y-direction), k̂ (z-direction). Any vector can be written: **A** = Aₓî + Aᵧĵ + Azk̂. Example: a force of 30 N east and 40 N north is written **F** = 30î + 40ĵ N.

#### The Dot Product (Scalar Product)
**A** · **B** = AB cos(θ), where θ is the angle between them. The result is a scalar. Component form: **A** · **B** = AₓBₓ + AᵧBᵧ + AzBz. Special cases: perpendicular vectors → dot product = 0 (cos 90° = 0). Parallel vectors → dot product = AB (cos 0° = 1).

#### Work as a Dot Product
Work W = **F** · **d** = Fd cos(θ). If you push a box with 50 N at 30° to the horizontal, and it moves 10 m horizontally, W = 50 × 10 × cos(30°) = 500 × 0.866 = 433 J. Only the component of force along the displacement does work.

#### When the Dot Product is Zero
If force is perpendicular to motion (θ = 90°), no work is done. Gravity does no work on a satellite in circular orbit (force toward center, motion perpendicular). A normal force does no work on a sliding object (normal force ⊥ surface, motion along surface).

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate **A** · **B** if **A** = 3î + 4ĵ and **B** = 2î − 3ĵ.
2. A force **F** = 10î + 0ĵ N pushes a block that moves **d** = 5î + 3ĵ m. Calculate the work done.
3. At what angle between force and displacement is work maximized? Minimized?

### Applying Vectors: Navigation and Force Problems
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** applying-vectors-navigation-force-problems
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** vector addition · resultant force · navigation
**Summary:** Apply vector addition and component decomposition to navigation problems and multi-force physics scenarios.

#### Navigation Problem: River Crossing
A boat travels at 4.0 m/s east relative to water. The river current is 3.0 m/s south. What is the boat's velocity relative to ground? Vₓ = 4.0 m/s, Vᵧ = −3.0 m/s. |V| = √(4² + 3²) = 5.0 m/s. Angle = arctan(3/4) = 36.9° south of east. If the river is 100 m wide, crossing time = 100/4 = 25 s. Drift south = 3.0 × 25 = 75 m.

#### Multi-Force Problem: Block on Incline
A 10 kg block sits on a 30° frictionless incline. Find the acceleration. Decompose gravity along and perpendicular to the slope: Component along slope = mg sin(30°) = 10 × 9.8 × 0.5 = 49 N (down slope). Perpendicular component = mg cos(30°) = 84.9 N (into slope, balanced by normal). Net force = 49 N → a = 49/10 = 4.9 m/s².

#### Three-Force Equilibrium
Three forces act on a point: **F₁** = 100 N east, **F₂** = 80 N north, **F₃** = ? (equilibrium). For equilibrium, **F₃** = −(**F₁** + **F₂**) = −(100î + 80ĵ) = −100î − 80ĵ N. Magnitude = √(100² + 80²) = 128 N, direction = 38.7° south of west.

2. A 5 kg block is on a 45° frictionless incline. What is the acceleration down the slope?
3. Two forces of 60 N and 80 N act at right angles on an object. What single force would produce equilibrium?

### Reading Physics Graphs: Interpreting Motion and Relationships
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** reading-physics-graphs-motion-relationships
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** slope · area under curve · linearization
**Summary:** Graphs are physics tools — slope and area under a curve carry physical meaning that equations alone don't show.

#### Position-Time Graphs
The slope of a position-time graph = velocity. Constant slope → constant velocity (uniform motion). Changing slope → acceleration. The y-intercept = initial position. Horizontal line → at rest. Negative slope → moving in negative direction. Steeper slope → faster speed.

#### Velocity-Time Graphs
Slope of v-t graph = acceleration. Area under a v-t graph = displacement. A horizontal line → constant velocity, zero acceleration. A straight upward line → constant (positive) acceleration. Area under the curve from t₁ to t₂ = change in position.

#### Force-Extension (Hooke's Law) Graphs
A spring force graph (F vs. x) is linear with slope = k (spring constant). The area under this graph = elastic potential energy stored = ½kx². Recognizing what the slope and area represent turns every graph into a physics equation.

#### Linearization
Many physics relationships are not linear. To graph them as straight lines: if y = kx², plot y vs. x² (slope = k). If y = A/x, plot y vs. 1/x (slope = A). If y = Ae^(Bt), plot ln(y) vs. t (slope = B). Linearization makes it easy to extract constants from experimental data.

#### Review Questions
1. What does the slope of a velocity-time graph represent?
2. What does the area under a force-displacement graph represent?
3. A student plots T² vs. L for a pendulum and gets a straight line. What is the slope in terms of physics constants?

### Uncertainty, Error Bars, and Line of Best Fit
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** uncertainty-error-bars-best-fit-line
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** error bars · line of best fit · residuals
**Summary:** Error bars represent measurement uncertainty on graphs; the line of best fit and residuals reveal whether a proposed model matches the data.

#### What Error Bars Mean
Error bars extend above and below each data point by ± the uncertainty. If error bars on y use ±Δy, the true value lies within that bar with ~68% confidence (one standard deviation). Overlapping error bars between two conditions suggests the difference may not be statistically significant.

#### Drawing a Line of Best Fit
The best-fit line minimizes the total squared distance from all points to the line (least-squares method). It should pass through the middle of the data, with roughly equal points above and below. It does NOT need to go through any specific point, including the origin, unless physics demands it.

#### Slope and Its Uncertainty
Extract slope from the best-fit line using two points far apart on the line (not data points). Calculate maximum and minimum slopes from the range of reasonable fit lines within the error bars. Report slope ± uncertainty.

#### Residuals
A residual = (measured y) − (predicted y from line). Plot residuals vs. x. If residuals are random, the linear model fits. If residuals show a pattern (curve, oscillation), the model is wrong — you need a different functional form.

#### Review Questions
1. What does it mean if two data points have overlapping error bars?
2. How do you determine if a linear model is appropriate from a residual plot?
3. A graph of spring extension vs. force yields slope = 0.025 m/N. What is the spring constant k?

### Statistical Analysis in Physics Experiments
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** statistical-analysis-physics-experiments
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** mean · standard deviation · standard error
**Summary:** Mean, standard deviation, and standard error are the statistical tools that transform repeated measurements into reliable physics values.

#### Mean and Standard Deviation
Mean x̄ = Σxᵢ/n. Standard deviation σ = √[Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/(n−1)]. Standard deviation measures spread — how much individual measurements vary around the mean. A small σ → precise measurements. A large σ → scattered measurements.

#### Standard Error of the Mean
Standard error = σ/√n. This estimates how close the sample mean is to the true population mean. It shrinks as you take more measurements. Reporting your result as x̄ ± SE (e.g., 9.81 ± 0.03 m/s²) means you're 68% confident the true value is in that range.

#### Normal Distribution
Many measurement errors follow a normal (Gaussian) distribution: bell-shaped, symmetric. About 68% of measurements fall within 1σ of the mean; 95% within 2σ; 99.7% within 3σ. This is the basis for confidence intervals in physics reporting.

#### Propagating Uncertainty Through Calculations
Addition/subtraction: add absolute uncertainties. Multiplication/division: add relative (percentage) uncertainties. Example: density = mass/volume. Δmass/mass = 0.1/50.0 = 0.2%; Δvolume/volume = 0.5/20.0 = 2.5%. Δdensity/density = 2.7%. If density = 2.50 g/cm³, absolute uncertainty = 2.5 × 2.7% ≈ 0.07 g/cm³.

#### Review Questions
1. Five measurements: 9.80, 9.78, 9.81, 9.82, 9.79 m/s². Calculate the mean and estimate standard error as σ/√n.
2. What happens to standard error as you quadruple the number of measurements?
3. If the uncertainty in force is 2% and in distance is 3%, what is the uncertainty in work (W = Fd)?

### Graphing Lab Data: Pendulum Period vs. Length
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** graphing-lab-data-pendulum-period
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** linearization · best-fit line · extracting constants
**Summary:** A complete worked example of graphing experimental data, linearizing the relationship, and extracting the gravitational acceleration g from a pendulum experiment.

#### The Experiment
You measure the period T of a pendulum at different lengths L. Theory says T = 2π√(L/g). Data collected:

| L (m) | T (s) |
|-------|-------|
| 0.25 | 1.00 |
| 0.50 | 1.41 |
| 1.00 | 2.00 |
| 1.50 | 2.45 |

#### Linearizing the Data
T = 2π√(L/g) → T² = (4π²/g)L. Plot T² vs. L. This should give a straight line through the origin with slope m = 4π²/g. From the table: T² values are 1.00, 1.99, 4.00, 6.00 s². Plot T² (y) vs. L (x).

#### Extracting g from the Slope
From the graph, slope ≈ (6.00−0)/(1.50−0) = 4.00 s²/m. Since slope = 4π²/g: g = 4π²/slope = 4 × 9.87/4.00 = 39.48/4.00 = 9.87 m/s². Compare to accepted g = 9.81 m/s². Percent error = 0.6%.

#### Evaluating Fit Quality
Plot residuals: T²_measured − T²_predicted. If the residuals are small and random, the linear model is excellent. If there's a systematic offset, there may be air resistance effects, or the pivot point adds effective length.

#### Review Questions
1. Why do we plot T² vs. L instead of T vs. L for a pendulum?
2. From a T² vs. L graph, what is the physical meaning of the y-intercept?
3. A student's best-fit slope is 3.80 s²/m. What value of g does this give?
