#### Observation and Question
Physics begins with a quantifiable observation. Before forming a hypothesis, physicists measure and record phenomena precisely — noting units, significant figures, and conditions. A good physics question is specific: not "why do things fall?" but "does a heavier object fall faster than a lighter one when air resistance is negligible?"

#### Forming a Testable Hypothesis
A hypothesis must be falsifiable — it must be possible to disprove it. "Heavier objects fall at the same rate in a vacuum" is testable because you can conduct the experiment and get a definitive result. A hypothesis that can explain any outcome ("maybe it depends") is not scientific.

#### Designing a Controlled Experiment
In a controlled experiment, only one variable changes at a time — the independent variable. All other variables (mass, temperature, surface) are held constant. The dependent variable is what you measure. Controls allow you to attribute the result to the one thing you changed, not to hidden factors.

#### Analyzing and Concluding
Data is analyzed for trends, often through graphing. Physicists look for proportional relationships (linear graphs), inverse relationships (hyperbolas), or square relationships (parabolas). A conclusion states whether the data supports or refutes the hypothesis — it does not "prove" it true, only fails to disprove it.

#### Science Bowl Clue
Bowl questions about scientific method often test the difference between independent and dependent variables, or ask what makes a hypothesis "scientific." Remember: falsifiability is the key word. If a question asks what makes a good experiment, the answer almost always involves controlling variables.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between an independent variable and a dependent variable?
2. Why must a scientific hypothesis be falsifiable?
3. A student tests whether temperature affects the period of a pendulum but also changes the pendulum's length between trials. What is wrong with this experiment?
