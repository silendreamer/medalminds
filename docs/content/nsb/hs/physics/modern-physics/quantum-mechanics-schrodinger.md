#### Wave Function & Probability
In quantum mechanics, an electron's state is described by a wave function ψ(x, t). The wave function is not directly observable; instead, |ψ(x)|² gives the probability density of finding the electron at position x. The electron doesn't have a definite position until measured; the wave function represents all possible positions weighted by probability. The wave function must normalize: ∫|ψ|² dx = 1 (probability of finding it somewhere is 1).

#### Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation
For a particle in a potential V(x), the time-independent Schrödinger equation is: −(ℏ²/2m)(d²ψ/dx²) + V(x)ψ = Eψ. This is an eigenvalue equation: solutions ψ and E exist only for specific (quantized) values of E. These allowed energies are the system's quantum levels. Solving Schrödinger's equation for a hydrogen atom reproduces Bohr's energy levels without assuming classical orbits.

#### Quantum Numbers & Orbitals
Hydrogen's wave functions are labeled by quantum numbers (n, l, m_l): n (principal, determines energy), l (orbital angular momentum quantum number, l = 0, 1, ..., n − 1), and m_l (magnetic quantum number, m_l = −l, ..., 0, ..., l). Each set of quantum numbers defines an orbital (a spatial probability distribution for the electron). The s orbital (l = 0) is spherical; p orbitals (l = 1) are dumbbell-shaped; d orbitals (l = 2) are cloverleaf-shaped.

#### Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle
Heisenberg's uncertainty principle states that Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2, where Δx is the uncertainty in position and Δp is the uncertainty in momentum. The more precisely you know position, the less precisely you know momentum, and vice versa. This is not a measurement limitation but a fundamental quantum property: particles don't have well-defined positions and momenta simultaneously. For an electron confined to atomic dimensions (Δx ~ 10⁻¹⁰ m), the uncertainty in momentum is huge (Δp ~ 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s), meaning high uncertainty in velocity.
