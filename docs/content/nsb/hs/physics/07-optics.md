## Subtopic: Reflection & Mirrors

### Plane Mirrors
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** plane-mirrors
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** virtual image · image distance · law of reflection · mirror equation
**Summary:** A plane mirror produces a virtual, upright image at a distance behind the mirror equal to the object distance in front.

#### Properties of Plane Mirror Images
When an object is placed in front of a plane mirror, light reflects according to the law of reflection (angle of incidence = angle of reflection). The reflected rays, when extended behind the mirror, appear to come from a point behind the mirror—the image location. For a plane mirror, the image is virtual (light doesn't actually pass through it), upright, and the same size as the object. The image distance behind the mirror equals the object distance in front: d_i = −d_o (negative sign indicates a virtual image).

#### Locating Images by Geometry
To find the image position, draw rays from the object to the mirror and apply the law of reflection. Two rays are sufficient: one perpendicular to the mirror (reflects straight back) and one at an angle (reflects at the same angle on the opposite side). The image is located where these reflected rays (extended backward) intersect. For an extended object, each point has a corresponding image point.

#### Curved Relationships
For a plane mirror, the magnification is m = 1 (image size equals object size). The mirror equation 1/d_o + 1/d_i = 1/f simplifies for a plane mirror: since R → ∞ (infinite radius of curvature), f → ∞, and 1/d_o + 1/d_i = 0, giving d_i = −d_o as expected.

#### Multiple Mirrors
When two plane mirrors are placed at an angle θ to each other, the number of images formed is n = (360°/θ) − 1 (for 360°/θ an integer). For example, two mirrors at 90° form 3 images. The images arrange symmetrically around the angle bisector. This principle is used in kaleidoscopes and mirror systems for optical instruments.

#### Review Questions
1. An object 2 m in front of a mirror is how far from its image?
2. What is the magnification of a plane mirror?
3. Two plane mirrors at 60° to each other form how many images?

### Curved Mirrors: Spherical Mirrors
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** curved-mirrors-spherical
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** concave mirror · convex mirror · focal length · mirror equation · real vs virtual images
**Summary:** Curved mirrors (spherical) focus or diverge light; concave mirrors can form real, inverted images, while convex mirrors always form virtual, upright images.

#### Concave & Convex Mirrors
A concave (converging) mirror curves inward and can focus light to a real image. A convex (diverging) mirror curves outward and always produces virtual images. The center of curvature (C) is located at distance R = 2f from the mirror surface, where f is the focal length. For a concave mirror, light rays parallel to the principal axis converge at the focal point (f > 0). For a convex mirror, reflected rays appear to diverge from the focal point (f < 0).

#### Mirror Equation
The mirror equation relates object distance (d_o), image distance (d_i), and focal length (f): 1/d_o + 1/d_i = 1/f. For a concave mirror with real object and d_o > f, a real, inverted, magnified image forms at d_i > f. For d_o < f, a virtual, upright, magnified image forms (d_i < 0). For a convex mirror, d_i is always negative (virtual), and magnification is less than 1 (image smaller than object).

#### Magnification
The magnification is m = −d_i/d_o. For a real image (d_i > 0), m is negative (inverted). For a virtual image (d_i < 0), m is positive (upright). The magnitude |m| gives the size ratio. A magnification of −2 means the image is inverted and twice the object size. A magnification of 0.5 means the image is upright and half the object size.

#### Ray Diagrams
Three key rays are used to locate images: (1) A ray parallel to the principal axis reflects through the focal point (or appears to come from it for convex). (2) A ray through the focal point reflects parallel to the principal axis. (3) A ray through the center of curvature reflects back along itself. The image is where these rays intersect (real) or appear to intersect (virtual).

#### Review Questions
1. A concave mirror has focal length 10 cm. An object 30 cm in front creates an image. Where is the image, and is it real or virtual?
2. A convex mirror has focal length −15 cm. Where does an object 10 cm in front appear?
3. If magnification is −3, is the image real or virtual? Upright or inverted?

### Ray Diagrams & Image Formation
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ray-diagrams-image-formation
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** principal axis · focal point · object at different distances · image characteristics
**Summary:** Ray diagrams visually show how mirrors and lenses form images by tracing the paths of light rays.

#### Ray Diagram Construction
To construct a ray diagram: (1) Draw the principal axis (horizontal line through mirror/lens). (2) Mark the focal point F and center of curvature C. (3) Draw the object (usually a vertical arrow). (4) Trace at least two principal rays to locate the image. (5) Note the image's position, size, orientation, and whether it's real or virtual.

#### Image Formation in Concave Mirrors at Different Object Distances
- Object beyond C (d_o > 2f): Real, inverted, reduced image at distance f < d_i < 2f.
- Object at C (d_o = 2f): Real, inverted, same-size image at d_i = 2f (1:1 magnification).
- Object between C and F (f < d_o < 2f): Real, inverted, magnified image at d_i > 2f.
- Object at F (d_o = f): Image at infinity (parallel rays after reflection).
- Object between F and mirror (d_o < f): Virtual, upright, magnified image behind mirror.

#### Image Formation in Convex Mirrors
For any object distance in a convex mirror, the image is always virtual, upright, and reduced in size (|m| < 1). As object distance increases, the image approaches the focal point but never reaches it. Convex mirrors provide wide-angle views (hence their use in security mirrors and vehicle side mirrors).

#### Worked Example: Concave Mirror
Object height 5 cm, object distance 40 cm, focal length 10 cm. Using 1/d_o + 1/d_i = 1/f: 1/40 + 1/d_i = 1/10. Solving: 1/d_i = 1/10 − 1/40 = 3/40, so d_i = 13.3 cm. Magnification m = −d_i/d_o = −13.3/40 ≈ −0.33. Image height = |m| × object height = 0.33 × 5 ≈ 1.7 cm, inverted.

#### Worked Example: Convex Mirror
Object height 10 cm, object distance 30 cm, focal length −12 cm. Using 1/30 + 1/d_i = 1/(−12): 1/d_i = −1/12 − 1/30 = −5/60 − 2/60 = −7/60, so d_i ≈ −8.6 cm (virtual). Magnification m = −(−8.6)/30 ≈ 0.29. Image height ≈ 2.9 cm, upright.

#### Review Questions
1. Draw a ray diagram showing a concave mirror with an object between F and the mirror.
2. An object 20 cm from a concave mirror (f = 5 cm) forms an image where?
3. Why are convex mirrors used in security applications?

### Defects & Aberrations
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** mirror-defects-aberrations
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** spherical aberration · coma · astigmatism · chromatic aberration · diffraction limit
**Summary:** Real mirrors and lenses deviate from ideal behavior, producing image defects that limit optical system quality.

#### Spherical Aberration
Spherical mirrors don't focus all rays to a single point; rays far from the principal axis focus closer to the mirror than paraxial rays (near the axis). This spherical aberration blurs images. Parabolic mirrors eliminate this by focusing all rays to a single focal point, which is why telescopes and searchlights use parabolic reflectors rather than spherical ones.

#### Astigmatism
Astigmatism occurs when an optical system has different focal lengths in different planes. Off-axis objects have images that are blurred differently in horizontal and vertical directions. This is a major source of image degradation in wide-angle systems. Cylindrical lenses have astigmatism by design (different focal lengths in perpendicular directions).

#### Coma
Coma is an aberration appearing as comet-like asymmetrical blurring of off-axis objects. Rays from an off-axis point fail to focus at a single spot, instead creating a tail-like pattern. This limits the field of view of optical systems.

#### Diffraction Limit
Even perfect optical systems are limited by diffraction. The minimum resolvable angular separation is θ ≈ λ/D, where λ is the wavelength and D is the aperture diameter. This Rayleigh criterion sets the diffraction-limited resolution. Larger telescopes (larger D) have better resolution. Shorter wavelengths (UV, X-ray) have better resolution than visible light.

#### Review Questions
1. How does parabolic shape eliminate spherical aberration?
2. Why do larger telescopes have better resolution?
3. Explain astigmatism and when it appears.

## Subtopic: Refraction & Snell's Law

### Snell's Law & Refraction
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** snells-law-refraction
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** refractive index · Snell's law · critical angle · total internal reflection
**Summary:** Light bends when crossing a boundary between media with different refractive indices; Snell's law quantifies this bending.

#### Refractive Index & Snell's Law
The refractive index of a medium is n = c/v, where c is the speed of light in vacuum (3 × 10⁸ m/s) and v is the speed in the medium. Vacuum has n = 1; air ≈ 1.0003 ≈ 1; water ≈ 1.33; glass ≈ 1.5–1.9. Snell's law states: n₁ sin(θ₁) = n₂ sin(θ₂), where θ₁ and θ₂ are the incident and refracted angles (measured from the normal). When light enters a denser medium (n₂ > n₁), it bends toward the normal (θ₂ < θ₁). When entering a less dense medium, it bends away.

#### Critical Angle & Total Internal Reflection
Light traveling from a denser to a less dense medium can undergo total internal reflection. The critical angle θ_c occurs when sin(θ_c) = n₂/n₁. For angles greater than θ_c, all light is reflected (none is transmitted). For example, water to air: sin(θ_c) = 1/1.33, so θ_c ≈ 48.8°. Light in water at incident angles > 48.8° undergoes total internal reflection. This principle is used in optical fibers (light trapped by total internal reflection).

#### Examples of Refraction
- A fish in water appears to be in a shallower location than it really is (water refracts light away from normal as it exits).
- A straw in water appears bent at the surface.
- Mirages form when light refracts through layers of air at different temperatures.
- Rainbows form by dispersion: different wavelengths refract at slightly different angles.

#### Worked Example: Snell's Law Calculation
Light in water (n = 1.33) hits a glass surface (n = 1.5) at incident angle 40°. What is the refracted angle? Using n₁ sin(θ₁) = n₂ sin(θ₂): 1.33 sin(40°) ≈ 1.33 × 0.643 ≈ 0.855 = 1.5 sin(θ₂). So sin(θ₂) ≈ 0.570, giving θ₂ ≈ 34.7°. The light bends toward the normal (since the glass is denser).

#### Review Questions
1. Light hits a glass surface (n = 1.5) from air at 30°. What is the refracted angle?
2. What is the critical angle for total internal reflection from water (n = 1.33) to air?
3. Why does a swimming pool appear shallower than it is?

### Dispersion & Prisms
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** dispersion-prisms
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** dispersion · wavelength dependence · prisms · spectroscopy · rainbows
**Summary:** Different wavelengths of light refract at slightly different angles, causing dispersion that separates white light into its component colors.

#### Refractive Index & Wavelength
The refractive index of a material depends on wavelength: n(λ). Generally, shorter wavelengths (blue) have higher n than longer wavelengths (red). This wavelength dependence is dispersion. When white light enters a dispersive medium, different colors refract at different angles, separating the beam into its constituent colors.

#### Prisms
A prism is a transparent object with flat surfaces at an angle. White light entering the prism refracts at the first surface, separates by wavelength inside, and refracts again at the second surface. The result is a spectrum of colors. The amount of separation (angular dispersion) depends on the prism's apex angle and the wavelength dependence of the material's refractive index.

#### Rainbows
A rainbow forms when sunlight enters water droplets in the atmosphere. Light refracts into the droplet, reflects internally, and refracts out. Different wavelengths refract at different angles, producing the spectrum. The primary rainbow (one internal reflection) is more intense and shows red on the outside, violet on the inside. The secondary rainbow (two internal reflections) is dimmer and shows reversed colors.

#### Spectroscopy
By passing light through a prism or grating, scientists can separate light into its components and measure intensities at different wavelengths. Spectroscopy reveals the composition of materials, temperatures of stars, and velocities of galaxies (via Doppler shift). Absorption spectra show which wavelengths are absorbed; emission spectra show which wavelengths are emitted.

#### Worked Example: Prism Angle
White light hits a prism with apex angle 60° and refractive index n = 1.5 (for the mean wavelength). At the first surface, light from air (n₁ = 1) enters at incident angle 45°. Using Snell's law: sin(45°) = 1.5 sin(θ_refracted). So sin(θ_refracted) ≈ 0.707/1.5 ≈ 0.471, giving θ_refracted ≈ 28.1°. This example shows how to trace a ray through a prism.

#### Review Questions
1. Why are shorter wavelengths refracted more than longer wavelengths?
2. Explain the formation of a rainbow using refraction and dispersion.
3. How is spectroscopy used to identify the composition of a star?

### Lenses
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** lenses
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** convex lens · concave lens · lens equation · magnification · focal length
**Summary:** Lenses refract light to focus or diverge it; converging (convex) lenses form real or virtual images depending on object distance, while diverging (concave) lenses always form virtual images.

#### Lens Types & Focal Length
A converging (convex) lens is thicker at the center and focuses parallel rays to a focal point at distance f from the lens. A diverging (concave) lens is thinner at the center and makes parallel rays appear to diverge from a virtual focal point. The focal length is related to radius of curvature and refractive index by the lensmaker's equation: 1/f = (n − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂), where R₁ and R₂ are the radii of the two surfaces.

#### Lens Equation
The thin-lens equation is identical in form to the mirror equation: 1/d_o + 1/d_i = 1/f. For a converging lens (f > 0), a real object (d_o > 0) can form a real (d_i > 0) or virtual (d_i < 0) image depending on d_o. If d_o > f, the image is real, inverted, at distance d_i = (d_o · f)/(d_o − f). If d_o < f, the image is virtual, upright, at distance d_i = −|d_i|.

#### Magnification
The magnification m = −d_i/d_o gives the size and orientation. For a converging lens with d_o > f, m < 0 (inverted image). For d_o < f, m > 0 (virtual, upright image, magnified). For a diverging lens, m is always positive (upright) and |m| < 1 (reduced). The image size is h_i = m · h_o.

#### Lens Combinations
Multiple lenses in sequence each refract the light. If lenses are in contact (separation ≈ 0), the effective power is the sum: 1/f_eff = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂. This allows construction of systems with desired focal lengths and magnifications. Telescopes, microscopes, and compound lenses use multiple elements.

#### Review Questions
1. A converging lens has focal length 20 cm. An object 60 cm away forms an image where, and is it real or virtual?
2. A diverging lens always forms what type of image?
3. Two converging lenses (f₁ = 10 cm, f₂ = 15 cm) are placed in contact. What is the effective focal length?
