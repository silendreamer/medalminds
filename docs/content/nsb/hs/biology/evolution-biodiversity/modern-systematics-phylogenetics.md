#### Cladistics
Cladistics groups organisms by shared derived characters (**synapomorphies**) — traits inherited from a common ancestor that are not present in more ancient ancestors. A **clade** (or monophyletic group) includes an ancestor and all of its descendants. A **paraphyletic** group excludes some descendants (e.g., traditional "Reptilia" excludes birds, which are technically reptiles). A **polyphyletic** group includes organisms from different lineages united by convergent evolution (e.g., "warm-blooded animals" would group birds with mammals, but these evolved endothermy independently).

#### Reading Phylogenetic Trees
Each **node** (branching point) represents a common ancestor. The **root** is the oldest ancestor. **Sister taxa** are the two groups that diverged most recently from a shared node. **Outgroup**: a lineage that diverged before the group of interest, used to determine which characters are ancestral vs. derived. Branch lengths may represent time or amount of molecular change. A flat phylogenetic tree is an unrooted **cladogram**; a scaled tree with branch lengths is a **phylogram**.

#### Key Terms
- Synapomorphy: shared derived character defining a clade
- Plesiomorphy: ancestral character (not useful for cladistics)
- Homoplasy: similarity due to convergent evolution, not shared ancestry — misleads cladistic analysis
- Parsimony principle: the phylogeny requiring the fewest evolutionary changes is preferred

2. Why is "Reptilia" (as traditionally defined, excluding birds) considered paraphyletic?
3. On a phylogenetic tree, what does a node represent, and what does a branch length represent?
