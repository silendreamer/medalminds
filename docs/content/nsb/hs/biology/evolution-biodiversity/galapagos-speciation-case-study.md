#### Darwin's Finches: Adaptive Radiation
A single ancestral finch species colonized the Galápagos ~2–3 million years ago. The founding population was small (founder effect) and encountered diverse, unoccupied niches on multiple islands. Through allopatric speciation on different islands, 18 species evolved — each adapted to different food sources. Ground finches (genus Geospiza) crack seeds; warbler finches (Certhidea) glean insects; woodpecker finches (Camarhynchus) use tools (cactus spines) to probe bark; vampire finch (Geospiza septentrionalis) drinks blood from boobies. Beak morphology correlates precisely with diet. Peter and Rosemary Grant documented natural selection on beak size in real time — after the 1977 drought, large-beaked finches survived better because only large, hard seeds remained.

#### Character Displacement
When two closely related species compete for the same resources in the same area, selection favors individuals that use different resources — their phenotypes diverge. Galápagos ground finch beak sizes are more different on islands where both species co-occur than on islands with only one species. This competition-driven divergence is character displacement and can accelerate speciation.

#### Review Questions
1. Why did so many finch species evolve in the Galápagos rather than on nearby mainland South America?
2. After the 1977 Galápagos drought, average beak depth in Geospiza fortis increased. What does this demonstrate about evolution?
3. Define adaptive radiation and give one example other than Darwin's finches.
