#### Allopatric Speciation
The most common mode of speciation in animals. A geographic barrier (mountain range, river, ocean, glacier) physically separates a population into two isolated subpopulations. With no gene flow, populations diverge through genetic drift and different selective pressures. Given enough time, populations accumulate enough differences that they can no longer interbreed even if the barrier is removed. Evidence: closely related species often live on opposite sides of barriers (fish species pairs separated by the Isthmus of Panama, formed 3 million years ago when continents connected). Ring species (herring gulls around the Arctic) show that gradual geographic variation can produce reproductive isolation at the endpoints.

#### Sympatric Speciation
Speciation without geographic isolation — occurs within a single area. More controversial for animals; well-documented in plants through polyploidy. **Polyploidy** (whole-genome duplication) is the most common mode of plant speciation: a tetraploid plant (4n) cannot mate with its diploid (2n) parent — instant reproductive isolation. Allopolyploidy (hybridization between two species followed by genome doubling) created modern wheat (hexaploid, 6n = 42 chromosomes), cotton, and many other crop plants. Hawthorn flies (*Rhagoletis pomonella*) represent possible sympatric speciation in animals: some populations shifted from hawthorn to apple trees (introduced in 1800s) and now show temporal and behavioral isolation from hawthorn-feeding populations.

#### Review Questions
1. The Isthmus of Panama separated Caribbean and Pacific populations of marine organisms 3 million years ago. What type of speciation would have resulted, and what would you expect to find regarding species pairs today?
2. A tetraploid plant arises in a population of diploid plants. Explain why the tetraploid is immediately reproductively isolated from the parent population.
3. Why is sympatric speciation more commonly documented in plants than in animals?
