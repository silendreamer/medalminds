#### Why Most Aneuploidies Are Lethal
Most aneuploidies result in lethality in utero because of genetic imbalance — three copies of many genes (trisomy) or one copy (monosomy) disrupts development. The more genes in an imbalanced chromosome, the worse the prognosis. Humans have only a few viable trisomies because those chromosomes are small (few genes) or relatively gene-poor.

#### Viable Trisomies: Autosomes 13, 18, and 21
Trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome) and trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome) involve small chromosomes but are severe; infants often die within the first year. Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome) is more viable; life expectancy is now ~60 years. The genes on chromosome 21 don't cause as severe an imbalance as other trisomies. Importantly, all three trisomies can result from nondisjunction during meiosis in either parent, though maternal age increases risk (older eggs have more meiotic errors).

#### Down Syndrome (Trisomy 21) Phenotype and Genotype
Individuals with trisomy 21 have 47 chromosomes (three copies of chromosome 21). Common traits include intellectual disability (IQ typically 30–50 without intervention), characteristic facial features (epicanthal folds, flattened face), and cardiac defects. However, there is significant variability. Some individuals with Down syndrome have higher cognitive ability and can attend school, live semi-independently, and contribute meaningfully to their communities. Early intervention (speech, physical, occupational therapy) significantly improves outcomes.

#### Sex Chromosome Aneuploidies
Monosomy X (Turner syndrome) with 45 chromosomes is viable; individuals are typically female with short stature, ovarian dysgenesis, and sometimes cardiac or renal abnormalities. The viability of X monosomy reflects X-inactivation compensation in normal females — with only one X, the cell is naturally haploinsufficient. XXY (Klinefelter), XXX, and XYY individuals have variable phenotypes; some are phenotypically normal.

#### Mosaicism
Mosaicism occurs when an individual has two or more cell populations with different genotypes. This can arise from nondisjunction early in development (early = most cells affected; late = fewer cells affected) or from anaphase lag (chromosome loss). A mosaic trisomy 21 individual (mixture of diploid and trisomy 21 cells) may have milder phenotype than a full trisomy 21 individual. Mosaicism can mask karyotype if a sample happens to include only normal cells.

#### Maternal Age and Trisomy Risk
Trisomy risk increases with maternal age, particularly after age 35. At age 20, the risk of Down syndrome is ~1/1500; at age 45, it's ~1/30. This age effect is thought to reflect the prolonged arrest in prophase I — oocytes spend decades in meiosis I before ovulation, and this long wait increases the likelihood of errors in spindle formation or cohesin degradation.
