#### What Are Sister Chromatids?
During S phase of interphase, DNA replication creates two identical copies of each chromosome, still joined at the centromere. These copies are sister chromatids — genetically identical until crossing over occurs. Under the microscope at metaphase, a single chromosome appears as an X shape: two sister chromatids held together by the centromere. Both sister chromatids carry the same alleles until they separate during anaphase II of meiosis.

#### Homologous vs Sister Chromatids
Homologous chromosomes are a pair — one from each parent. They're similar in size and gene order but carry different alleles (versions of genes). Sister chromatids, by contrast, are identical copies made by DNA replication. During meiosis I, homologous pairs separate. During meiosis II, sister chromatids separate. This two-step separation is the core of meiosis.

#### The Centromere's Role
The centromere is the constricted region where sister chromatids attach and where kinetochore proteins bind to spindle fibers. It's not a gene — it's a structural anchor. The strength of this attachment ensures sister chromatids don't separate prematurely. When spindle fibers pull, they pull at the kinetochores, not the DNA itself.

#### Telomeres and Chromosome Stability
Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences at chromosome ends that protect coding genes from erosion during replication. They shorten with each cell division. In somatic cells, telomerase is usually inactive; telomeres eventually shorten until the cell stops dividing (Hayflick limit, ~50 divisions). Germ cells (egg and sperm precursors) and cancer cells express active telomerase to maintain telomere length.
