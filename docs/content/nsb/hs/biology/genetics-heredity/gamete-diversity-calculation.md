#### Calculating Genetic Diversity from Independent Assortment
Humans have 23 chromosome pairs. During meiosis I, each pair orients randomly at the metaphase plate. The number of possible combinations from orientation alone is 2^23 ≈ 8.4 million. A single male's sperm are not all identical; they differ in which alleles segregate together. This diversity is independent assortment — Mendel's second law.

#### Crossing Over Multiplies Diversity
Crossing over happens during prophase I at multiple sites on each chromosome. Each crossover exchanges alleles between homologous chromosomes. The frequency of crossing over between two loci depends on their distance: loci farther apart have more crossovers between them. This creates even more gamete diversity than independent assortment alone.

#### Worked Example: Mouse Genetics
A mouse heterozygous for 5 unlinked genes (AaBbCcDdEe) produces gametes through independent assortment. Without crossing over, the number of possible gamete types is 2^5 = 32 (one for each combination of dominant/recessive alleles). With crossing over near each locus, the number increases. If each locus has, say, a 1% chance of a crossover between it and the next locus, roughly 32 × (small multiplier) types appear.

#### Why Siblings Are Genetically Unique
Two offspring of the same parents can have very different genotypes. Each parent produces millions of genetically distinct gametes via independent assortment and crossing over. The probability that two siblings inherit the exact same set of alleles for all genes is extraordinarily low (except for identical twins, who arise from a single zygote, not two independent gametes).
