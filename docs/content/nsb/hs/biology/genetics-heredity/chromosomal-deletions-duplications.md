#### Deletions: Loss of Genetic Material
A deletion is a segment of chromosome that is lost or removed. If a chromosome loses a segment containing genes, those genes are absent. A person with a deletion is monosomic for that region (one copy instead of the normal two). Deletions are usually harmful because they remove essential genes. Small deletions might be tolerated; large deletions are often lethal. Wolf-Hirschhorn syndrome results from a deletion of 4p (short arm of chromosome 4); it causes intellectual disability and growth retardation.

#### Duplications: Extra Genetic Material
A duplication is a segment of chromosome that is repeated. A person with a duplication has three copies of that region (trisomy for that segment) — one from each homologous chromosome, plus an extra copy. Duplications can arise from unequal crossing over (where sister chromatids or homologous chromosomes misalign and exchange unequal segments). A duplication is generally less harmful than a deletion because the cell has functional copies of the genes, just extra ones, though overexpression can be problematic.

#### Unequal Crossing Over
If two homologous chromosomes misalign during prophase I, crossing over can be unequal. One recombinant chromosome gets a duplication of one segment and a deletion of another. The other recombinant loses that segment and gains a duplicate elsewhere. If a chromosome has tandem repeats (same sequence repeated next to each other), unequal crossing over can expand or contract the repeat number, leading to duplications or deletions.

#### Pseudoautosomal Regions
The X and Y chromosomes have small regions of homology called pseudoautosomal regions (PARs), where they can undergo crossing over during meiosis. Genes in these regions behave like autosomal genes, not sex-linked. Unequal crossing over in PARs can lead to XY XX or XYY XY gametes, causing sex chromosome aneuploidy.

#### Fragile Sites and Chromosome Instability
Fragile X syndrome involves an expansion of a CGG trinucleotide repeat at a fragile site on the X chromosome. Normal individuals have 5–44 repeats; affected individuals have >200 repeats. The expansion causes gene silencing (methylation) and intellectual disability. The repeat number can expand when passed from parent to child, a phenomenon called genetic anticipation.
