#### Prophase I: Crossing Over Happens Here
Homologous chromosomes pair up in a process called synapsis. While paired, they exchange genetic material at points called chiasmata — this is crossing over (recombination). A single crossover between two loci exchanges alleles between homologous chromosomes. The frequency of crossing over between two genes is proportional to their distance apart, which geneticists use to build linkage maps. Prophase I is longer and more complex than prophase II.

#### Metaphase I and the Key Decision
Homologous pairs line up at the metaphase plate, not individual chromosomes. The orientation of each pair is random — either the maternal or paternal chromosome of a pair can face either pole. This random orientation alone generates 2^n combinations (where n = number of chromosome pairs). In humans, that's 2^23 ≈ 8 million possible combinations from orientation alone.

#### Anaphase I: The Reductional Step
Homologous chromosomes separate — not sister chromatids. Each pole receives one chromosome from each homologous pair. If you start with 46 chromosomes (23 pairs), you end with 23 at each pole. Each chromosome still consists of two sister chromatids held together.

#### Telophase I and Cytokinesis
After anaphase I separation, a nuclear envelope reforms and cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm. Two haploid cells result, each with half the original chromosome number. Importantly, the sister chromatids are NOT separated yet. A brief interkinesis (interphase-like stage) follows, but typically without DNA replication.
