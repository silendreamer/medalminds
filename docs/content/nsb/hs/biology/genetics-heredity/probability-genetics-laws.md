#### Rule of Multiplication
If two events are independent, the probability both occur is the product of their individual probabilities. For a dihybrid cross, if the probability of A_ at one locus is 3/4 and B_ at another is 3/4, the probability of A_B_ is 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16. This applies because the alleles at different loci segregate independently. The multiplication rule is key to predicting multi-locus crosses.

#### Rule of Addition
If two events are mutually exclusive (cannot both happen), the probability that one or the other occurs is the sum of their individual probabilities. For a monohybrid cross (Aa × Aa), the probability of a heterozygous offspring (Aa) is 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2, since the child could inherit A from mother and a from father, or a from mother and A from father. These are two mutually exclusive ways to be Aa.

#### Conditional Probability and Pedigrees
If a woman's brother has an autosomal recessive disease, we know both parents are carriers (Aa). Given this information, what is the probability the woman is a carrier? She could be AA (1/4 chance among siblings), Aa (1/2 chance), or aa (1/4 chance, but she's unaffected, so ruled out). Among unaffected siblings, she is AA with probability 1/3 or Aa with probability 2/3. This conditional probability changes based on additional information.

#### Worked Example: Triple Heterozygote
An organism heterozygous at three independent loci (AaBbCc) is self-crossed. What is the probability of an aabbcc offspring? Each locus segregates independently: P(aa) = 1/4, P(bb) = 1/4, P(cc) = 1/4. By the multiplication rule, P(aabbcc) = 1/4 × 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/64. Among 192 offspring, expect ~3 aabbcc individuals.

#### Probability and Expected vs Observed
Probability predicts the expected ratio, not the observed ratio in any small sample. If a monohybrid cross is expected to produce 1:1 (dominant:recessive), a sample of 10 offspring might show 7:3 due to chance. A sample of 1000 is more likely to show ~500:500. Larger samples better reflect the underlying probability.

#### Review Questions
1. In a dihybrid cross (AaBb × AaBb), use the multiplication rule to calculate the probability of aabb offspring.
2. A woman's father has a recessive genetic disease (aa). The disease is rare, so her mother is most likely AA. What is the probability that the woman (who is unaffected) is a carrier (Aa)?
3. If you perform a monohybrid cross (Aa × Aa) and predict a 1:1 ratio but observe 12 dominant and 8 recessive in your sample of 20, does this result contradict the 1:1 prediction? Why or why not?
