## Subtopic: Meiosis & Chromosome Behavior

### DNA Replication and Chromosome Structure
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** dna-replication-chromosome-structure
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** DNA replication · semiconservative replication · sister chromatids · homologous chromosomes
**Summary:** DNA copies itself before meiosis to create sister chromatids attached at the centromere; homologous pairs align during meiosis I.

#### What Are Sister Chromatids?
During S phase of interphase, DNA replication creates two identical copies of each chromosome, still joined at the centromere. These copies are sister chromatids — genetically identical until crossing over occurs. Under the microscope at metaphase, a single chromosome appears as an X shape: two sister chromatids held together by the centromere. Both sister chromatids carry the same alleles until they separate during anaphase II of meiosis.

#### Homologous vs Sister Chromatids
Homologous chromosomes are a pair — one from each parent. They're similar in size and gene order but carry different alleles (versions of genes). Sister chromatids, by contrast, are identical copies made by DNA replication. During meiosis I, homologous pairs separate. During meiosis II, sister chromatids separate. This two-step separation is the core of meiosis.

#### The Centromere's Role
The centromere is the constricted region where sister chromatids attach and where kinetochore proteins bind to spindle fibers. It's not a gene — it's a structural anchor. The strength of this attachment ensures sister chromatids don't separate prematurely. When spindle fibers pull, they pull at the kinetochores, not the DNA itself.

#### Telomeres and Chromosome Stability
Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences at chromosome ends that protect coding genes from erosion during replication. They shorten with each cell division. In somatic cells, telomerase is usually inactive; telomeres eventually shorten until the cell stops dividing (Hayflick limit, ~50 divisions). Germ cells (egg and sperm precursors) and cancer cells express active telomerase to maintain telomere length.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between sister chromatids and homologous chromosomes?
2. At what stage of meiosis do sister chromatids separate?
3. Why do telomeres shorten with each cell division in somatic cells?

### Meiosis I: Reduction Division
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** meiosis-i-reduction-division
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** prophase I · crossing over · metaphase I · anaphase I · reductional division
**Summary:** Meiosis I is the reductional division: homologous pairs separate, cutting the chromosome number in half.

#### Prophase I: Crossing Over Happens Here
Homologous chromosomes pair up in a process called synapsis. While paired, they exchange genetic material at points called chiasmata — this is crossing over (recombination). A single crossover between two loci exchanges alleles between homologous chromosomes. The frequency of crossing over between two genes is proportional to their distance apart, which geneticists use to build linkage maps. Prophase I is longer and more complex than prophase II.

#### Metaphase I and the Key Decision
Homologous pairs line up at the metaphase plate, not individual chromosomes. The orientation of each pair is random — either the maternal or paternal chromosome of a pair can face either pole. This random orientation alone generates 2^n combinations (where n = number of chromosome pairs). In humans, that's 2^23 ≈ 8 million possible combinations from orientation alone.

#### Anaphase I: The Reductional Step
Homologous chromosomes separate — not sister chromatids. Each pole receives one chromosome from each homologous pair. If you start with 46 chromosomes (23 pairs), you end with 23 at each pole. Each chromosome still consists of two sister chromatids held together.

#### Telophase I and Cytokinesis
After anaphase I separation, a nuclear envelope reforms and cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm. Two haploid cells result, each with half the original chromosome number. Importantly, the sister chromatids are NOT separated yet. A brief interkinesis (interphase-like stage) follows, but typically without DNA replication.

#### Review Questions
1. What is crossing over, and when does it occur during meiosis?
2. Why is anaphase I called the "reductional" step?
3. How many possible orientations can a pair of homologous chromosomes take at metaphase I in a cell with 20 chromosome pairs?

### Meiosis II: Separation of Sister Chromatids
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** meiosis-ii-sister-chromatid-separation
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** meiosis II · sister chromatid separation · anaphase II · four haploid gametes
**Summary:** Meiosis II separates sister chromatids; it's similar to mitosis but starts with haploid cells.

#### Prophase II to Metaphase II
Meiosis II resembles mitosis. The nuclear envelope breaks down; spindles form. Individual chromosomes (each still as two sister chromatids) line up at the metaphase plate. This is essentially mitosis — but the starting cell is already haploid (n), not diploid (2n).

#### Anaphase II: Sister Chromatids Finally Separate
Cohesin proteins holding sister chromatids together are cleaved. Sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles. After this anaphase, daughter cells have single-stranded chromosomes (one allele per locus). The cell now has one copy of each chromosome.

#### Telophase II and Cytokinesis
Nuclear envelopes reform around the newly separated chromosomes. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm. The result: four haploid daughter cells, each genetically unique due to crossing over and independent assortment. A single diploid cell that enters meiosis produces four non-identical gametes.

#### Why Two Divisions?
Meiosis I reduces chromosome number via homologous separation; meiosis II reduces chromatid number via sister chromatid separation. If cells just did sister chromatid separation without first reducing homologous pairs, diploid cells would stay diploid. The two-step process is essential for sexual reproduction.

#### Review Questions
1. How is anaphase II similar to and different from mitotic anaphase?
2. Why does meiosis II produce four haploid cells from one diploid cell?
3. At the start of meiosis II, is each chromosome single-stranded or double-stranded? Why?

### Nondisjunction and Aneuploidy
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** nondisjunction-aneuploidy
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** nondisjunction · aneuploidy · trisomy · monosomy · Down syndrome
**Summary:** When chromosomes fail to separate during meiosis, gametes receive too many or too few copies, causing aneuploidy.

#### What Is Nondisjunction?
Nondisjunction is the failure of chromosomes or chromatids to separate during cell division. It can occur in meiosis I (homologous pairs don't separate) or meiosis II (sister chromatids don't separate). The result: one gamete gets two copies of a chromosome; the other gets zero. Upon fertilization, the zygote is aneuploid — not 2n.

#### Trisomy and Monosomy
Trisomy = three copies of one chromosome (2n + 1). Down syndrome (trisomy 21) is the most common autosomal trisomy compatible with life; individuals have three copies of chromosome 21. Monosomy = one copy (2n - 1). Autosomal monosomies are usually lethal in utero. Monosomy X (Turner syndrome) is an exception, sometimes viable in females with mosaic patterns.

#### Sex-Linked Aneuploidy
Aneuploidy of sex chromosomes is often viable because the Y carries few genes and X-inactivation compensates for multiple X copies. XXY (Klinefelter), XXX, and XYY are examples. These syndromes can have variable phenotypes depending on the number of Y chromosomes and X-inactivation patterns.

#### Down Syndrome: Life Expectancy and Genetics
Trisomy 21 individuals have three copies of chromosome 21, giving them 47 chromosomes total. Common traits include intellectual disability, distinct facial features, and heart defects. However, life expectancy has increased dramatically (now ~60 years) with medical support. It's important to recognize that trisomy 21 individuals have high capacity for learning and contribution to society.

#### Review Questions
1. What is nondisjunction, and how does it lead to aneuploidy?
2. Why are monosomies of autosomes usually lethal while monosomy X can be viable?
3. How many chromosomes does a person with Down syndrome have in each cell?

### Meiosis Application: Gamete Diversity
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** gamete-diversity-calculation
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** independent assortment · crossing over · genetic variation · combinatorial diversity
**Summary:** Independent assortment of chromosomes and crossing over ensure no two gametes (except identical twins) are exactly alike.

#### Calculating Genetic Diversity from Independent Assortment
Humans have 23 chromosome pairs. During meiosis I, each pair orients randomly at the metaphase plate. The number of possible combinations from orientation alone is 2^23 ≈ 8.4 million. A single male's sperm are not all identical; they differ in which alleles segregate together. This diversity is independent assortment — Mendel's second law.

#### Crossing Over Multiplies Diversity
Crossing over happens during prophase I at multiple sites on each chromosome. Each crossover exchanges alleles between homologous chromosomes. The frequency of crossing over between two loci depends on their distance: loci farther apart have more crossovers between them. This creates even more gamete diversity than independent assortment alone.

#### Worked Example: Mouse Genetics
A mouse heterozygous for 5 unlinked genes (AaBbCcDdEe) produces gametes through independent assortment. Without crossing over, the number of possible gamete types is 2^5 = 32 (one for each combination of dominant/recessive alleles). With crossing over near each locus, the number increases. If each locus has, say, a 1% chance of a crossover between it and the next locus, roughly 32 × (small multiplier) types appear.

#### Why Siblings Are Genetically Unique
Two offspring of the same parents can have very different genotypes. Each parent produces millions of genetically distinct gametes via independent assortment and crossing over. The probability that two siblings inherit the exact same set of alleles for all genes is extraordinarily low (except for identical twins, who arise from a single zygote, not two independent gametes).

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate the number of possible gamete types (genotypes) for an organism heterozygous at 4 unlinked loci.
2. Why does crossing over increase gamete diversity beyond what independent assortment alone generates?
3. For two siblings to be genetically identical, what would have to happen?

### Meiosis Application: Linkage Mapping
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** linkage-mapping-recombination
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** recombination frequency · linkage groups · genetic map units · crossover distance
**Summary:** The frequency of recombination between two genes reflects their distance on a chromosome; geneticists use this to map genes.

#### Recombination Frequency as Distance
Two genes on the same chromosome are linked — they tend to be inherited together. But crossing over can separate them. The recombination frequency (RF) is the percentage of offspring that show a recombinant phenotype (a new combination of alleles not seen in either parent). RF is proportional to the distance between genes: RF = (number of recombinants / total offspring) × 100%.

#### Mapping Units (centiMorgans)
1 map unit (m.u.) = 1 centiMorgan (cM) = 1% recombination frequency. If genes A and B show 5% RF, they are 5 m.u. apart. This unit is based on the assumption that crossing over occurs randomly and that one crossover event between two loci is observable. For genes far apart on the same chromosome, interference (one crossover inhibits nearby crossovers) can reduce observed RF.

#### Three-Point Cross Example
To map three genes (A, B, C) on a chromosome, researchers perform a three-point cross: cross a triple heterozygote (AaBbCc) with a homozygous recessive (aabbcc). Examine offspring phenotypes. Parental types appear most frequently (no crossing over between any pair). Single crossovers between A–B are next most frequent, then single crossovers between B–C. Double crossovers (between both pairs) are rarest. Use the data to calculate RF between each pair and determine gene order.

#### Worked Example: Fruit Fly Mapping
In *Drosophila*, researchers cross white-eyed, vestigial-winged (wvg) females (heterozygous) with white, wild-type males (wvg+). They observe offspring and count phenotype frequencies: If wild-type eyes/vestigial wings appears 40 times and white eyes/wild-type wings 38 times (recombinant classes), with 100 total offspring, RF = (40+38)/100 = 78%. The genes are 78 m.u. apart — far enough that crossing over occurs almost half the time, so they assort nearly independently.

#### Review Questions
1. If two genes show 12% recombination frequency, how far apart are they on the chromosome?
2. In a three-point cross, which classes of offspring appear most frequently, and why?
3. Why is interference important when mapping genes far apart on the same chromosome?

### Meiosis Application: Human Pedigree Analysis
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** human-pedigree-meiotic-inheritance
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** pedigree notation · autosomal vs sex-linked · carrier status · inheritance patterns
**Summary:** Human pedigrees show how alleles segregate across generations; meiosis ensures each parent contributes one allele per locus.

#### Reading a Pedigree
Squares = males; circles = females. Filled symbols = affected individuals; unfilled = unaffected; half-filled = carriers (heterozygotes who don't show the phenotype). Horizontal line connects a mating pair; vertical lines descend to offspring. Siblings are drawn on a sibship line (horizontal line above them).

#### Autosomal Dominant vs Recessive Patterns
Autosomal dominant: affected individuals can have one mutant allele (Aa). Unaffected parents (aa) can have affected children only if both are carriers — impossible for autosomal dominant (unless a new mutation). Vertical transmission is typical; affected individuals often have affected parents. Autosomal recessive: affected individuals are homozygous (aa). Unaffected parents (Aa × Aa, or Aa × aa) can have affected children. Horizontal transmission is common (siblings affected, parents unaffected).

#### Sex-Linked (X-Linked) Patterns
In X-linked recessive inheritance, males are more frequently affected (XaY); females must be homozygous (XaXa) to show the phenotype. An affected male (XaY) has an unaffected mother (XAXa, carrier). The mother passes her Xa to all daughters (all carriers) and her XA to all sons (all unaffected). Affected females are rare and usually result from an affected father (XaY) and carrier mother (XAXa).

#### Worked Pedigree: Hemophilia
Hemophilia A is X-linked recessive. A carrier mother (XHXh) and unaffected father (XHY) have children: sons have a 50% chance of hemophilia (XhY), daughters have a 50% chance of being carriers (XHXh). If a hemophilic male (XhY) mates with an unaffected, non-carrier female (XHX H), all daughters are carriers (XHXh) — they inherited Xh from father and XH from mother — but no daughters are affected. All sons are unaffected.

#### Using Pedigrees to Predict Inheritance
To predict the probability a future child will have a recessive trait, identify the carrier status of both parents. If both parents are Aa, the child has a 1/4 chance of aa. If one parent is Aa and the other is aa, the child has a 1/2 chance. If one or both parents are AA, the child cannot be aa. For X-linked traits, consider the mother's and father's genotypes separately and use a Punnett square for X.

#### Review Questions
1. In an autosomal dominant pedigree, can two unaffected parents have an affected child? Why or why not?
2. A hemophilic male (XhY) has a daughter with an unaffected female. What is the genotype and phenotype of their daughters?
3. If a woman is a carrier of an autosomal recessive trait and her partner is unaffected and non-carrier, what is the probability their child is affected?

## Subtopic: Mendelian Genetics & Probability

### Alleles, Genotype, and Phenotype
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** alleles-genotype-phenotype
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** allele · dominant · recessive · genotype · phenotype · heterozygous · homozygous
**Summary:** An allele is a variant form of a gene; genotype is the pair of alleles; phenotype is the observable trait.

#### Genes and Alleles
A gene is a stretch of DNA coding for a protein (or regulatory RNA). At each locus on a chromosome, there can be multiple alleles — different versions of that gene. The ABO blood type locus, for example, has three alleles: A, B, and O. Hemoglobin S (sickle-cell) and hemoglobin A (normal) are two alleles at the beta-globin locus.

#### Dominant and Recessive Alleles
An allele is dominant if its phenotype appears in heterozygotes (one copy is enough). An allele is recessive if the phenotype only appears in homozygotes (two copies required). If a tall pea plant (T_) crossed with a short plant (tt) produces all tall offspring, then the tall allele is dominant and the short allele is recessive. The heterozygous (Tt) tall plant still produces tall offspring — the tall phenotype masks the presence of the recessive allele.

#### Homozygous and Heterozygous
Homozygous = both alleles at a locus are the same (AA or aa). A homozygous dominant individual (AA) has two copies of the dominant allele. A homozygous recessive individual (aa) expresses the recessive phenotype. Heterozygous = the two alleles are different (Aa). If the trait is dominant, a heterozygote shows the dominant phenotype but carries and can pass the recessive allele to offspring.

#### Genotype vs Phenotype
Genotype is the genetic makeup — the pair of alleles (AA, Aa, or aa). Phenotype is the observable or measurable trait. A plant with genotype Aa for the color locus might be red (if A is dominant over a). Its phenotype is red; its genotype is Aa. Two organisms with different genotypes (AA and Aa) can have the same phenotype (both red) if A is completely dominant.

#### Incomplete Dominance and Codominance
In some cases, heterozygotes show an intermediate phenotype — this is incomplete dominance. Red × white snapdragons produce pink heterozygotes. In codominance, both alleles are equally expressed. AB blood type individuals (IAIB genotype) express both A and B antigens; neither is dominant.

#### Review Questions
1. Define the terms allele, genotype, and phenotype, using a specific example.
2. A plant is homozygous dominant for flower color (RR, red). What is its genotype and phenotype?
3. If a heterozygous tall plant (Tt) crosses with a homozygous recessive short plant (tt), what is the probability of tall offspring?

### Punnett Squares and Test Crosses
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** punnett-squares-test-crosses
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** monohybrid cross · test cross · Punnett square · 1:1 ratio · 1:2:1 ratio
**Summary:** Punnett squares visually represent crosses and predict offspring ratios; test crosses reveal hidden recessive alleles.

#### Monohybrid Cross: Aa × Aa
A monohybrid cross involves one locus. Cross a heterozygous plant (Aa) with another heterozygote (Aa). Draw a 2×2 Punnett square. Parent gametes on each axis are A or a. Each cell represents a possible offspring genotype: AA, Aa, Aa, or aa. The predicted ratio is 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa. Phenotypically, if A is dominant, the ratio is 3 dominant (A_) : 1 recessive (aa). This 3:1 ratio is a hallmark of a monohybrid cross between two heterozygotes.

#### Test Cross: A_ × aa
To determine if a dominant-phenotype individual is AA or Aa, perform a test cross: mate the unknown with a homozygous recessive (aa). If the unknown is AA, all offspring are Aa (all dominant phenotype). If the unknown is Aa, offspring are 1 Aa (dominant) : 1 aa (recessive) — a 1:1 ratio. A 1:1 ratio immediately reveals the unknown individual is heterozygous. A 3:1 ratio never appears in a test cross.

#### Dihybrid Cross: AaBb × AaBb
A dihybrid cross involves two loci. Use a 4×4 Punnett square. Each parent can contribute AB, Ab, aB, or ab. Nineten cells in the grid represent possible offspring. If both loci are on different chromosomes (independent assortment), the phenotypic ratio is 9 A_B_ : 3 A_bb : 3 aaB_ : 1 aabb (9:3:3:1). This ratio reveals Mendel's law of independent assortment.

#### Chi-Square Test for Goodness of Fit
Observed offspring ratios rarely match predicted ratios exactly due to chance. The chi-square (χ²) test assesses whether observed data differ significantly from expected data. χ² = Σ[(Observed − Expected)² / Expected]. Compare χ² to a critical value; if χ² is small, data fit the hypothesis. This statistical tool determines if deviations are random variation or evidence of a different genetic model.

#### Worked Example: Corn Genetics
A corn plant with purple kernels (A_) is crossed with a plant with yellow kernels (aa). If the purple plant is AA, all offspring are Aa (all purple). If the purple plant is Aa, offspring are 1 Aa (purple) : 1 aa (yellow). Grow 100 seeds and count: if you observe ~50 purple and ~50 yellow, the original plant was Aa. If all are purple, it was AA.

#### Review Questions
1. Draw a Punnett square for a monohybrid cross (Tt × Tt). What are the genotypic and phenotypic ratios?
2. Why is a test cross useful for determining if an individual showing a dominant phenotype is homozygous or heterozygous?
3. In a dihybrid cross (AaBb × AaBb), what is the expected frequency of aabb offspring?

### Probability in Genetics
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** probability-genetics-laws
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** rule of multiplication · rule of addition · independent events · conditional probability
**Summary:** Probability rules quantify genetic predictions; multiplication rule applies to independent events; addition rule to mutually exclusive events.

#### Rule of Multiplication
If two events are independent, the probability both occur is the product of their individual probabilities. For a dihybrid cross, if the probability of A_ at one locus is 3/4 and B_ at another is 3/4, the probability of A_B_ is 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16. This applies because the alleles at different loci segregate independently. The multiplication rule is key to predicting multi-locus crosses.

#### Rule of Addition
If two events are mutually exclusive (cannot both happen), the probability that one or the other occurs is the sum of their individual probabilities. For a monohybrid cross (Aa × Aa), the probability of a heterozygous offspring (Aa) is 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2, since the child could inherit A from mother and a from father, or a from mother and A from father. These are two mutually exclusive ways to be Aa.

#### Conditional Probability and Pedigrees
If a woman's brother has an autosomal recessive disease, we know both parents are carriers (Aa). Given this information, what is the probability the woman is a carrier? She could be AA (1/4 chance among siblings), Aa (1/2 chance), or aa (1/4 chance, but she's unaffected, so ruled out). Among unaffected siblings, she is AA with probability 1/3 or Aa with probability 2/3. This conditional probability changes based on additional information.

#### Worked Example: Triple Heterozygote
An organism heterozygous at three independent loci (AaBbCc) is self-crossed. What is the probability of an aabbcc offspring? Each locus segregates independently: P(aa) = 1/4, P(bb) = 1/4, P(cc) = 1/4. By the multiplication rule, P(aabbcc) = 1/4 × 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/64. Among 192 offspring, expect ~3 aabbcc individuals.

#### Probability and Expected vs Observed
Probability predicts the expected ratio, not the observed ratio in any small sample. If a monohybrid cross is expected to produce 1:1 (dominant:recessive), a sample of 10 offspring might show 7:3 due to chance. A sample of 1000 is more likely to show ~500:500. Larger samples better reflect the underlying probability.

#### Review Questions
1. In a dihybrid cross (AaBb × AaBb), use the multiplication rule to calculate the probability of aabb offspring.
2. A woman's father has a recessive genetic disease (aa). The disease is rare, so her mother is most likely AA. What is the probability that the woman (who is unaffected) is a carrier (Aa)?
3. If you perform a monohybrid cross (Aa × Aa) and predict a 1:1 ratio but observe 12 dominant and 8 recessive in your sample of 20, does this result contradict the 1:1 prediction? Why or why not?

### Non-Mendelian Inheritance Patterns
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** non-mendelian-inheritance-patterns
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** incomplete dominance · codominance · epistasis · polygenic traits · maternal inheritance
**Summary:** Many traits don't follow simple Mendelian patterns; multiple alleles, gene interactions, and non-nuclear genes complicate inheritance.

#### Incomplete Dominance
In some crosses, heterozygotes show an intermediate phenotype. Red snapdragons (RR) × white (WW) produce pink offspring (RW). The heterozygote is not red (dominant) but intermediate. This is incomplete dominance: the heterozygote phenotype is different from both homozygotes. The F1 × F1 cross (RW × RW) produces 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white in the F2, not the typical 3:1 Mendelian ratio.

#### Codominance
In codominance, both alleles are fully expressed in heterozygotes. Human ABO blood types: An individual with genotype IAIB has both A and B antigens (blood type AB). Neither allele is dominant; both are expressed equally. Compare this to incomplete dominance: a type AB person has a distinct phenotype (AB) that is not intermediate between A and B, but rather expresses both.

#### Epistasis: Gene Interactions
Epistasis occurs when one gene's alleles mask or modify the expression of alleles at another locus. Classic example: Labrador coat color. E_B_ = black, E_bb = chocolate, eeB_ = yellow, eebb = yellow. The ee genotype masks the B locus effect; all ee individuals are yellow regardless of genotype at the B locus. An F1 cross (EeBb × EeBb) produces 9 black : 3 chocolate : 4 yellow (not 9:3:3:1).

#### Polygenic Traits
Height, skin color, and intelligence are controlled by many genes (polygenes), each with small additive effects. A 3-locus polygenic trait (AaBbCc) crossed with itself produces offspring with a range of phenotypes, from aabbcc (minimum) to AABBCC (maximum). The distribution is often roughly normal (bell-curve). Environmental factors also influence the final phenotype.

#### Maternal Inheritance
Mitochondria and chloroplasts are inherited maternally (from the mother's egg cytoplasm, not from the nucleus). A mitochondrial mutation passed from mother to child will appear in all cells. An affected mother passes the mutation to all her children; an affected father does not pass it to any children. Mitochondrial genetic diseases show a distinctive pedigree pattern unlike autosomal or X-linked traits.

#### Review Questions
1. Distinguish between incomplete dominance and codominance, using an example for each.
2. In a Labrador coat color cross (EeBb × EeBb), what is the expected phenotypic ratio, and why?
3. If a mitochondrial disease is passed from mother to both her sons and daughters, can her sons pass it to their children?

### Mendelian Genetics Application: Probability Problems
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** mendelian-probability-application
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** compound probability · expected frequencies · genetic prediction
**Summary:** Multi-locus crosses require combining probabilities; predict offspring frequencies in complex genetic scenarios.

#### Three-Locus Cross with Partial Linkage
A trihybrid cross (AaBbCc) is performed. Suppose A and B are linked (10 m.u. apart) but C is unlinked. The gamete types from the AaBb parent are: AB and ab (parental, 45% each), Ab and aB (recombinant, 5% each). The C locus segregates independently (1/2 C, 1/2 c in all gametes). To find the frequency of A_B_cc offspring, multiply: P(A_B_) × P(cc) = (parental AB frequency from AaBb × AaBb cross) × 1/4.

#### Worked Example: Linked Genes
Genes A and B are 15 m.u. apart. A trihybrid (ABc/abc) × homozygous recessive (abc/abc) produces offspring. Parental gametes from the trihybrid (AB/abc) are AB and abc (42.5% each). Recombinant gametes are Ab and aB (7.5% each). The homozygous recessive contributes only abc. So offspring are: ABabc (42.5%), abcabc (42.5%), Ababc (7.5%), aBcabc (7.5%). Phenotypically, the ratio is 42.5 A_bc : 42.5 aabc : 7.5 A_BC : 7.5 aaBC (a modified 1:1 ratio due to linkage).

#### Recombination Frequency Unknown
Two genes show a 1:1 recombinant:parental ratio when crossed with a homozygous recessive. This indicates ~50% recombination, implying the genes are either on different chromosomes or very far apart on the same chromosome (>50 m.u.). If genes show a 3:1 parental:recombinant ratio, they are linked with ~25% recombination frequency.

#### Sex-Linked Probability
An X-linked trait is controlled by alleles X^A (normal) and X^a (disease). A carrier woman (X^A X^a) and normal man (X^A Y) have children. Sons: 1/2 X^A Y (normal) and 1/2 X^a Y (affected). Daughters: 1/2 X^A X^A (normal) and 1/2 X^A X^a (carrier). If the couple plans three children, the probability of at least one affected son is 1 − (1/2)^3 = 7/8.

#### Predicting Genetic Diseases in Offspring
A couple plans pregnancy. The man has no family history of cystic fibrosis (CF); the woman's brother has CF (autosomal recessive). The woman is definitely a carrier (Aa). The man is likely AA (since CF is rare, ~1/2500 births) but could be a carrier. The probability of an affected child depends on the man's actual genotype. If he is AA, no children are affected. If he is Aa (low probability), 1/2 of children are affected. Genetic counseling uses population frequencies to estimate these probabilities.

#### Review Questions
1. Two genes (D and E) show 8% recombination. A testcross of DdEe produces 200 offspring. How many should be double recombinants?
2. A woman is heterozygous for an autosomal recessive disease; her partner is unaffected but from a family with the disease. What is the probability their first child is affected?
3. In an X-linked recessive trait, a carrier woman and affected man have a daughter. What is the probability their daughter is affected?

### Mendelian Genetics Application: Corn and Fruit Flies
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** classical-genetics-model-organisms
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** model organisms · corn kernel color · *Drosophila* eye color · classical experiments
**Summary:** Corn and fruit flies were used in seminal genetic studies; their simple genetics and short generation times made them ideal subjects.

#### Corn (Maize) Genetics: Barbara McClintock
Barbara McClintock used corn to study transposable elements. She observed variegated kernel colors (red and purple spots on yellow or white background) caused by Ac/Ds elements (Activator/Dissociation transposons). When an Ac element is present, it can excise from the pigment gene (C), restoring function and producing red/purple color. When Ac is absent, the ds element remains inserted, disrupting the gene and producing the background color. Kernels show a spotted pattern depending on when excision occurred during kernel development — early excision = large colored sector, late = small dot.

#### Eye Color in *Drosophila*
The white gene (w) in *Drosophila* was one of the first genes mapped to a specific chromosome. Wild-type flies have red eyes (w+); mutant white-eyed flies (w) have white eyes. The gene is X-linked. Crosses: white-eyed female (X^w X^w) × red-eyed male (X^+ Y) produce red-eyed females (X^+ X^w, carriers) and white-eyed males (X^w Y). The reciprocal cross (red-eyed female × white-eyed male) produces different results in the F1: all red-eyed females (X^+ X^w) and all white-eyed males (X^w Y). This sex-specific pattern proved the gene is on the X chromosome.

#### Multiple Genes in Corn Kernel Pigmentation
Corn kernel color is polygenic and shows a range of phenotypes. A 3-locus model (A, B, C loci) contributes to purple vs yellow. Genes act epistatically and additively. Double recessive plants (aabbcc) are colorless or light yellow; every dominant allele adds pigment. Seeds can be selected for specific color combinations, making corn ideal for studying polygenic inheritance.

#### Complementary Gene Action
When two recessive alleles at different loci restore a wild-type phenotype, genes are complementary. Example: In *Drosophila*, two mutants with white eyes (one at the white locus, one at the brown locus) will produce red-eyed offspring if crossed. Each parent provides the functional allele the other lacks (w+ and bw+). This proves the mutations are at different genes.

#### Worked Example: Corn Crosses
A corn plant with purple kernels (A_B_) is crossed with a yellow plant (aa__). F1 seeds are all purple (AaBb or Aa__). F1 × F1 produces F2 with a 9:7 ratio (9 purple : 7 yellow) if a third gene shows epistasis. This 9:7 ratio is a modified dihybrid ratio, indicating gene interaction.

### Blood Type Genetics: Multiple Alleles
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** blood-type-genetics-multiple-alleles
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** multiple alleles · ABO blood type · Rh factor · codominance · allele frequency
**Summary:** The ABO blood type locus has three alleles; individuals carry two, resulting in four blood types via codominance and dominance.

#### The Three ABO Alleles
The ABO locus on chromosome 9 has three alleles: I^A, I^B, and i. I^A codes for A antigen; I^B codes for B antigen; i codes for neither. I^A and I^B are codominant to each other (both expressed in IAIB) but both are dominant to i. Genotypes: IAIA or I^A i = type A; IBIB or I^B i = type B; IAIB = type AB; ii = type O.

#### Blood Type Inheritance
A type A parent (I^A I^A or I^A i) and type B parent (I^B I^B or I^B i) can have children of different blood types depending on whether they are homozygous or heterozygous. If both are heterozygous (I^A i × I^B i), offspring can be type A, B, AB, or O (1:1:1:1). A type O child (ii) proves both parents carry the i allele.

#### Rh Factor Incompatibility
The Rh factor is another locus. Rh+ (dominant D allele) is prevalent in most populations; Rh− (recessive dd) is less common. An Rh− mother carrying an Rh+ fetus can develop antibodies if fetal blood enters her circulation during delivery. These anti-D antibodies don't harm the first Rh+ infant but can cause hemolytic disease in subsequent Rh+ pregnancies (erythroblastosis fetalis). Preventive measures include RhoGAM injections (anti-D antibodies) to prevent maternal sensitization.

#### Allele Frequency and Hardy-Weinberg
In human populations, ABO allele frequencies vary. For example, type O is most common in Native American populations but rarer in Asian populations. The Hardy-Weinberg principle predicts genotype frequencies from allele frequencies: if p = frequency of I^A and q = frequency of I^B, then the frequency of type A (IAIA + I^A i) = p^2 + 2p(1−p−q). Population studies reveal how evolution and migration affect allele frequencies.

#### Transfusion and Blood Type Compatibility
Type O blood is a universal donor (no A or B antigens to trigger an immune response). Type AB is a universal recipient (has both A and B antigens, won't reject other types). A type A person transfused with type B blood will have an immune reaction (anti-B antibodies attack B antigen on donor RBCs). Understanding blood type genetics is critical for safe transfusion.

1. What are the possible blood types of a couple where one parent is IAIB (type AB) and the other is ii (type O)?
2. Why is an Rh− mother at risk of hemolytic disease in her second Rh+ pregnancy but not the first?
3. A child has type O blood (ii). One parent is type B. What is the other parent's blood type?

### Sex-Linked Traits: X-Linked Patterns
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** sex-linked-x-linked-inheritance
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** X-linked dominant · X-linked recessive · sex-specific phenotypes · hemizygous · X-inactivation
**Summary:** Genes on the X chromosome show sex-specific inheritance; males are hemizygous and more often affected by recessive conditions.

#### Hemizygous Males and X-Linked Recessive Traits
Males have one X chromosome (XY); females have two (XX). A male with one recessive allele (X^a Y) expresses the recessive phenotype because there's no allele on the Y to mask it (hemizygous condition). A female needs two recessive alleles (X^a X^a) to express a recessive phenotype. This is why X-linked recessive traits like color blindness and hemophilia are more common in males than females.

#### X-Linked Recessive Cross: Carrier Female × Affected Male
A carrier female (X^A X^a) × affected male (X^a Y) produce: daughters X^A X^a (carrier) and X^a X^a (affected); sons X^A Y (unaffected) and X^a Y (affected). Daughters have a 50% chance of being affected; sons have a 50% chance of being affected. Notably, the carrier mother has an equal probability of passing the defective allele to sons and daughters, but the Y chromosome in sons guarantees they express the trait.

#### X-Linked Recessive Cross: Unaffected Female × Affected Male
An unaffected female (X^A X^A) × affected male (X^a Y) produce all carrier daughters (X^A X^a) and all unaffected sons (X^A Y). All daughters inherit the X^a from their father and the X^A from their mother. All sons inherit the X^A from their mother and the Y from their father. In the F2 (carrier females crossed with unaffected males), 1/2 of sons are affected.

#### X-Linked Dominant Inheritance
X-linked dominant alleles are expressed in heterozygous females (X^A X^a). An affected male (X^A Y) × unaffected female (X^a X^a) produce affected daughters (X^A X^a) but unaffected sons (X^a Y). All daughters inherit the X^A from father; all sons inherit the X^a from mother and the Y from father. X-linked dominant traits are typically rarer and often more severe in hemizygous males (sometimes lethal).

#### X-Inactivation in Females
Females have two X chromosomes but use only one per cell — the other is inactivated into a Barr body. This X-inactivation is random and occurs early in development. A heterozygous female (X^A X^a) is a mosaic: some cells express X^A, others express X^a. This can result in intermediate phenotypes or patchy phenotypes. A carrier of hemophilia might have slightly reduced clotting factors; a carrier of color blindness might have color vision deficiency in part of her visual field.

#### Review Questions
1. Why are X-linked recessive traits more common in males than females?
2. An affected male (X^a Y) has children with an unaffected female (X^A X^A). Describe the phenotypes of their daughters and sons.
3. What is X-inactivation, and how does it result in mosaic phenotypes in heterozygous females?

### Sex-Influenced and Sex-Limited Traits
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** sex-influenced-limited-traits
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** sex-influenced · sex-limited · baldness · dominance modifications · heterozygote phenotype varies by sex
**Summary:** Sex hormones can modify dominance relationships; some traits are expressed only in one sex despite being autosomal.

#### Sex-Influenced Traits: Baldness
Human male baldness is often autosomal but sex-influenced. Let B = allele for baldness, b = allele for non-baldness. The allele B is dominant in males but recessive in females. A heterozygous male (Bb) is bald; a heterozygous female (Bb) is non-bald. BB males and females are bald; bb males and females are non-bald. The dominance relationship flips depending on sex — a reversal caused by sex hormones (androgens in males promote baldness; estrogens in females protect).

#### Phenotypic Ratios in Sex-Influenced Traits
A cross between two heterozygotes (Bb × Bb) produces 1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb. In males, BB and Bb are bald; bb is non-bald (3 bald : 1 non-bald). In females, BB is bald; Bb and bb are non-bald (1 bald : 3 non-bald). The phenotypic ratio differs by sex despite the same genotypic ratios.

#### Sex-Limited Traits: Egg Laying in Chickens
Sex-limited traits are expressed in only one sex, even though the alleles are autosomal. Egg-laying ability in chickens is sex-limited to females. A rooster doesn't lay eggs regardless of his genotype. A hen's phenotype depends on her alleles: the allele for high egg production is dominant. The alleles are present in both sexes but only expressed in females due to anatomy and physiology (ovaries, hormonal cycles).

#### Distinction: Sex-Linked vs Sex-Influenced vs Sex-Limited
Sex-linked: gene is on the X or Y chromosome (e.g., color blindness). Sex-influenced: gene is autosomal but dominance differs by sex (e.g., baldness). Sex-limited: gene is autosomal but expression occurs in only one sex (e.g., egg production). A pedigree can reveal which category a trait falls into: sex-linked traits often skip generations in females but appear in all sons of affected females; sex-influenced and sex-limited traits appear in both sexes but with different frequencies or patterns.

#### Phenotypic Prediction from Genotype and Sex
To predict an offspring's phenotype, determine the genotype first, then apply the sex-specific dominance rules. If parents are both Bb and the offspring is male with genotype Bb, the male is bald. If the same offspring were female with genotype Bb, she is non-bald. The same genotype produces opposite phenotypes depending on sex.

1. In a sex-influenced trait like baldness, why is a heterozygous male bald but a heterozygous female non-bald?

3. Why is egg-laying ability in chickens considered sex-limited rather than sex-linked?

### Non-Mendelian Application: Complex Pedigree Analysis
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** complex-pedigree-analysis
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** pedigree interpretation · conditional probability · carrier detection · genetic counseling
**Summary:** Real pedigrees combine multiple inheritance patterns; accurate interpretation requires knowledge of dominance, linkage, and population frequencies.

#### Interpreting Mixed Autosomal and X-Linked Traits
Some pedigrees involve both autosomal and X-linked loci. An individual's phenotype depends on both. For example, if a man is heterozygous for an autosomal trait (Aa) and hemizygous for an X-linked trait (X^b Y), his phenotype reflects both loci. Mating such an individual with a woman (Aa X^B X^b) requires separate analysis of each locus, then combining results.

#### Carrier Detection in Rare Recessive Diseases
A woman's brother has an autosomal recessive disease (genotype aa). Both parents are carriers (Aa). The woman is unaffected. What is her probability of being a carrier? Among the unaffected siblings, she could be AA (1/3) or Aa (2/3). If she marries an unaffected man from the general population (where the disease frequency is low), the probability he is a carrier is roughly equal to the disease allele frequency in the population (let's say 1/200). The probability their child is affected is (2/3) × (1/200) × (1/4) ≈ 1/1200 — much lower than if both parents were known carriers.

#### Genetic Counseling Scenario: Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD)
DMD is X-linked recessive. A woman's brother has DMD. Her father is unaffected; her mother must be a carrier (X^D X^d). The woman is unaffected; she is either X^D X^D or X^D X^d. Given her mother is a carrier, the woman has a 1/2 chance of being a carrier herself. If she has a son, the probability he has DMD is (1/2 chance she's a carrier) × (1/2 chance she passes X^d to son) = 1/4. Knowing her brother's status and her parents' genotypes allowed geneticists to calculate her risk.

#### Worked Pedigree: Hemophilia A (X-Linked Recessive)
A woman's father has hemophilia A (X^h Y). The woman is unaffected and married to an unaffected man (X^H Y). What is the probability their first son has hemophilia? The woman's father is X^h Y, so the woman inherited X^h from him and X^H from her unaffected mother. The woman is X^H X^h (carrier). Her unaffected husband is X^H Y. Their son has a 1/2 chance of inheriting X^h from mother (hemophilia) and a 1/2 chance of inheriting X^H (unaffected). Overall, P(son has hemophilia) = 1/2.

#### Consanguinity (Related Parents)
When parents are related (consanguineous marriage), the probability their child inherits two recessive alleles increases. If parents are first cousins and both are carriers of an autosomal recessive disease, the risk to a child is higher than if they were unrelated (P = 1/4) because they are more likely to carry the same rare alleles by descent. Consanguinity is common in some populations but uncommon in others, reflecting cultural and historical factors.

1. A woman's brother has cystic fibrosis (autosomal recessive). Her partner has a sister with cystic fibrosis but is unaffected. What is the probability their first child has cystic fibrosis?
2. A man with hemophilia A (X^h Y) and an unaffected woman (X^H X^H) have a daughter. Is their daughter affected, a carrier, or unaffected? Will their daughter ever have hemophilia symptoms?
3. Why does consanguinity increase the risk of autosomal recessive diseases in offspring?

## Subtopic: Chromosomal Genetics

### Chromosomal Aberrations: Deletions and Duplications
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** chromosomal-deletions-duplications
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** deletion · duplication · unbalanced translocation · genetic imbalance · lethality
**Summary:** Deletions remove genetic material; duplications add extra copies; both cause genetic imbalances that are usually harmful.

#### Deletions: Loss of Genetic Material
A deletion is a segment of chromosome that is lost or removed. If a chromosome loses a segment containing genes, those genes are absent. A person with a deletion is monosomic for that region (one copy instead of the normal two). Deletions are usually harmful because they remove essential genes. Small deletions might be tolerated; large deletions are often lethal. Wolf-Hirschhorn syndrome results from a deletion of 4p (short arm of chromosome 4); it causes intellectual disability and growth retardation.

#### Duplications: Extra Genetic Material
A duplication is a segment of chromosome that is repeated. A person with a duplication has three copies of that region (trisomy for that segment) — one from each homologous chromosome, plus an extra copy. Duplications can arise from unequal crossing over (where sister chromatids or homologous chromosomes misalign and exchange unequal segments). A duplication is generally less harmful than a deletion because the cell has functional copies of the genes, just extra ones, though overexpression can be problematic.

#### Unequal Crossing Over
If two homologous chromosomes misalign during prophase I, crossing over can be unequal. One recombinant chromosome gets a duplication of one segment and a deletion of another. The other recombinant loses that segment and gains a duplicate elsewhere. If a chromosome has tandem repeats (same sequence repeated next to each other), unequal crossing over can expand or contract the repeat number, leading to duplications or deletions.

#### Pseudoautosomal Regions
The X and Y chromosomes have small regions of homology called pseudoautosomal regions (PARs), where they can undergo crossing over during meiosis. Genes in these regions behave like autosomal genes, not sex-linked. Unequal crossing over in PARs can lead to XY XX or XYY XY gametes, causing sex chromosome aneuploidy.

#### Fragile Sites and Chromosome Instability
Fragile X syndrome involves an expansion of a CGG trinucleotide repeat at a fragile site on the X chromosome. Normal individuals have 5–44 repeats; affected individuals have >200 repeats. The expansion causes gene silencing (methylation) and intellectual disability. The repeat number can expand when passed from parent to child, a phenomenon called genetic anticipation.

### Inversions and Translocations
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** inversions-translocations-rearrangements
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** inversion · translocation · balanced rearrangement · segregation patterns · crossover products
**Summary:** Inversions flip chromosome segments; translocations move segments between chromosomes; balanced rearrangements may have no phenotype.

#### Inversions: Flipped Segments
An inversion is a reversal of a chromosome segment. If the normal order is ABCDEFG and a segment is inverted, it becomes ADCBEFG (the DEF portion is flipped). A person heterozygous for an inversion (one normal chromosome, one inverted) has a balanced genome overall but can produce unbalanced gametes if crossing over occurs within the inverted region. Crossover products between the inverted and normal segments are duplicated/deleted for the portions outside the inversion, usually causing aneuploidies in offspring.

#### Paracentric vs Pericentric Inversions
A paracentric inversion does not include the centromere (e.g., ABCDEFG → ABFEDCG). A pericentric inversion includes the centromere. During crossing over in the inversion loop (the structure formed when inverted and normal homologs pair), recombinant chromatids are unbalanced. Products have duplications and deletions, often with abnormal chromosome counts.

#### Translocations: Segments Move Between Chromosomes
A translocation moves a segment from one chromosome to another. A reciprocal translocation exchanges segments between two non-homologous chromosomes. If person A has chromosomes with segments swapped between their chromosome 5 and chromosome 15, they are a balanced translocation carrier — they have all genetic material but rearranged. They usually have no phenotype.

#### Robertsonian Translocation
A Robertsonian translocation occurs when two acrocentric chromosomes (centromere near the end) fuse at the centromere, creating one large chromosome. The short arms (which contain mostly repetitive DNA) are lost. A person with a Robertsonian translocation has 45 chromosomes instead of 46, but can be phenotypically normal if they retain genetic material. However, during meiosis, gamete segregation is unbalanced; some gametes lack genetic material, others have duplicates.

#### Segregation Patterns in Translocation Carriers
A balanced translocation carrier (e.g., 45 chromosomes with a Robertsonian fusion of 14 and 21) can produce unbalanced gametes during meiosis. If two normal-appearing parents have a child with trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), one parent might be a balanced 14/21 translocation carrier. The carrier has 45 chromosomes; the child with trisomy 21 has 47.

#### Segregation Problems and Reduced Fertility
Balanced translocation carriers have a higher risk of miscarriage and infertility because many of their gametes are unbalanced. An individual with a reciprocal translocation can produce six types of gametes (adjacent-1, adjacent-2, and alternate segregation patterns), only two of which are balanced. This reduces the number of viable offspring.

### Aneuploidy: Trisomy and Monosomy (Detailed)
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** aneuploidy-trisomy-monosomy-detail
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** trisomy · monosomy · karyotype · viable trisomies · mosaicism
**Summary:** Aneuploidy is an abnormal number of chromosomes; most aneuploidies are lethal, but a few autosomal trisomies and X monosomy can be viable.

#### Why Most Aneuploidies Are Lethal
Most aneuploidies result in lethality in utero because of genetic imbalance — three copies of many genes (trisomy) or one copy (monosomy) disrupts development. The more genes in an imbalanced chromosome, the worse the prognosis. Humans have only a few viable trisomies because those chromosomes are small (few genes) or relatively gene-poor.

#### Viable Trisomies: Autosomes 13, 18, and 21
Trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome) and trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome) involve small chromosomes but are severe; infants often die within the first year. Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome) is more viable; life expectancy is now ~60 years. The genes on chromosome 21 don't cause as severe an imbalance as other trisomies. Importantly, all three trisomies can result from nondisjunction during meiosis in either parent, though maternal age increases risk (older eggs have more meiotic errors).

#### Down Syndrome (Trisomy 21) Phenotype and Genotype
Individuals with trisomy 21 have 47 chromosomes (three copies of chromosome 21). Common traits include intellectual disability (IQ typically 30–50 without intervention), characteristic facial features (epicanthal folds, flattened face), and cardiac defects. However, there is significant variability. Some individuals with Down syndrome have higher cognitive ability and can attend school, live semi-independently, and contribute meaningfully to their communities. Early intervention (speech, physical, occupational therapy) significantly improves outcomes.

#### Sex Chromosome Aneuploidies
Monosomy X (Turner syndrome) with 45 chromosomes is viable; individuals are typically female with short stature, ovarian dysgenesis, and sometimes cardiac or renal abnormalities. The viability of X monosomy reflects X-inactivation compensation in normal females — with only one X, the cell is naturally haploinsufficient. XXY (Klinefelter), XXX, and XYY individuals have variable phenotypes; some are phenotypically normal.

#### Mosaicism
Mosaicism occurs when an individual has two or more cell populations with different genotypes. This can arise from nondisjunction early in development (early = most cells affected; late = fewer cells affected) or from anaphase lag (chromosome loss). A mosaic trisomy 21 individual (mixture of diploid and trisomy 21 cells) may have milder phenotype than a full trisomy 21 individual. Mosaicism can mask karyotype if a sample happens to include only normal cells.

#### Maternal Age and Trisomy Risk
Trisomy risk increases with maternal age, particularly after age 35. At age 20, the risk of Down syndrome is ~1/1500; at age 45, it's ~1/30. This age effect is thought to reflect the prolonged arrest in prophase I — oocytes spend decades in meiosis I before ovulation, and this long wait increases the likelihood of errors in spindle formation or cohesin degradation.

### Chromosomal Genetics Application: Karyotyping and Diagnosis
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** karyotyping-diagnosis-chromosomal-abnormalities
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** karyotype · G-banding · fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) · chromosomal diagnosis · prenatal testing
**Summary:** Karyotyping and molecular methods identify chromosomal abnormalities; prenatal diagnosis allows early detection and informed decision-making.

#### G-Banding Karyotype
A G-banded karyotype is produced by staining metaphase chromosomes with Giemsa dye. The stain creates a pattern of light and dark bands (G-bands) along each chromosome, allowing visual identification of all 46 chromosomes and detection of large abnormalities (deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations). The banding pattern is consistent and species-specific; geneticists use it to match chromosomes into a standard karyotype and identify aberrations.

#### Standard Karyotype Notation
The notation "46,XY" means 46 total chromosomes with an XY sex chromosome complement (male). "47,XX,+21" means 47 chromosomes with a trisomy 21 (female). "46,XX,der(14;21)(q10;q10)" indicates a balanced Robertsonian translocation of chromosomes 14 and 21. Each chromosome is numbered 1–22, with X and Y; inversions, deletions, and translocations are noted by their breakpoints.

#### FISH (Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization)
FISH uses fluorescent DNA probes to detect specific chromosome sequences. A probe for chromosome 21 will hybridize (bind) to chromosome 21 DNA in metaphase cells. A trisomy 21 cell will show three signals (three copies of chromosome 21); a normal cell shows two. FISH is faster than karyotyping (hours vs days) and can detect smaller abnormalities than G-banding. Multicolor FISH (using different colored probes) can identify all chromosomes and translocations simultaneously.

#### Prenatal Diagnosis: Amniocentesis and CVS
Amniocentesis retrieves fetal cells from amniotic fluid (usually after 15 weeks gestation); chorionic villus sampling (CVS) retrieves placental cells (after 10 weeks). Both can be karyotyped or tested via FISH to detect aneuploidy. Down syndrome screening by karyotype has nearly 100% accuracy. However, both procedures carry a small miscarriage risk (~1/200). Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) using cell-free fetal DNA from maternal blood is now common; it has high sensitivity for trisomies but is not diagnostic (confirmed by karyotype if positive).

#### Worked Example: Prenatal Diagnosis Scenario
A 40-year-old pregnant woman has NIPT results suggesting increased risk of trisomy 21. Karyotyping via amniocentesis confirms 47,XX,+21 (female with Down syndrome). The couple receives genetic counseling about Down syndrome phenotype and outcomes. They decide to continue the pregnancy. After birth, the infant undergoes cardiac screening (common in Down syndrome) and early intervention programs are arranged.

#### Mosaic Diagnosis Challenge
A karyotype of a mosaic trisomy 21 individual might show a mixture of normal (46,XX) and trisomic (47,XX,+21) cells. The proportion of trisomic cells varies by tissue; some tissues might have none (false negative karyotype) while others have many (false positive if that's the only tissue tested). FISH and other methods can help quantify the proportion of abnormal cells.

## Subtopic: Population Genetics & Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

### Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium: Theory
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hardy-weinberg-equilibrium-theory
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** allele frequency · genotype frequency · Hardy-Weinberg equation · equilibrium conditions
**Summary:** The Hardy-Weinberg principle predicts that allele and genotype frequencies remain constant across generations if certain conditions hold.

#### The Hardy-Weinberg Equation
If a locus has two alleles, A (frequency p) and a (frequency q), where p + q = 1, then after random mating, the genotype frequencies are: p^2 (AA), 2pq (Aa), and q^2 (aa). This is the Hardy-Weinberg equation: p² + 2pq + q² = 1. Genotype frequencies can be calculated from allele frequencies; conversely, allele frequencies can be calculated from genotype frequencies.

#### Why Equilibrium Is Reached in One Generation
If a population starts with unequal gamete frequencies, random mating will produce equilibrium genotypes in just one generation. For example, if 60% of gametes carry A and 40% carry a, the first generation offspring have genotypes: 0.36 AA, 0.48 Aa, 0.16 aa. In the F1, the allele frequency is still p = 0.6 (calculated as p = freq(AA) + 1/2·freq(Aa) = 0.36 + 0.24 = 0.6). Subsequent generations maintain these frequencies indefinitely, assuming no other evolutionary forces.

#### Conditions for Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium
The Hardy-Weinberg principle applies if and only if: (1) no mutations, (2) random mating, (3) no migration, (4) no natural selection, (5) large population size (no genetic drift). In natural populations, these conditions are rarely all met. Deviation from expected frequencies reveals that one or more forces are acting. A test of Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, therefore, detects whether evolution is occurring.

#### Calculating Allele Frequency from Genotype Frequency
If a population has 100 AA, 200 Aa, and 100 aa individuals (total 400), the allele frequency is: frequency(A) = (2×100 + 200) / (2×400) = 400/800 = 0.5 = p. frequency(a) = (200 + 2×100) / (2×400) = 400/800 = 0.5 = q. Check: p + q = 1. ✓

#### Multiple Alleles
The Hardy-Weinberg equation extends to more than two alleles. For three alleles A (frequency p), B (frequency q), and C (frequency r), where p + q + r = 1, the genotype frequencies in equilibrium are: p², q², r², 2pq, 2pr, 2qr, and so on. The general form is (p + q + r + ...)² = 1.

2. A population has 400 AA, 400 Aa, and 200 aa individuals. Calculate the allele frequencies.
3. Why is Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium important in population genetics?

### Deviations from Hardy-Weinberg: Mutation, Selection, Migration
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** evolution-forces-deviations-hardy-weinberg
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** mutation · natural selection · migration (gene flow) · allele frequency change · evolutionary drivers
**Summary:** Mutations, selection, and migration drive allele frequency change; deviations from Hardy-Weinberg indicate evolutionary forces at work.

#### Mutation: Source of New Alleles
Mutation is the only source of new alleles in a population. The mutation rate is typically low (~10^-8 per base pair per generation for point mutations), so mutation alone usually does not change allele frequencies dramatically. However, over many generations, mutation can shift allele frequencies, especially for rare alleles. Reverse mutation (mutant back to wild-type) can also occur, creating a balance.

#### Natural Selection: Fitness and Differential Survival
Natural selection occurs when different genotypes have different fitnesses (survival and reproduction probabilities). If AA individuals produce more offspring than aa, the A allele increases in frequency each generation. The strength of selection is measured by the selection coefficient (s), where relative fitness of aa is 1 − s (s ranges from 0 to 1). Strong selection (large s) rapidly changes allele frequencies; weak selection acts slowly.

#### Worked Example: Selection Against Recessive Homozygotes
In a population with genotypes AA, Aa, and aa, suppose aa individuals have fitness 0.8 (20% reduced) and others have fitness 1. The selection coefficient is s = 0.2. If the initial frequency of a is q = 0.5, selection will reduce q each generation. The new frequency after one generation is roughly q' ≈ q × (1 − sq/(1 − sq²)), which is slightly less than 0.5. Over many generations, the a allele decreases toward zero, but slowly (because heterozygotes are fit and carry the allele).

#### Migration (Gene Flow): Allele Transfer Between Populations
Migration introduces alleles from one population to another. Even small amounts of migration can counteract the effects of drift and local selection. If a population has allele frequency p₁ and receives migrants from a population with frequency p₂, the new allele frequency is p' = p₁(1 − m) + p₂m, where m is the migration rate. Migration homogenizes allele frequencies across populations.

#### Genetic Drift: Random Change in Small Populations
In small populations, random sampling can cause large changes in allele frequency (genetic drift). If a population has n individuals, the variance in allele frequency change per generation is roughly p(1−p)/2n. Drift causes random fluctuations and can lead to fixation (frequency = 1) or loss (frequency = 0) of alleles. Drift acts faster in smaller populations (stronger effect for small n).

#### Balancing Selection: Maintaining Polymorphism
Some alleles are maintained at intermediate frequencies by balancing selection. Heterozygotes (Aa) might have higher fitness than either homozygote (AA or aa) — heterozygote advantage. This maintains both alleles in the population. The sickle-cell allele (HbS) in malaria-endemic regions is maintained at ~10% frequency in some African populations because heterozygotes (HbA/HbS) are resistant to malaria but don't suffer severe anemia.

2. A recessive allele a has a frequency of 0.1. If selection against aa is very weak (s = 0.01), how many generations will it take to halve its frequency?
3. How does migration influence allele frequencies in populations connected by gene flow?

### Population Genetics Application: Microevolution and Allele Frequency
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** microevolution-allele-frequency-change
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** microevolution · allele frequency change · population bottleneck · founder effect · adaptive radiation
**Summary:** Microevolution is observable change in allele frequencies within populations over short timescales; multiple evolutionary forces drive these changes.

#### Tracking Allele Frequency Change Over Time
Peppered moths in industrial England provide a classic example. Before industrialization, the light-colored morph (dominant) was common (~99%); the dark morph (recessive) was rare (~1%). With industrial soot, dark moths on blackened trees survived better (selection), and by the 1950s, dark moths were ~90% of the population. This rapid change was driven by strong natural selection against the light morph in polluted environments. When air quality improved, the light morph rebounded.

#### Microevolution in Bacterial Populations
Antibiotic resistance in bacteria demonstrates microevolution over even shorter timescales. In a bacterial population, a rare mutant with antibiotic resistance gene has frequency ~10^-6. After exposure to antibiotics, sensitive bacteria die, and the resistant mutant increases to ~90% frequency in days. This is microevolution driven by extreme selection (resistant survive, sensitive die).

#### Founder Effect and Genetic Drift
When a small group (founders) colonizes a new area, allele frequencies in the founding population may differ from the source population by chance. This founder effect can lead to fixation of some alleles and loss of others, particularly if the founding population is very small. The Amish population in Pennsylvania has unusually high frequencies of polydactyly (extra fingers) and other recessive traits due to founder effects and subsequent inbreeding in a small, isolated population.

#### Population Bottleneck and Loss of Genetic Variation
A bottleneck occurs when population size crashes (e.g., disease, environmental disaster), drastically reducing genetic diversity. Surviving individuals carry only a subset of the genetic variation of the pre-bottleneck population. The cheetah underwent a severe bottleneck ~10,000 years ago; today, all cheetahs are nearly genetically identical. This loss of diversity makes populations vulnerable to disease and environmental change.

#### Worked Example: California Condor Recovery
The California condor was reduced to 27 individuals (1987), making it the lowest diversity of any bird species. Genetic drift and inbreeding continue to erode diversity. Breeding programs attempt to minimize further loss, but the low baseline genetic diversity constrains recovery. The condor illustrates how bottlenecks create long-term genetic consequences even if the population size recovers.

#### Adaptive Radiation: Diversification from a Common Ancestor
Adaptive radiation occurs when a single ancestral species colonizes a new environment and rapidly diversifies into multiple species filling different ecological niches. Darwin's finches in the Galápagos are a famous example: all ~18 finch species descended from a common finch ancestor in the last few million years. Each species has a beak shape adapted to a different food source (seeds, insects, nectar). This rapid diversification is driven by natural selection in a species-poor environment.

2. A small population of beetles is established by 10 founders carrying alleles at frequencies different from the source population. Why might allele frequencies drift more rapidly in this small population?
3. Why does a bottleneck reduce genetic diversity, and what are the long-term consequences for population health?
