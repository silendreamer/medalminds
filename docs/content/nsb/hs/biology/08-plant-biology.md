## Subtopic: Plant Structure & Organization

### Meristems and Plant Growth
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** meristems-plant-growth-apical-lateral
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** apical meristem · lateral meristem · indeterminate growth · primary vs secondary growth
**Summary:** Meristems are regions of undifferentiated cells that drive plant growth; apical meristems extend shoots and roots, while lateral meristems thicken stems.

#### Apical Meristems: The Growing Tips
Apical meristems at the shoot and root tips contain small, undifferentiated cells that divide by mitosis to produce new cells. These cells then differentiate into specialized tissues (xylem, phloem, epidermis). The apical meristem of the shoot produces leaves and branches in a spiral or whorled pattern. The apical meristem of the root produces root cap cells (which protect the tip) and tissues for water and mineral absorption. Apical meristems enable indeterminate growth — plants grow continuously throughout their lives, unlike animals with determinate body size.

#### Lateral Meristems: Thickening the Stem
The vascular cambium is a lateral meristem between the xylem and phloem. It divides to produce new xylem (toward the center, forming growth rings) and phloem (outward, pushed by accumulating xylem). The cork cambium in the bark divides to produce cork cells that form a protective outer layer. Lateral growth is secondary growth — it increases stem diameter without increasing height. In trees, the number of growth rings equals the tree's age (one ring per year in temperate climates with distinct seasons).

#### Primary Growth: Height and Branching
Primary growth extends the shoot and root through cell division and elongation at apical meristems. The cells behind the meristem elongate, pushing the meristem tip forward and extending the plant. As the shoot grows, leaves and lateral branches form. This primary growth determines overall plant architecture and is most vigorous in young plants and growing season.

#### Secondary Growth: Annual Rings and Tree Age
As a tree ages, lateral meristems add wood. Xylem cells form thick-walled secondary xylem (wood) that accumulates year after year. Early wood (spring) has larger cells with thinner walls, forming light-colored rings. Late wood (summer/fall) has smaller, thick-walled cells, forming darker rings. The contrast between them creates a visible growth ring. Counting rings reveals age. Extremely cold or drought years produce narrow rings; favorable years produce wide rings — so tree rings record climate history (dendrochronology).

#### Differentiation After Meristematic Growth
After a cell leaves the meristem and begins elongating, it differentiates. Elongating cells in the root hair zone develop root hairs (extensions that increase surface area for absorption). Epidermal cells in leaves flatten and become transparent to allow light penetration. Cells destined to become xylem develop thick, rigid secondary walls and often die after maturation (water transport needs dead cells so water moves freely). This differentiation creates the specialized tissues of a mature plant.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the difference between apical and lateral meristems, and what tissues do they produce?
2. Why is indeterminate growth an advantage for plants but a disadvantage for many animals?
3. A tree has 50 growth rings. What is the tree's age, and what does a particularly narrow ring indicate?

### Vascular Tissues: Xylem and Phloem
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** vascular-tissues-xylem-phloem-transport
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** xylem · phloem · vascular bundles · vessel elements · sieve tubes · transpiration-cohesion-tension
**Summary:** Xylem transports water and minerals from roots to shoots via passive transport; phloem distributes sugars via active transport.

#### Xylem: Water and Mineral Transport
Xylem is composed of vessel elements (in flowering plants) and tracheids. Vessel elements are stacked end-to-end with perforated end walls, forming continuous tubes. Tracheids have tapered ends with pits (small openings); water moves through pits. Both vessel elements and tracheids have thick, lignified secondary cell walls for structural support and are dead at maturity — the cell contents are gone, leaving hollow tubes. Water and minerals move through the xylem under tension (negative pressure), pulled upward by transpiration and the cohesive properties of water.

#### Phloem: Sugar Distribution
Phloem is composed of sieve tube elements (in flowering plants) and companion cells. Sieve tube elements are stacked end-to-end with sieve plates (areas of open pores) in their end walls, allowing cytoplasm and plasmodesmata connections. Companion cells are nucleated cells adjacent to sieve tubes; they have numerous mitochondria and actively transport sugars into the sieve tubes. Phloem is living tissue. Sugars move through phloem under positive pressure (active transport), driven by ATP and concentration gradients. The movement of sugars is called translocation.

#### Vascular Bundles and Stem Structure
In herbaceous plants, vascular bundles are arranged in a ring in the stem. Each bundle contains both xylem (inner) and phloem (outer), separated by a thin vascular cambium. In trees, the xylem accumulates as wood in the center; phloem accumulates in the bark. The arrangement allows efficient nutrient and water transport while supporting the plant's weight.

#### Water Transport: Cohesion-Tension Theory
Water transported through xylem is pulled upward by a gradient of water potential. Water in the leaves is absorbed during photosynthesis and transpired (lost to air) through stomata. This loss creates a tension (negative pressure) that pulls water up from the roots. Water molecules cohere to each other (hydrogen bonding), forming a continuous column under tension. The column is stronger than the tensile strength of steel, yet water is pulled up to the tops of 100-meter-tall redwoods with no active transport (xylem cells are dead). This passive transport is efficient for water over long distances.

#### Translocation: Pressure-Flow Hypothesis
Sugars produced in photosynthesizing leaves (source) are actively transported into phloem via companion cells. This increases osmotic potential in the sieve tubes, drawing in water. The resulting positive pressure pushes sugar-rich sap toward roots (sink) and other non-photosynthetic tissues. At the sink, sugars are unloaded and used. This pressure-flow hypothesis explains directional transport based on water potential differences.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain the structural and functional differences between xylem and phloem.
2. How does the cohesion-tension theory explain water transport to the top of a tall tree?
3. In translocation, why does sugar move from leaves (source) to roots (sink)?

### Roots and Root Systems
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** roots-root-systems-absorption-anchorage
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** taproot vs fibrous root · root hair zone · endodermis · root pressure · nutrient uptake
**Summary:** Roots absorb water and minerals, anchor plants, and store nutrients; the root system structure maximizes surface area for absorption.

#### Root System Architecture: Taproots vs Fibrous Roots
A taproot system has one main root (tap root) with smaller lateral roots branching off — typical of dicots like carrots and dandelions. A fibrous root system has many roots of similar size all originating from the stem — typical of monocots like grasses. Taproots penetrate deep soil and access deep water; fibrous roots spread broadly and stabilize soil. Each system is adapted to the plant's ecology and climate.

#### Root Zones: Cap, Meristem, Elongation, Differentiation
The root cap covers the apical meristem and secretes slime for lubrication, helping the root penetrate soil. The meristematic zone contains actively dividing cells. Behind the meristem, the elongation zone cells rapidly elongate without dividing, pushing the root tip deeper. Further back, the differentiation (maturation) zone contains root hairs and mature tissues for absorption. This zonation maximizes growth efficiency.

#### Root Hairs and Absorption
Root hairs are single-celled extensions of epidermal cells in the maturation zone. They greatly increase surface area for water and mineral absorption. A single root hair is thin-walled and contains many mitochondria; the thin wall allows rapid water uptake, and mitochondria provide ATP for active transport of minerals. Root hairs are short-lived (days to weeks) and are constantly replaced as the root continues to grow and push new hairs into new soil.

#### The Endodermis and Casparian Strip
One cell layer inward from the epidermis is the endodermis. Endodermal cells have a thickened, waxy band on their cell walls called the Casparian strip. This band blocks the free diffusion of water and minerals through the cell wall and intercellular spaces, forcing them to pass through the cell membrane (and the symplasm). This selective barrier allows the plant to regulate uptake and prevent loss of minerals from the xylem.

#### Root Pressure and Guttation
In spring, when soil is wet and transpiration is low (night, cool weather), roots continue to absorb water via osmosis. Water accumulates in the xylem, creating positive pressure (root pressure) that can push water up into the plant, sometimes forcing water out of leaves in a process called guttation (visible as water droplets on leaf tips). Root pressure can drive water up several meters but is not the primary driver of long-distance transport (that's transpiration).

#### Review Questions
1. Distinguish between taproot and fibrous root systems, and explain where each is typically found.
2. Why are root hairs important for absorption, and why are they short-lived?
3. Explain the role of the Casparian strip in the endodermis.

### Leaves and Photosynthetic Structure
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** leaf-structure-photosynthetic-adaptations
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** blade vs petiole · stomata · mesophyll · C3 vs C4 leaf anatomy · xerophyte adaptations
**Summary:** Leaf structure maximizes light capture and gas exchange while minimizing water loss; variations reflect evolutionary adaptation to different environments.

#### Leaf Parts and Basic Structure
A leaf typically consists of a blade (flat photosynthetic surface) and a petiole (stalk) connecting to the stem. The blade has a network of veins (vascular tissue) that conduct water, minerals, and sugars. The epidermis is a single-cell layer on both upper and lower surfaces; it's often coated with a waxy cuticle that reduces water loss. The mesophyll between upper and lower epidermis contains the photosynthetic tissues.

#### Mesophyll Layers: Palisade and Spongy
The upper mesophyll is typically palisade parenchyma — tall, columnar cells tightly packed and rich in chloroplasts, positioned to intercept light. The lower mesophyll is spongy parenchyma — loosely packed cells with large intercellular air spaces, allowing gas diffusion. This stratification maximizes light capture while allowing gas exchange. The spongy mesophyll connects to stomata (pores) on the lower epidermis, enabling CO₂ entry for photosynthesis and water loss via transpiration.

#### Stomata: Guard Cells and Gas Exchange
Stomata are small openings in the epidermis, each surrounded by two guard cells. Guard cells have differential cell wall thickness and contain chloroplasts. When light stimulates photosynthesis in guard cells, they accumulate potassium ions and organic acids, lowering water potential and absorbing water. Turgor pressure increases, and the guard cells expand, opening the stoma. At night, guard cells lose potassium and water, wilting and closing the stoma. This is an elegant system: stoma opens when light is available for photosynthesis and closes at night or during drought to save water.

#### C3 vs C4 Leaf Anatomy
C3 plants (wheat, rice) have typical mesophyll structure. C4 plants (corn, sugarcane) have a modified anatomy with a layer of bundle sheath cells around the vein. C4 photosynthesis concentrates CO₂ around Rubisco in bundle sheath cells, reducing photorespiration. This adaptation is advantageous in hot, dry climates where CO₂ is limiting. C4 leaves have more compact structure and smaller stomata.

#### Xerophyte Adaptations: Drought-Resistant Leaves
Plants in arid environments (xerophytes) have leaves modified to conserve water: thick, waxy cuticles; sunken stomata (in pits, reducing air movement around the opening); reduced leaf surface area (smaller, rolled, or needle-like leaves); and thicker mesophyll cells with fewer air spaces. Examples: desert shrubs, cacti, pines. These adaptations reduce transpiration but also reduce CO₂ uptake, making photosynthesis less efficient. Xerophytes sacrifice productivity for survival in water-limited environments.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain the structural differences between palisade and spongy mesophyll, and their functions.
2. How do guard cells open and close stomata, and why is this regulation important?
3. How are C4 and xerophyte leaves adapted to hot, dry conditions?

### Plant Root and Shoot Systems Application: Nutrient Uptake and Transport
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** nutrient-uptake-transport-whole-plant
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** mineral nutrients · nitrogen fixation · nutrient cycling · active transport · whole-plant transport
**Summary:** Plants obtain nutrients from soil and air; transport and allocation of nutrients reflects demand and developmental stage.

#### Essential Nutrients and Their Functions
Plants require macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) and micronutrients (iron, magnesium, boron). Nitrogen is a component of proteins and nucleic acids; phosphorus is in ATP and phospholipids; potassium regulates osmotic potential and enzyme activity. Iron is in cytochromes (electron transport); magnesium is in chlorophyll. Deficiency in any nutrient shows in symptoms: nitrogen deficiency causes yellowing (loss of chlorophyll); phosphorus deficiency causes purpling (anthocyanin accumulation); potassium deficiency causes weak stems and yellowing of lower leaves.

#### Nitrogen Uptake and Nitrogen Fixation
Plants absorb nitrogen as nitrate (NO₃⁻) or ammonium (NH₄⁺) via active transport into root hair cells. Most plants cannot fix atmospheric N₂. Legumes (peas, beans) have root nodules containing symbiotic bacteria (*Rhizobium*) that convert N₂ to ammonia, which the plant uses. The plant provides carbohydrates to bacteria; bacteria provide usable nitrogen. This symbiosis is mutualistic. Nitrogen-fixing crops are used in crop rotation to replenish soil nitrogen naturally.

#### Nutrient Transport from Roots to Shoots
After entering root cells, nutrients are transported through the xylem to shoots. In spring, when transpiration is rapid, nutrient transport is efficient. However, some nutrients (potassium, phosphorus) can be remobilized from older leaves to young leaves via phloem during nutrient-limited conditions. This translocation redirects scarce nutrients to tissues with the highest growth and metabolic demands.

#### Worked Example: Nitrogen Availability and Plant Growth
A corn plant in nitrogen-poor soil shows stunted growth and yellow lower leaves (nitrogen remobilization to young leaves). Adding nitrogen fertilizer corrects the yellowing and increases growth. However, excessive nitrogen causes excessive vegetative growth with fewer seeds — too much nitrogen diverts resources from reproduction. Optimal nitrogen levels maximize yield by balancing vegetative growth and seed production.

#### Mycorrhizal Associations and Nutrient Uptake
Many plants form mutualistic associations with fungi (mycorrhizae). Fungal hyphae penetrate the soil far more efficiently than root hairs, accessing water and nutrients (especially phosphorus) unavailable to roots alone. The fungus receives carbohydrates from the plant. This symbiosis is especially important in nutrient-poor soils. Some plants (orchids) depend almost entirely on mycorrhizal fungi for germination and survival; other plants can survive without them but grow better with them.

#### Review Questions
1. What are the main roles of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in plants? What deficiency symptoms would you observe?
2. How does nitrogen fixation by *Rhizobium* bacteria benefit both the plant and the bacteria?
3. Why might a mycorrhizal association be particularly important for a plant growing in nutrient-poor soil?

## Subtopic: Photosynthesis (Light Reactions & Calvin Cycle)

### The Light Reactions: Photosystem II and Electron Transport
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** light-reactions-photosystem-ii-electron-transport
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** photolysis · electron transport chain · photosystem II · plastoquinone · cytochrome b6f complex
**Summary:** Light reactions in thylakoid membranes split water, generate electron transport, and produce ATP and NADPH.

#### Photosystem II (PSII) and Water Splitting
Photosystem II is a protein complex in the thylakoid membrane. Chlorophyll and accessory pigments in PSII's antenna absorb photons and funnel energy to the reaction center, where a special pair of chlorophyll a molecules (P680) is excited. The excited P680 donates an electron to a primary acceptor, generating a positive charge on P680. This positive charge is strong enough to oxidize water: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻. This photolysis (light-driven splitting) of water is the ultimate source of electrons for all photosynthesis and is responsible for producing atmospheric oxygen.

#### The Electron Transport Chain: PSII to Cytochrome b6f
Electrons from PSII pass through a series of carriers: plastoquinone (PQ), the cytochrome b6f complex, and plastocyanin (PC). As electrons flow through cytochrome b6f, they lose energy, which is coupled to pumping protons (H⁺) from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen. This creates a proton gradient — the basis for ATP synthesis. The electron transport chain is analogous to the inner membrane electron transport chain of mitochondria but occurs in thylakoids instead.

#### Photosystem I (PSI) and NADPH Formation
Electrons from cytochrome b6f enter photosystem I. PSI's reaction center (P700) absorbs photons and re-energizes electrons to even higher energy. These high-energy electrons reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH via the enzyme NADP⁺ reductase. NADPH is a reducing power (electron donor) used in the Calvin cycle to fix CO₂. Note: PSI's electrons come from PSII's electron transport; PSII doesn't directly feed into PSI.

#### The Z Scheme: Linear Electron Flow
The sequential reduction of water (PSII) → plastoquinone → cytochrome b6f → plastocyanin → PSI → NADP⁺ creates a "Z" shape when plotting energy levels. The Z scheme represents the two-photosystem system's coordinated function: PSII and PSI are each energized by photons, and together they extract electrons from water and transfer them to NADPH. This linear electron flow is triggered by light and is essential for photosynthesis.

#### Cyclic Electron Flow and Additional ATP
In cyclic electron flow, electrons from PSI can cycle back through cytochrome b6f and return to PSI instead of reducing NADP⁺. This route bypasses PSII and regenerates ATP without producing NADPH. Cyclic flow occurs when NADP⁺ is limited (all converted to NADPH) or when a high ATP:NADPH ratio is needed. Plants use both linear and cyclic flow to produce the optimal ratio of ATP to NADPH for the Calvin cycle.

#### Review Questions
1. What is photolysis, and why is it critical for life on Earth?
2. Explain the role of the proton gradient in the thylakoid lumen in ATP synthesis.
3. How does cyclic electron flow differ from linear electron flow, and when would cyclic flow be advantageous?

### The Light Reactions: Photophosphorylation (ATP Synthesis)
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** light-reactions-photophosphorylation-atp-synthesis
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** proton gradient · chemiosmosis · ATP synthase · thylakoid lumen · stroma
**Summary:** Electron transport pumps protons into the thylakoid lumen; ATP synthase uses this gradient to phosphorylate ADP to ATP.

#### Proton Pumping and the Thylakoid Lumen
As electrons move through the electron transport chain (especially through the cytochrome b6f complex), the energy released is used to actively transport protons from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen. This Q cycle couples electron flow to proton pumping. The result is a proton gradient: the thylakoid lumen has high [H⁺] and low pH (~4), while the stroma has low [H⁺] and high pH (~8). This gradient is a form of potential energy.

#### Chemiosmosis in Photosynthesis
The proton gradient creates an electrochemical gradient — protons "want" to diffuse back to the stroma (down their concentration gradient). However, the thylakoid membrane is impermeable to protons except through ATP synthase channels. ATP synthase is a molecular motor: protons flowing through it drive the phosphorylation of ADP + Pi → ATP. This chemiosmotic coupling is identical in principle to mitochondrial ATP synthesis but occurs in thylakoids instead. Roughly 3 protons are required to synthesize one ATP (3 H⁺/ATP).

#### ATP Synthase Structure and Function
ATP synthase has two main parts: the F₀ subunit (embedded in the thylakoid membrane) forms a proton channel, and the F₁ subunit (in the stroma) catalyzes ATP synthesis. As protons flow through F₀, they rotate the rotor, which is mechanically coupled to F₁. The rotating F₁ causes conformational changes in ADP and Pi binding sites, catalyzing phosphorylation. This is a beautiful example of molecular machinery — rotary motion at the molecular scale drives chemical synthesis.

#### Stoichiometry and Efficiency
Under ideal conditions, 8 photons produce 3 ATP and 2 NADPH (approximately). In the Calvin cycle, fixing one CO₂ requires 3 ATP and 2 NADPH. Thus, fixing three CO₂ requires 9 ATP and 6 NADPH, requiring ~24 photons. The quantum yield of photosynthesis (moles of O₂ produced per mole of photons) is typically 1/8 to 1/10, reflecting the minimum photons needed.

#### Regulation of Photophosphorylation
Light intensity directly controls electron transport rate and thus proton pumping and ATP synthesis. At low light, fewer photons are absorbed, slowing electron transport. At saturating light, electron transport is maximal. Temperature affects enzyme kinetics; very high temperatures denature proteins. Photophosphorylation is tightly regulated to match the ATP and NADPH demands of the Calvin cycle.

#### Review Questions
1. How does the proton gradient in the thylakoid lumen contribute to ATP synthesis?
2. Explain how ATP synthase converts a proton gradient into chemical energy (ATP).
3. Why is roughly three photons required to synthesize one ATP in photosynthesis?

### The Calvin Cycle: Carbon Fixation and Reduction
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** calvin-cycle-carbon-fixation-reduction
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** RuBisCO · 3-phosphoglycerate · glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate · ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate · regulation
**Summary:** The Calvin cycle (dark reactions) uses ATP and NADPH from the light reactions to fix CO₂ into sugars via three stages: fixation, reduction, regeneration.

#### Stage 1: Carbon Fixation (Carboxylation)
The enzyme ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RuBisCO) catalyzes the first step: CO₂ + RuBP (5-carbon) → 2 × 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PG, 3-carbon). RuBisCO is the most abundant protein on Earth. The reaction is relatively slow (~3 reactions/second), which is why plants need so much of it. The carboxyl group (COO⁻) from CO₂ attaches to the 5-carbon sugar, splitting it into two 3-carbon molecules.

#### Stage 2: Reduction (Synthesis of G3P)
3-phosphoglycerate is phosphorylated by ATP: 3-PG + ATP → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate + ADP. Then 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate is reduced by NADPH: 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate + NADPH → glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P) + NADP⁺ + Pi. For every 3 CO₂ fixed (producing 6 × 3-PG), 6 ATP and 6 NADPH are consumed to produce 6 G3P. This reduction stage is energy-intensive — the ATP and NADPH from the light reactions are used here.

#### Stage 3: Regeneration (RuBP Regeneration)
Of the 6 G3P molecules produced from 3 CO₂, one is used as a net gain (the carbon that was fixed). The other 5 G3P (15 carbons total) are rearranged through a series of reactions to regenerate 3 RuBP (15 carbons total). These rearrangements involve transketolase and aldolase enzymes and use one additional ATP. After regeneration, the 3 RuBP re-enter the cycle to fix 3 more CO₂.

#### The Numbers: 3 CO₂ to 1 G3P Gain
Fixing 3 CO₂ requires 9 ATP and 6 NADPH. The pathway produces 6 G3P but regenerates 3 RuBP. The net gain is 1 G3P (3-carbon sugar), which can be used to synthesize glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆): 2 G3P → 1 glucose. So, producing one glucose requires 2 cycles (6 CO₂ fixed, 18 ATP and 12 NADPH consumed).

#### Regulation of the Calvin Cycle
Light controls the cycle indirectly: light reactions produce ATP and NADPH, which drive the cycle's reduction phase. When light is off, ATP and NADPH drop, reducing RuBisCO's activity (feedback inhibition). RuBisCO is also directly regulated by a light-activated enzyme (thioredoxin system) that modulates its conformation. Magnesium ions in the stroma increase during illumination, activating RuBisCO and other Calvin cycle enzymes.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the role of RuBisCO, and why is it the most abundant protein on Earth?
2. How many ATP and NADPH are required to synthesize one glucose via the Calvin cycle?
3. Explain why the Calvin cycle is called the "dark reactions," even though it depends on the light reactions.

### Photorespiration: The Oxygenase Activity of RuBisCO
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** photorespiration-rubisco-oxygenase-activity
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** oxygenase activity · glycolate · 2-phosphoglycolate · C3 cycle · evolutionary baggage
**Summary:** RuBisCO can catalyze both carboxylation and oxygenation; under high O₂/low CO₂, oxygenation predominates, leading to wasteful photorespiration.

#### The Problem: RuBisCO's Oxygenase Activity
RuBisCO evolved billions of years ago when Earth's atmosphere was high in CO₂ and low in O₂. The enzyme evolved to favor carboxylation over oxygenation at ancient atmospheric ratios. Today, O₂ is 210 mM and CO₂ is 0.04% (~17 mM). RuBisCO can catalyze both reactions: CO₂ + RuBP → 2 × 3-PG (carboxylation) and O₂ + RuBP → 3-PG + 2-phosphoglycolate (oxygenation). At current O₂/CO₂ ratios, oxygenation occurs ~25% of the time in C3 plants.

#### The Photorespiration Pathway
When RuBisCO catalyzes oxygenation, 2-phosphoglycolate (2-PG) is produced instead of 3-PG. 2-PG cannot directly enter the Calvin cycle; it's dephosphorylated to glycolate. Glycolate is transported to peroxisomes, where it's oxidized, producing CO₂ and reducing power. Some of the carbon is lost as CO₂; some is converted back to 3-PG and returned to the chloroplast. The cycle wastes energy (ATP) and carbon, reducing photosynthetic efficiency by ~25%.

#### Why Photorespiration Persists
Photorespiration seems wasteful and maladaptive — why hasn't it been selected against? The answer is that RuBisCO has two activities (carboxylation and oxygenation) from a single active site. Evolution has optimized the ratio but cannot eliminate oxygenation without disrupting carboxylation. Additionally, the oxygenase activity may provide a protective mechanism under stress: under high light and low CO₂ (photoinhibition risk), photorespiration dissipates excess light energy safely.

#### C4 and CAM Adaptations to Reduce Photorespiration
C4 plants concentrate CO₂ around RuBisCO via a preliminary CO₂ fixation step using phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEP carboxylase), which is specific for CO₂ and doesn't have oxygenase activity. This produces oxaloacetate (4-carbon), which is decarboxylated in bundle sheath cells to regenerate CO₂ at high concentration, minimizing oxygenation. CAM plants (cacti) perform CO₂ fixation at night (cooler, stomata open) and photosynthesize during the day (stomata closed, high CO₂ inside leaf).

#### Evolution and Geological Time
This story illustrates how evolution leaves "scars" — vestigial activities and inefficiencies that reflect ancient history. RuBisCO is a molecular fossil of Earth's ancient atmosphere. As CO₂ levels rose millions of years ago, the oxygenase activity became more of a burden. But C3 plants persist globally because they're not severely limited (25% loss isn't fatal) and because they have other advantages (faster growth in high CO₂).

#### Review Questions
1. What is photorespiration, and why does RuBisCO catalyze both carboxylation and oxygenation?
2. How do C4 plants reduce photorespiration compared to C3 plants?
3. Why hasn't photorespiration been completely eliminated by natural selection?

### Photosynthesis Application: Photosynthetic Efficiency and Environmental Factors
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** photosynthetic-efficiency-light-co2-temperature
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** photosynthetic rate · limiting factors · light saturation · compensation point · productivity
**Summary:** Photosynthetic rate depends on light, CO₂, temperature, and water; understanding these relationships predicts plant growth and crop yield.

#### Photosynthetic Rate and Light Intensity
At low light, photosynthetic rate increases linearly with light intensity (light-limited rate). As light increases, the rate plateaus (light-saturation point), where all photosystem reaction centers are processing electrons. Beyond saturation, increasing light doesn't increase photosynthetic rate (may even decrease it due to photoinhibition). The light compensation point is the light intensity where photosynthesis equals respiration (no net carbon gain). Shade plants have lower compensation points (tolerate low light); sun plants require higher light for maximum rate.

#### CO₂ Concentration and the Carboxylation Plateau
At low CO₂ (below 150 ppm), photosynthetic rate is limited by RuBisCO's carboxylation rate (Km ~3 μM). Increasing CO₂ increases the rate until saturation (~1000 ppm in some plants), where RuBisCO is working maximally. Modern atmospheric CO₂ (410 ppm) is in the rising part of this curve; plants are CO₂-limited. Elevated CO₂ (e.g., in greenhouses) increases photosynthetic rate and yield. However, very high CO₂ (>1500 ppm) can inhibit transpiration and reduce nutrient uptake, offsetting gains.

#### Temperature Optimum and Enzyme Kinetics
Photosynthetic enzymes have temperature optima (typically 25–35°C for temperate plants, 40–50°C for heat-adapted plants). Below optimum, enzyme kinetics are slower; above optimum, enzymes denature. Leaf temperature is often 5–15°C above air temperature due to solar heating and reduced transpiration. On very hot days, plants may close stomata to avoid water loss, but this increases leaf temperature and reduces photosynthesis — a trade-off between water conservation and carbon gain.

#### Water Availability and Stomatal Conductance
Water is necessary for photosynthesis (as a substrate) and for maintaining turgor (keeping stomata open). During drought, water potential in leaves drops, and guard cells lose turgor, closing stomata. Closed stomata reduce CO₂ entry, limiting photosynthesis. Severely drought-stressed plants may have photosynthetic rates near zero despite bright sunshine. Irrigation increases photosynthetic rate and yield, but can cause waterlogging and root hypoxia in some soils.

#### Worked Example: Crop Yield and Photosynthetic Rate
A corn crop's yield depends on total carbon assimilation during the growing season. Photosynthetic rate depends on light (latitude, season, cloudiness), temperature (seasonal cycle), water (rainfall or irrigation), and CO₂ (relatively constant). In temperate regions, late spring/early summer has optimal light and temperature; midsummer drought can limit yield. Farmers use irrigation to offset drought and apply fertilizer to ensure adequate nitrogen for enzyme synthesis. Under optimal conditions, corn can assimilate 10–15 tons C/hectare/year, yielding grain with ~40% of that carbon.

#### Modeling Photosynthesis: Light Response Curves
Scientists measure net photosynthesis (gross photosynthesis minus respiration) versus light intensity, generating a light response curve. The curve's shape depends on plant type, temperature, and CO₂. From the curve, researchers determine: compensation point, saturation intensity, and maximum rate. These parameters predict how a plant will perform under different environmental conditions and are used in crop models to estimate regional yield.

#### Review Questions
1. Define the light compensation point and explain why it varies between sun and shade plants.
2. Why is atmospheric CO₂ concentration not currently a limiting factor for photosynthesis in most natural ecosystems?
3. How do water stress and stomatal closure reduce photosynthetic rate?
