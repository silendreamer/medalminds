#### Enzymes as Biological Catalysts
**Enzymes** are proteins (and some RNA molecules called **ribozymes**) that accelerate chemical reactions by lowering the **activation energy** — the minimum energy required for a reaction to proceed. Enzymes are not consumed by the reactions they catalyze and can be reused many times. Each enzyme is highly specific for its **substrate(s)**, determined by the complementary shape of its **active site**. The overall reaction rate (**turnover number**) for some enzymes can exceed 10^6 substrate molecules per second.

#### Active Site Models
Two models describe how enzymes bind substrates. The classic **lock-and-key model** proposes that the enzyme's active site is a rigid, pre-formed cavity that exactly matches the substrate's shape. The more accurate **induced-fit model** shows that the active site is flexible and undergoes a conformational change upon substrate binding, optimizing interactions and positioning catalytic residues. The transient **enzyme-substrate (ES) complex** stabilizes the **transition state**, lowering the energy barrier and accelerating product formation.

#### Environmental Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity
Enzyme activity is sensitive to:
- **Temperature** — activity increases with temperature up to an optimum; excess heat causes **denaturation**, unfolding the active site.
- **pH** — each enzyme has an optimal pH (e.g., pepsin ~2, trypsin ~8); extremes disrupt ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds that maintain tertiary structure.
- **Substrate concentration** — activity rises until all active sites are saturated (**Vmax**); **Km** (Michaelis constant) measures substrate affinity.

#### Enzyme Inhibition
**Competitive inhibitors** structurally resemble the substrate and bind reversibly to the **active site**, blocking substrate access; their effect can be overcome by increasing substrate concentration. **Noncompetitive inhibitors** bind at an **allosteric site** (not the active site), changing the enzyme's conformation and reducing its catalytic efficiency regardless of substrate concentration. **Irreversible inhibitors** (e.g., nerve agents, aspirin) form covalent bonds that permanently inactivate the enzyme. **Allosteric regulation** by activators or inhibitors provides fine-tuned metabolic control; **feedback inhibition** (product inhibits an upstream enzyme) is a common homeostatic mechanism.

#### Review Questions
1. How does the induced-fit model differ from the lock-and-key model of enzyme-substrate binding?
2. A researcher adds excess substrate to a reaction and finds that a certain inhibitor no longer reduces the reaction rate. Is the inhibitor competitive or noncompetitive? Explain.
3. Why does raising temperature beyond an enzyme's optimum eventually decrease reaction rate?
