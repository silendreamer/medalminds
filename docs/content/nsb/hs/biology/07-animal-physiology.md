## Subtopic: Homeostasis

### Homeostasis: Maintaining the Internal Environment
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** homeostasis-internal-environment
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** homeostasis · negative feedback · set point · thermoregulation
**Summary:** Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment through feedback mechanisms that counter deviations from a set point.

#### The Concept of Homeostasis
Homeostasis (from Greek: homos = same, stasis = standing) is the physiological process by which organisms maintain stable internal conditions despite changing external environments. Claude Bernard (19th century) first described the concept of the *milieu intérieur* (internal environment). Parameters regulated include body temperature, blood glucose, blood pH (7.35–7.45), blood oxygen/CO2, blood pressure, osmolarity, and ion concentrations. Failure of homeostasis leads to disease or death — diabetic ketoacidosis (blood glucose too high), hypothermia (body temperature too low), and alkalosis/acidosis (blood pH out of range) are examples.

#### Negative Feedback
The most common homeostatic mechanism. A change in a variable (stimulus) is detected by a receptor/sensor, information is processed by a control center, and an effector responds to counteract the change and restore the set point. The response opposes the stimulus (negative = opposing). Example: body temperature regulation. Thermoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect a rise in core temperature; the hypothalamus (control center) activates cooling effectors: vasodilation (blood vessels near skin dilate to radiate heat), sweating (evaporative cooling), and behavioral responses (seeking shade). When temperature returns to set point, the response is switched off.

#### Positive Feedback
Amplifies the stimulus rather than opposing it — moves the variable further from the set point. Used for processes that need to go to completion rapidly: childbirth (uterine contractions stimulate oxytocin release → stronger contractions → more oxytocin), blood clotting (platelet aggregation triggers more platelet activation → clot formation), action potential depolarization (Na+ influx causes more Na+ channels to open). Positive feedback loops must be broken by an external event (delivery of baby; completion of clot; reaching peak membrane potential).

#### Thermoregulation: Ectotherms vs. Endotherms
**Ectotherms** (poikilotherms): body temperature determined by environment; behavior is primary thermoregulatory tool (basking, seeking shade). Lizards, fish, invertebrates. Advantage: low metabolic energy cost. **Endotherms** (homeotherms): generate metabolic heat to maintain stable body temperature independent of environment. Birds and mammals. Advantage: active at cold temperatures, consistent enzyme activity. Cost: ~80–90% of energy budget goes to maintaining body temperature. **Heterotherms**: intermediate — some fish (tuna), torpor/hibernation in mammals.

#### Review Questions
1. Define homeostasis and explain why it is necessary for the chemical reactions of life to proceed normally.
2. A person's blood glucose level drops below the set point. Describe the negative feedback loop that would restore normal levels, naming the receptor, control center, effector, and response.
3. Why is the positive feedback loop during childbirth adaptive even though it moves the system further from a "set point"?

### Osmoregulation and Excretion
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** osmoregulation-excretion
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** osmosis · osmoregulation · nephron · aquaporin · ADH
**Summary:** Osmoregulation is the control of water and solute balance; the kidney is the primary osmoregulatory organ in vertebrates, filtering blood and producing urine.

#### Osmosis and Osmolarity
Water moves by osmosis from low solute concentration (high water potential) to high solute concentration (low water potential) across a semipermeable membrane. **Osmolarity** measures solute concentration (mOsm/L). Cells placed in a hypotonic solution (lower solute than cytoplasm) gain water and may lyse; in hypertonic solution they shrink (crenate). Human blood plasma osmolarity: ~290 mOsm/L, tightly regulated.

#### The Nephron: Structure and Function
The functional unit of the kidney. From Bowman's capsule to collecting duct: **(1) Glomerulus/Bowman's capsule** — filtration by hydrostatic pressure; everything small enough passes into filtrate (water, glucose, urea, ions; not proteins or blood cells). **(2) Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)** — reabsorption of ~65% of filtered water, all glucose and amino acids, Na+, K+, HCO3−. Active transport. **(3) Loop of Henle** — descending limb: water reabsorbed (aquaporins, impermeable to solutes); ascending limb: NaCl actively pumped out (impermeable to water). Creates medullary osmotic gradient. **(4) Distal convoluted tubule (DCT)** — selective reabsorption of Na+/K+, regulated by aldosterone. **(5) Collecting duct** — water reabsorption regulated by ADH (antidiuretic hormone / vasopressin); inserts aquaporins into duct cells when ADH is high.

#### ADH and Aldosterone
**ADH (vasopressin)**: released from posterior pituitary when plasma osmolarity rises (detected by hypothalamic osmoreceptors) or blood volume falls. ADH inserts aquaporin-2 channels into collecting duct cells → more water reabsorbed → concentrated urine. Alcohol inhibits ADH → frequent, dilute urination → dehydration. **Aldosterone**: steroid hormone from adrenal cortex, released when blood Na+ falls or blood pressure drops (via renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system). Stimulates Na+ reabsorption in DCT and collecting duct → water follows osmotically → blood volume and pressure increase.

#### Review Questions
1. Trace a molecule of urea from the glomerulus to the renal pelvis, naming each nephron segment and what happens to urea at each stage.
2. A person drinks no water for 12 hours. Predict the changes in ADH levels and urine concentration, and explain the mechanism.
3. Why does alcohol cause frequent urination, and what problem can this create?

### Homeostasis Application: Diabetes and Blood Glucose Regulation
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** homeostasis-diabetes-blood-glucose
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** insulin · glucagon · Type 1 vs Type 2 diabetes · pancreas · islets of Langerhans
**Summary:** Blood glucose regulation by insulin and glucagon is a classic negative feedback homeostatic system; disruption causes diabetes mellitus.

#### Normal Blood Glucose Regulation
Normal fasting blood glucose: 70–100 mg/dL. After a meal, blood glucose rises → beta cells in islets of Langerhans (pancreas) release **insulin** → liver, muscle, and fat cells take up glucose (GLUT4 transporter in muscle/fat requires insulin) → liver stores glucose as glycogen → blood glucose returns to set point. When blood glucose falls (between meals, exercise) → alpha cells release **glucagon** → liver breaks down glycogen (glycogenolysis) and makes glucose from non-carbohydrate sources (gluconeogenesis) → blood glucose rises.

#### Type 1 vs. Type 2 Diabetes
**Type 1 (insulin-dependent)**: autoimmune destruction of beta cells → no insulin production → cells cannot take up glucose → hyperglycemia. Treatment requires exogenous insulin. Onset typically in childhood. Without insulin, the body metabolizes fats → ketone bodies accumulate → diabetic ketoacidosis (life-threatening). **Type 2 (insulin-resistant)**: cells become resistant to insulin (GLUT4 transporter not responding) → pancreas produces more insulin → eventually beta cells exhaust → hyperglycemia. Associated with obesity, sedentary lifestyle. Treatment: lifestyle changes, metformin (reduces hepatic glucose output), eventually insulin. **Gestational diabetes**: occurs during pregnancy; resolves after delivery but increases risk of Type 2 later.

#### Review Questions
1. A person with Type 1 diabetes stops taking insulin. What happens to blood glucose levels and why? What is the danger of the metabolic switch to fat metabolism in this case?
2. Contrast the mechanisms underlying Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes.
3. Why does glucagon target the liver rather than muscle for glycogenolysis?

**Summary:** Cross-subtopic homeostasis review connecting thermoregulation, osmoregulation, and blood glucose control.

#### Cross-Concept Connections
All homeostatic mechanisms share the same architecture: sensor → control center → effector → corrective response → feedback to sensor. The integrative physiological response to exercise illustrates how multiple homeostatic systems operate simultaneously and interact.

## Subtopic: Nervous System & Signal Transduction

### Neuron Structure and the Action Potential
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** neuron-structure-action-potential
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** neuron · resting potential · action potential · depolarization · refractory period
**Summary:** Neurons transmit electrical signals as action potentials — rapid, all-or-nothing changes in membrane potential driven by ion channel opening and closing.

#### Neuron Anatomy
**Dendrites**: receive signals from other neurons or sensory receptors; branch extensively. **Cell body (soma)**: contains nucleus; integrates inputs. **Axon**: long process that carries action potential away from cell body; may be myelinated. **Axon terminals (synaptic boutons)**: release neurotransmitters into synapse. **Myelin sheath**: made by Schwann cells (PNS) or oligodendrocytes (CNS); insulates axon; speeds conduction. **Nodes of Ranvier**: gaps in myelin where action potentials regenerate (saltatory conduction — "jumping" between nodes; much faster than unmyelinated conduction).

#### Resting Membrane Potential
~-70 mV (inside negative relative to outside). Maintained by: (1) Na+/K+ ATPase pump — actively moves 3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in per cycle (net outward positive charge movement); (2) K+ leak channels — K+ leaks out down its concentration gradient (more permeable at rest than Na+). At rest: high Na+ outside, high K+ inside. The membrane is polarized.

#### The Action Potential
**Threshold**: stimulus sufficient to depolarize membrane to ~-55 mV triggers action potential. **Depolarization**: voltage-gated Na+ channels open rapidly → Na+ rushes in → membrane potential rises to ~+30 mV. **Repolarization**: Na+ channels inactivate; voltage-gated K+ channels open slowly → K+ rushes out → membrane potential falls. **Hyperpolarization (undershoot)**: K+ channels slow to close → membrane briefly more negative than rest. **Refractory period**: absolute refractory (no new action potential possible — Na+ channels inactivated); relative refractory (strong stimulus can trigger action potential — K+ channels still open). All-or-nothing: either full action potential or none.

#### Saltatory Conduction
Myelinated axons conduct faster because action potentials jump between nodes of Ranvier (unmyelinated gaps). Conduction velocity: unmyelinated ~0.5–2 m/s; myelinated up to ~120 m/s. Multiple sclerosis: autoimmune destruction of myelin → slowed or blocked conduction.

#### Review Questions
1. Draw and label the phases of an action potential. For each phase, state which ion channels open or close and in which direction ions move.
2. Why is the action potential described as "all-or-nothing"? What is the consequence of not reaching threshold?
3. Why does multiple sclerosis slow nerve conduction? How does myelination normally increase conduction velocity?

### Synaptic Transmission and Neurotransmitters
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** synaptic-transmission-neurotransmitters
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** synapse · neurotransmitter · EPSP · IPSP · reuptake
**Summary:** Synaptic transmission involves neurotransmitter release, receptor binding, and postsynaptic potential generation; drugs and toxins act by modifying these steps.

#### Chemical Synapse Mechanism
Action potential reaches axon terminal → voltage-gated Ca²+ channels open → Ca²+ influx → synaptic vesicles fuse with presynaptic membrane (exocytosis) → neurotransmitter released into synaptic cleft → diffuses across cleft → binds postsynaptic receptors. **Ionotropic receptors**: directly linked ion channels — fast response (ms). **Metabotropic receptors (GPCRs)**: linked to G-proteins → second messenger cascades → slower but more diverse effects. Neurotransmitter is then cleared by: (1) enzymatic degradation (acetylcholinesterase breaks down ACh), (2) reuptake into presynaptic terminal (serotonin, dopamine), or (3) diffusion.

#### Excitatory and Inhibitory Postsynaptic Potentials
**EPSP** (excitatory postsynaptic potential): depolarization — brings membrane potential closer to threshold; caused by opening Na+ or closing K+ channels. **IPSP** (inhibitory postsynaptic potential): hyperpolarization — brings potential further from threshold; caused by opening Cl− channels (Cl− rushes in) or K+ channels. **Summation**: temporal summation (multiple signals from same neuron in rapid succession) + spatial summation (signals from multiple neurons). Axon hillock integrates all EPSPs and IPSPs — fires if net depolarization reaches threshold.

#### Key Neurotransmitters
- **Acetylcholine (ACh)**: neuromuscular junction; autonomic ganglia; memory/attention (CNS). Degraded by acetylcholinesterase. Nerve agents (sarin) and insecticides (organophosphates) inhibit acetylcholinesterase → ACh accumulates → continuous muscle stimulation → spastic paralysis.
- **Glutamate**: major excitatory NT in CNS (NMDA receptors important in learning/memory)
- **GABA**: major inhibitory NT in CNS; alcohol and benzodiazepines enhance GABA activity
- **Dopamine**: reward, movement (degeneration → Parkinson's disease)
- **Serotonin**: mood, sleep; SSRIs block reuptake → more serotonin in synapse → antidepressant effect
- **Norepinephrine**: fight-or-flight; attention

#### Review Questions
1. Trace the sequence of events from an action potential arriving at an axon terminal to the generation of an EPSP in the postsynaptic cell.
2. How does the drug curare (used in blowgun darts) cause muscle paralysis? (Hint: it blocks ACh receptors at the neuromuscular junction.)
3. SSRIs treat depression by blocking serotonin reuptake. Explain how this increases serotonin's effect at the synapse.

### Nervous System Organization
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** nervous-system-organization
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** CNS · PNS · sympathetic · parasympathetic · reflex arc
**Summary:** The nervous system is hierarchically organized into CNS and PNS divisions; the autonomic nervous system regulates involuntary functions through antagonistic sympathetic and parasympathetic branches.

#### Central vs. Peripheral Nervous System
**CNS**: brain and spinal cord; enclosed in bone (skull and vertebral column); protected by meninges and blood-brain barrier. **PNS**: all nervous tissue outside CNS — cranial nerves (12 pairs from brain), spinal nerves (31 pairs from spinal cord), sensory and motor pathways, ganglia. PNS subdivisions: **somatic** (voluntary control of skeletal muscle; sensory from external environment) and **autonomic** (involuntary control of smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands; sensory from internal organs).

#### Autonomic Nervous System: Sympathetic vs. Parasympathetic
Both are efferent (motor to effectors) and tonically active (always some firing). **Sympathetic** ("fight or flight"): preganglionic neurons in thoracolumbar spinal cord; short preganglionic → ganglion → long postganglionic (norepinephrine); effects: increased heart rate, dilated pupils, bronchodilation, decreased digestion, increased blood glucose. **Parasympathetic** ("rest and digest"): preganglionic from brainstem (cranial nerves) and sacral spinal cord; long preganglionic → ganglion near target → short postganglionic (acetylcholine); effects: decreased heart rate, constricted pupils, increased digestion, decreased blood glucose, bronchoconstriction. Most organs receive dual innervation — antagonistic effects.

#### Reflex Arc
Fastest neural responses bypass the brain: sensory receptor → afferent neuron → spinal cord interneuron (integration) → efferent neuron → effector. Knee-jerk (patellar) reflex is monosynaptic (no interneuron): stretch receptor in patellar tendon → afferent neuron → motor neuron → quadriceps muscle. Withdrawal reflex (touching a hot stove) is polysynaptic: pain receptor → interneurons in spinal cord → motor neuron to withdrawal muscles; simultaneous: pain signal travels to brain (slower) for conscious awareness and learning.

#### Review Questions
1. Compare the effects of the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system on heart rate, pupil size, and digestive activity.
2. Explain why a reflex arc is faster than a voluntary response that requires conscious processing in the brain.
3. What neurotransmitter is used by parasympathetic postganglionic neurons? By sympathetic postganglionic neurons?

## Subtopic: Endocrine System & Hormones

### Hormone Types and Mechanisms of Action
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hormone-types-mechanisms
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** peptide hormone · steroid hormone · receptor · second messenger · cAMP
**Summary:** Hormones are chemical messengers that act at a distance; peptide hormones act via membrane receptors and second messengers, while steroid hormones enter cells and act on DNA directly.

#### Peptide Hormones
Water-soluble; cannot cross the lipid bilayer; bind to membrane receptors. Binding activates **signal transduction cascades** via G-proteins (most) or receptor tyrosine kinases (insulin). G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) pathway: hormone → GPCR → G-protein activates adenylyl cyclase → ATP → **cAMP** (second messenger) → protein kinase A → phosphorylation cascade → cellular response. Response is fast (seconds to minutes), amplified (one hormone molecule → millions of cAMP molecules → millions of activated enzymes). Examples: epinephrine, glucagon, ADH, FSH, LH, TSH, insulin (receptor tyrosine kinase pathway).

#### Steroid Hormones
Lipid-soluble; synthesized from cholesterol; diffuse directly through plasma membrane; bind intracellular receptors. Hormone-receptor complex enters nucleus → binds hormone response elements (HRE) in DNA → alters gene transcription → change in protein synthesis. Response is slow (hours to days) but long-lasting. Examples: cortisol, aldosterone, estrogen, progesterone, testosterone, calcitriol (vitamin D). Important for long-term regulation: puberty, pregnancy, stress response, anti-inflammation.

#### Comparison
| Feature | Peptide | Steroid |
|---|---|---|
| Solubility | Water-soluble | Lipid-soluble |
| Receptor location | Plasma membrane | Intracellular (cytoplasm/nucleus) |
| Second messenger | Yes (cAMP, etc.) | No |
| Speed | Fast | Slow |
| Duration | Short | Long |
| Precursor | Amino acids | Cholesterol |

#### Review Questions
1. Explain why steroid hormones can enter cells directly while peptide hormones cannot.

3. Why are anabolic steroids banned in sports? What mechanism allows them to build muscle, and what are the side effects of long-term use?

### Major Endocrine Glands and Their Hormones
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** major-endocrine-glands-hormones
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** hypothalamus · pituitary · thyroid · adrenal · gonads · tropic hormones
**Summary:** The endocrine system consists of glands that produce hormones; the hypothalamus-pituitary axis controls most major endocrine glands through hierarchical feedback loops.

#### Hypothalamus-Pituitary Axis
The hypothalamus produces **releasing hormones** (GnRH, TRH, CRH, GHRH) and **inhibiting hormones** that travel via the portal blood system to the **anterior pituitary** (adenohypophysis), which releases **tropic hormones** (FSH, LH, TSH, ACTH, GH, prolactin) that target other endocrine glands. **Posterior pituitary** (neurohypophysis) stores and releases ADH and oxytocin (made in hypothalamus). Negative feedback: target gland hormones inhibit hypothalamus and anterior pituitary. Example: CRH → ACTH → cortisol → cortisol inhibits CRH and ACTH release.

#### Key Glands and Hormones
- **Thyroid**: T3 (triiodothyronine) and T4 (thyroxine) — metabolic rate, growth, development; calcitonin — lowers blood Ca²+
- **Parathyroid**: PTH (parathyroid hormone) — raises blood Ca²+ (activates osteoclasts, increases Ca²+ reabsorption in kidney, activates vitamin D)
- **Adrenal cortex**: cortisol (stress, anti-inflammatory, gluconeogenesis), aldosterone (Na+/water balance)
- **Adrenal medulla**: epinephrine and norepinephrine (fight-or-flight; released quickly)
- **Pancreas**: insulin (lowers blood glucose), glucagon (raises blood glucose), somatostatin (inhibits both)
- **Pineal gland**: melatonin (circadian rhythm, sleep-wake cycle)
- **Ovaries**: estrogen (follicular development, female secondary sex characteristics, uterine proliferation), progesterone (maintains pregnancy, luteal phase)
- **Testes**: testosterone (male secondary sex characteristics, spermatogenesis)

#### Review Questions
1. Explain why Addison's disease (adrenal cortex insufficiency) leads to low blood pressure, low blood Na+, and high blood K+.
2. A person has hypothyroidism (low T3/T4). Predict TSH and TRH levels and explain the feedback loop involved.
3. Distinguish the adrenal cortex from the adrenal medulla in terms of the types of hormones produced, their precursors, and the speed of their response.

### Endocrine Disorders: Application
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** endocrine-disorders-application
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** hyperthyroidism · Cushing's syndrome · acromegaly · diabetes insipidus
**Summary:** Understanding endocrine disorders requires tracing hormone feedback loops and predicting how excesses or deficiencies alter physiology.

#### Case Studies
**Hyperthyroidism (Graves' disease)**: autoimmune antibodies mimic TSH → continuous thyroid stimulation → excess T3/T4 → high metabolic rate, weight loss, heat intolerance, rapid heart rate, exophthalmos. TSH is suppressed by negative feedback. Treatment: radioactive iodine, thyroid surgery, anti-thyroid drugs.

**Cushing's syndrome**: excess cortisol (from pituitary adenoma producing excess ACTH, or adrenal tumor, or exogenous corticosteroids) → central obesity ("moon face," "buffalo hump"), hyperglycemia, immunosuppression, osteoporosis, hypertension, easy bruising. ACTH levels differ by cause (high in pituitary tumor, low in adrenal tumor).

**Acromegaly**: excess growth hormone in adults (after growth plates fuse) from pituitary adenoma → enlarged hands, feet, jaw, internal organs; arthritis; cardiomegaly. In children before growth plate fusion, excess GH causes gigantism.

**Diabetes insipidus**: insufficient ADH (central DI: hypothalamic/pituitary damage) or kidney unresponsive to ADH (nephrogenic DI) → excretion of large amounts of dilute urine → dehydration → extreme thirst. Distinguished from diabetes mellitus: no glucose in urine.

#### Review Questions
1. A patient has elevated blood glucose, weight gain around the abdomen, thinning skin, and frequent infections. Lab results show extremely high cortisol and low ACTH. What is the most likely diagnosis and where is the tumor?
2. Why do patients on long-term corticosteroid therapy (e.g., for asthma) have suppressed adrenal function when they stop taking the drug suddenly?
3. Compare and contrast diabetes insipidus and diabetes mellitus in terms of symptoms, underlying mechanisms, and what is found in the urine.

## Subtopic: Cardiovascular & Respiratory Systems

### The Heart: Structure and Cardiac Cycle
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** heart-structure-cardiac-cycle
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** cardiac cycle · systole · diastole · SA node · cardiac output
**Summary:** The heart is a dual pump; the cardiac cycle involves coordinated contraction (systole) and relaxation (diastole) controlled by the sinoatrial node.

#### Heart Anatomy
Four chambers: right atrium (receives deoxygenated blood from vena cava) → right ventricle (pumps to pulmonary artery → lungs) → left atrium (receives oxygenated blood from pulmonary veins) → left ventricle (pumps to aorta → body). Left ventricular wall is thicker (higher pressure needed for systemic circulation). **Valves**: atrioventricular (AV) valves — tricuspid (right) and bicuspid/mitral (left) — prevent backflow from ventricles to atria; semilunar valves — pulmonary and aortic — prevent backflow from arteries to ventricles. Heart sounds ("lub-dub"): AV valve closure (S1) and semilunar valve closure (S2).

#### The Cardiac Cycle
**Diastole**: heart relaxes; blood fills atria and ventricles passively; ~70% of ventricular filling. **Atrial systole**: atria contract; complete ventricular filling (~30% more). **Ventricular systole**: ventricles contract; AV valves close (S1); pressure builds; semilunar valves open; blood ejected into pulmonary artery and aorta; semilunar valves close (S2) when pressure falls. Cardiac output (CO) = Stroke volume (SV) × Heart rate (HR). At rest: CO ≈ 70 mL/beat × 70 beats/min ≈ 5 L/min = entire blood volume.

#### Intrinsic Conduction System
**SA node** (sinoatrial node; "pacemaker"): spontaneously depolarizes ~70 times/min; located in right atrium; initiates each heartbeat. Signal spreads through atria → **AV node** (atrioventricular node): delays signal ~0.1 sec (allows ventricles to fill) → **Bundle of His** → **Purkinje fibers**: spread signal rapidly through ventricular walls from apex upward → coordinated ventricular contraction. ECG (electrocardiogram) detects: P wave (atrial depolarization), QRS complex (ventricular depolarization), T wave (ventricular repolarization).

#### Review Questions

### Gas Exchange and Respiratory System
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** gas-exchange-respiratory-system
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** alveoli · partial pressure · hemoglobin · oxygen dissociation curve · Bohr effect
**Summary:** Gas exchange occurs by diffusion down partial pressure gradients across the alveolar surface; hemoglobin transports oxygen, and its affinity for O2 is regulated by pH, CO2, and temperature.

#### Respiratory Anatomy and Ventilation
Air path: nasal cavity/pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveolar ducts → alveoli. Alveoli: tiny air sacs (~300 million) with total surface area ~70 m²; lined with surfactant (reduces surface tension, prevents collapse); surrounded by capillaries. **Diaphragm**: primary muscle of inspiration; contracts → increases thoracic volume → pressure decreases → air flows in (Boyle's Law). Expiration at rest: passive (diaphragm relaxes); forced expiration: internal intercostals + abdominal muscles.

#### Gas Exchange by Diffusion
Gases move down partial pressure gradients. **Pulmonary gas exchange** (alveoli): PO2 alveoli (~104 mmHg) > PO2 blood entering (~40 mmHg) → O2 diffuses into blood. PCO2 blood entering (~45 mmHg) > PCO2 alveoli (~40 mmHg) → CO2 diffuses into alveoli. **Systemic gas exchange** (tissues): PO2 blood (~100 mmHg) > PO2 tissues (~40 mmHg) → O2 diffuses to tissues. PCO2 tissues (~45 mmHg) > PCO2 blood (~40 mmHg) → CO2 enters blood.

#### Hemoglobin and the Oxygen Dissociation Curve
Each hemoglobin (Hb) molecule has 4 heme groups, each binding 1 O2. Cooperative binding: binding of one O2 increases affinity for next (sigmoidal curve). **Bohr effect**: CO2 ↑ (or pH ↓, or temperature ↑) → shifts O2 dissociation curve rightward → Hb releases O2 more readily. This is adaptive: active tissues produce CO2 and acid → Hb releases more O2 precisely where needed. CO2 transported: ~70% as bicarbonate (HCO3−), ~23% bound to Hb (carbaminohemoglobin), ~7% dissolved. Carbon monoxide binds Hb with 200× greater affinity than O2 → displaces O2 → CO poisoning.

3. Why does CO poisoning produce a "cherry-red" coloration of the skin (carboxyhemoglobin has a bright red color), and why is it more dangerous than an equivalent reduction in hemoglobin due to anemia?

### Cardiovascular/Respiratory Application: Exercise Physiology
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** cardiovascular-respiratory-exercise-physiology
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** cardiac output · VO2 max · aerobic · anaerobic · lactate threshold
**Summary:** Exercise challenges the cardiovascular and respiratory systems; understanding the integrated physiological response reveals how these systems work together.

#### Cardiovascular Response to Exercise
Increased muscle activity → increased O2 demand → increased CO2 production → increased cardiac output (HR increases via sympathetic stimulation; SV increases via increased venous return and Frank-Starling mechanism). During maximal exercise: CO can increase from 5 L/min to 20–25 L/min (trained athletes may reach 40 L/min). **VO2 max**: maximum O2 consumption rate — the best single measure of aerobic fitness. **Frank-Starling law**: the greater the ventricular filling (end-diastolic volume), the more forceful the contraction — heart automatically adjusts output to match venous return.

#### Aerobic vs. Anaerobic Exercise
Aerobic: O2 supply meets demand → pyruvate enters Krebs cycle → full ATP yield (~30–32 ATP/glucose). Sustainable. Anaerobic: O2 supply insufficient → pyruvate → lactate (prevents NADH accumulation, allows glycolysis to continue). Rapid ATP but produces H+ → lactic acidosis → fatigue. **Lactate threshold**: exercise intensity at which lactate begins accumulating faster than it can be cleared; trained athletes have higher lactate thresholds. After exercise: "oxygen debt" (EPOC — excess post-exercise oxygen consumption) repays ATP and creatine phosphate debt, converts lactate back to glucose.

3. A trained marathon runner has a resting heart rate of 45 bpm. Explain physiologically why a trained endurance athlete has a lower resting heart rate than an untrained person, even though cardiac output at rest is the same.

### Innate vs. Adaptive Immunity
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** innate-vs-adaptive-immunity
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** innate immunity · adaptive immunity · antigen · pathogen-associated molecular pattern · toll-like receptor
**Summary:** The immune system has two branches: innate immunity provides rapid, non-specific defense; adaptive immunity provides slow but specific and memory-generating responses.

#### Innate Immunity: First and Second Lines
**First line** (physical barriers): skin (keratin), mucus, cilia, stomach acid, lysozyme (saliva, tears). **Second line** (innate immune cells and molecules): active when pathogens penetrate barriers. Key components: **phagocytes** (neutrophils — first responders, short-lived; macrophages — long-lived tissue residents; dendritic cells — bridges to adaptive immunity); **natural killer (NK) cells** — kill cells displaying abnormal surface signals (missing MHC I → virally infected or cancer cells); **complement system** — cascade of plasma proteins that lyse bacteria, coat pathogens for phagocytosis (opsonization), and attract immune cells (chemotaxis); **interferons** — secreted by virally infected cells to signal neighboring cells to increase antiviral defenses.

Pattern recognition: innate immune cells recognize **pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)** via **pattern recognition receptors (PRRs)** such as **Toll-like receptors (TLRs)**. PAMPs include LPS (bacterial outer membrane), flagellin, double-stranded RNA (viruses). PAMPs are conserved across microbial groups (humans don't have them) → discriminates self from non-self without needing prior exposure.

#### Adaptive Immunity Overview
Specific, slow (days to weeks for primary response), generates immunological memory. Two branches: **humoral immunity** (B cells → antibodies → targets extracellular pathogens); **cell-mediated immunity** (T cells → target intracellular pathogens and cancer cells). Requires antigen presentation: dendritic cells phagocytose pathogens → present peptide antigens on MHC molecules → activate T cells in lymph nodes. **Clonal selection**: only lymphocytes with receptors matching the antigen are activated and clonally expanded.

### Adaptive Immunity: B Cells, T Cells, and Antibodies
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** adaptive-immunity-b-t-cells-antibodies
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** B cell · T cell · antibody · MHC · clonal selection · immunological memory
**Summary:** B cells produce antibodies; T cells coordinate immune responses and kill infected cells; both cell types generate immunological memory after primary infection.

#### B Cells and Antibodies
B cells: originate and mature in **bone marrow**; express **B cell receptors (BCRs)** (membrane-bound antibodies). Activation: antigen binds BCR + T helper cell (CD4+) provides co-stimulation via cytokines → B cell proliferates (clonal expansion) → differentiates into **plasma cells** (antibody secretion factories) and **memory B cells**. Antibody (immunoglobulin) structure: 2 heavy + 2 light chains; **variable region** (antigen-specific; determines specificity); **constant region** (determines antibody class and effector function). Antibody classes: IgG (most abundant, long-term protection, crosses placenta), IgM (first produced in primary response, pentamer), IgA (secretory, mucous membranes, breast milk), IgE (allergies, parasites), IgD (B cell receptor).

#### T Cells and MHC
T cells: mature in **thymus**; express **T cell receptors (TCRs)**. Two major types: **CD4+ helper T cells** (recognize antigen on **MHC II** — expressed on antigen-presenting cells: dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells) → secrete cytokines to activate B cells and cytotoxic T cells. **CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (CTLs)** (recognize antigen on **MHC I** — expressed on all nucleated cells) → kill infected cells by releasing perforin (pores in membrane) and granzymes (activate apoptosis). NK cells target cells with **missing MHC I** (viruses downregulate MHC I to evade CTLs → NK cells detect absence).

#### Immunological Memory
Primary exposure → clonal selection → expansion → most effector cells die → small pool of **memory cells** survives long-term. **Secondary response**: memory cells activated rapidly → faster (1–3 days vs 1–2 weeks), stronger, and more prolonged antibody production; lower antigen dose needed. This is the basis of vaccination — exposure to antigen (attenuated pathogen, protein subunit, mRNA → antigen) generates memory without disease.

### Vaccines and Immune Evasion
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** vaccines-immune-evasion
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** vaccine types · herd immunity · antigenic variation · HIV · autoimmunity
**Summary:** Vaccines exploit immunological memory; pathogens evolve mechanisms to evade immune detection; autoimmunity results from failure of self-tolerance.

#### Vaccine Types
**Live attenuated**: weakened pathogen (MMR, varicella, yellow fever) — strong immune response, long-lasting, risk in immunocompromised. **Inactivated (killed)**: pathogen killed (influenza injection, polio Salk) — safer, weaker response, needs boosters. **Subunit**: specific proteins (HepB, HPV) — very safe, weaker response. **Toxoid**: inactivated toxin (tetanus, diphtheria) — generates anti-toxin antibodies. **mRNA vaccines** (COVID-19 mRNA vaccines): mRNA encoding antigen → host cells produce antigen → immune response; no DNA, no live pathogen; mRNA degrades quickly.

#### Herd Immunity
When enough of a population is immune (via infection or vaccination), pathogen transmission chains are broken even among unvaccinated individuals. Threshold depends on R0 (basic reproduction number): measles (R0 ~15) requires ~93–95% immunity; COVID-19 original strain (R0 ~2–3) requires ~50–67%. Herd immunity protects immunocompromised individuals who cannot be vaccinated.

#### Immune Evasion and Autoimmunity
HIV infects **CD4+ helper T cells** (via CD4 receptor and CCR5/CXCR4 coreceptors) → destroys the very cells that coordinate adaptive immunity → AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome). Influenza undergoes **antigenic drift** (point mutations in hemagglutinin/neuraminidase → gradual change → annual vaccine reformulation) and **antigenic shift** (reassortment of RNA segments between strains → sudden major change → pandemic potential). **Autoimmunity**: self-reactive lymphocytes survive self-tolerance checkpoints (thymus negative selection for T cells) → attack self-tissues. Type 1 diabetes (beta cells), multiple sclerosis (myelin), rheumatoid arthritis (synovial joints), lupus (SLE, DNA/nuclear antigens).

#### Review Questions
1. Why must the influenza vaccine be reformulated each year while the measles vaccine provides decades of protection?
2. Explain why HIV infection eventually leads to susceptibility to opportunistic infections like Pneumocystis pneumonia.
3. What is the difference between antigenic drift and antigenic shift in influenza, and which is more likely to cause a pandemic? Why?

### Digestion: Organs and Processes
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** digestion-organs-processes
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** alimentary canal · enzyme · villi · peristalsis · absorption
**Summary:** Digestion involves mechanical and chemical breakdown of food; absorption of nutrients occurs primarily in the small intestine via villi and microvilli.

#### Overview of Digestion
**Mechanical digestion**: physical breakdown without changing chemical structure — chewing, churning in stomach, peristalsis. **Chemical digestion**: enzymatic hydrolysis of polymers into monomers — polysaccharides → monosaccharides, proteins → amino acids, lipids → fatty acids + glycerol. Alimentary canal: mouth → pharynx → esophagus → stomach → small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum) → large intestine (colon) → rectum → anus. Surface area maximized by: **circular folds** → **villi** (finger-like projections) → **microvilli** (brush border on each villus cell) → ~600× increase in area. Monosaccharides: absorbed by cotransport with Na+ (SGLT1) → capillaries → portal vein → liver. Amino acids: similar Na+ cotransport. Fatty acids and glycerol: absorbed into intestinal cells → reassembled into triglycerides → packaged with cholesterol and proteins into **chylomicrons** → enter **lacteals** (lymphatic vessels in villi) → lymph system → thoracic duct → blood.

### Liver, Pancreas, and Metabolic Processing
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** liver-pancreas-metabolic-processing
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** liver functions · glycogen · bile · insulin · gluconeogenesis
**Summary:** The liver is the central metabolic organ — processing absorbed nutrients, synthesizing proteins, detoxifying compounds, and producing bile; the pancreas provides digestive enzymes and hormones.

#### Liver Functions
The liver receives nutrient-rich blood from the small intestine via the **portal vein** and processes it before release into systemic circulation. Key functions: (1) **Nutrient processing**: converts fructose/galactose to glucose; stores glucose as glycogen; converts excess amino acids to glucose (gluconeogenesis) or urea (urea cycle); packages lipids into VLDL lipoproteins. ### The Menstrual Cycle
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** menstrual-cycle
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** follicular phase · luteal phase · LH surge · estrogen · progesterone · corpus luteum
**Summary:** The menstrual cycle integrates ovarian and uterine cycles via hormonal feedback, with FSH/LH from the pituitary driving follicle development and ovulation.

#### Ovarian Cycle (~28 days)
**Follicular phase (days 1–14)**: FSH (anterior pituitary) stimulates follicle development; granulosa cells secrete estrogen; rising estrogen has positive feedback on pituitary → **LH surge** (day 13–14) → ovulation (secondary oocyte released from dominant follicle). **Luteal phase (days 14–28)**: LH transforms ruptured follicle into **corpus luteum**; corpus luteum secretes progesterone (dominant) and estrogen; progesterone → negative feedback → suppresses FSH and LH. #### Uterine Cycle
**Menstruation (days 1–5)**: progesterone withdrawal → uterine lining shed. **Proliferative phase (days 6–14)**: estrogen → endometrium thickens and grows. **Secretory phase (days 14–28)**: progesterone (from corpus luteum) → endometrium becomes highly vascularized and secretory (ready to receive blastocyst). If implantation occurs: trophoblast secretes **hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin)** → maintains corpus luteum → progesterone continues → no menstruation → pregnancy.

#### Hormonal Contraception
Combined oral contraceptives (estrogen + progestin): maintain high levels of both → negative feedback → suppress FSH and LH → no follicle development → no LH surge → no ovulation. Progesterone-only (minipill): thickens cervical mucus + thins endometrium; less reliable ovulation suppression. Emergency contraception (Plan B — levonorgestrel): high-dose progestin → delays or inhibits ovulation; must be taken within 72 hours.

### Fertilization, Development, and Pregnancy
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** fertilization-development-pregnancy
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** fertilization · cleavage · blastocyst · implantation · gastrulation
**Summary:** Fertilization initiates development; early embryonic stages (cleavage, blastocyst, gastrulation) establish the body plan and extraembryonic membranes that support the pregnancy.

#### Fertilization and Early Development
Fertilization: sperm penetrates secondary oocyte in fallopian tube → acrosome reaction (hydrolytic enzymes digest zona pellucida) → sperm-egg fusion → **cortical reaction** (release of enzymes hardens zona pellucida, preventing polyspermy) → secondary oocyte completes meiosis II → pronuclei fuse → zygote (2n). **Cleavage**: rapid mitotic divisions; cells (blastomeres) get smaller; no growth; 8-cell stage (morula) → **blastocyst** (inner cell mass = embryo; trophoblast = placenta) → implants in endometrium (day 6–10). **hCG** secretion by trophoblast maintains corpus luteum → progesterone continues.

Neural tube defects (spina bifida, anencephaly) linked to folic acid deficiency.

#### Placenta and Extraembryonic Membranes
**Placenta**: fetal and maternal blood do not mix; nutrients, O2, and antibodies (IgG) pass from mother to fetus; CO2 and waste pass to mother. **Amnion**: fluid-filled sac cushions fetus (amniotic fluid). **Chorion** forms placenta with uterine wall. **Yolk sac** (nutrient in birds/reptiles; site of early blood cell formation in humans). **Allantois** (waste storage in birds/reptiles; forms umbilical blood vessels in humans).

### Reproductive System Bowl Mastery
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** reproductive-system-bowl-mastery

**Key concepts:** LH surge · corpus luteum · hCG · spermatogenesis · oogenesis
**Summary:** High-yield reproductive system facts and toss-up stems for Science Bowl.

#### Bowl Toss-up Patterns
"For 10 points, what structure, formed from the ruptured ovarian follicle after ovulation, produces progesterone during the luteal phase?" → **corpus luteum**

"For 10 points, what hormone, secreted by the trophoblast of the early embryo, maintains the corpus luteum during early pregnancy and is detected by home pregnancy tests?" → **hCG** (human chorionic gonadotropin)

"For 10 points, what hormone surge from the anterior pituitary triggers ovulation on approximately day 14 of the menstrual cycle?" → **LH surge** (luteinizing hormone surge)

"For 10 points, what are the cells in the testes that produce testosterone?" → **Leydig cells** (interstitial cells)

"For 10 points, what embryonic stage, consisting of an inner cell mass and trophoblast, implants into the endometrium?" → **blastocyst**

#### High-Yield Facts
- Sperm produced at **~34–35°C** (testes must be in scrotum)
- Secondary oocyte arrested in **metaphase II** until fertilization
- Oogenesis: 1 ovum + 2–3 polar bodies per meiosis
- Germ layers: ecto (skin/nervous), meso (muscle/bone), endo (gut/lungs)
- Folic acid prevents **neural tube defects**
