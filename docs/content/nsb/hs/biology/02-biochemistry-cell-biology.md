## Subtopic: Water & Biological Chemistry

### Water: The Molecule That Makes Life Possible
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** water-molecule-life-possible
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** hydrogen bonding · cohesion · specific heat · polarity
**Summary:** Understand why water's unique chemical properties make it indispensable to every living organism on Earth.

#### Polarity and Hydrogen Bonding
Water (H₂O) is a polar molecule: oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen, pulling shared electrons toward itself, creating a partial negative charge (δ−) on oxygen and partial positive charges (δ+) on each hydrogen. This polarity allows adjacent water molecules to form hydrogen bonds — weak electrostatic attractions between the δ+ hydrogen of one molecule and the δ− oxygen of another. Each water molecule can form up to four hydrogen bonds simultaneously, creating a dynamic, constantly reorganizing network that gives water most of its remarkable properties.

#### Cohesion, Adhesion, and Surface Tension
Cohesion is the attraction of water molecules to each other via hydrogen bonds. Adhesion is the attraction of water molecules to other polar or charged surfaces. Together, these explain capillary action: water can rise in narrow tubes against gravity, driven by adhesion to the tube walls and cohesion pulling the water column behind it. This is how plants transport water from roots to leaves through xylem. Surface tension — water's resistance to being broken at its surface — results from the net inward pull of hydrogen bonds on surface molecules, strong enough to support the weight of insects like water striders.

#### Specific Heat and Temperature Moderation
Specific heat capacity is the energy required to raise 1 gram of a substance by 1°C. Water has an exceptionally high specific heat (4.18 J/g·°C) because energy input goes into breaking hydrogen bonds rather than increasing molecular kinetic energy. This means large bodies of water resist temperature fluctuations, stabilizing climates near coastlines. Inside cells, water buffers against temperature spikes that would denature enzymes. Evaporative cooling exploits water's high heat of vaporization (2,260 J/g): when sweat evaporates, the energetic water molecules that leave carry substantial heat with them, cooling the body efficiently.

#### Water as a Solvent
Water is called the "universal solvent" because its polarity allows it to dissolve ionic compounds (by surrounding ions with partial charges) and polar molecules (by forming hydrogen bonds with them). Hydrophilic substances dissolve readily. Hydrophobic substances (nonpolar, like lipids) do not, and are excluded from water — they aggregate to minimize contact with water, driving processes like cell membrane formation and protein folding. This hydrophobic effect is not a "force" per se but an entropic phenomenon: water's hydrogen bond network is more ordered around nonpolar solutes, so clustering them releases that order, increasing overall entropy.

#### Ionization and pH
Water undergoes autoionization: 2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻. At 25°C, [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁷ M, defining neutral pH 7. pH = −log[H⁺]. An acid donates H⁺, lowering pH; a base accepts H⁺ (or donates OH⁻), raising pH. Small pH changes represent large concentration changes — pH 5 is 10× more acidic than pH 6. Biological systems maintain tight pH control because enzymes have narrow pH optima; even a 0.1 unit pH change can significantly alter enzyme conformation and activity.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain why water has a high specific heat capacity in terms of hydrogen bonding.
2. A plant cell is placed in a solution more concentrated than the cell's contents. Predict what happens to the cell and explain the molecular basis.
3. Why do oils and water not mix? Explain in terms of polarity and the hydrophobic effect.

### Acids, Bases, and Buffers in Biological Systems
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** acids-bases-buffers-biological
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** pH scale · buffer systems · carbonic acid buffer
**Summary:** Learn how living organisms maintain stable pH despite constant acid production from metabolism.

#### The pH Scale
pH ranges from 0 (most acidic) to 14 (most basic), with 7 as neutral. Each whole-number change represents a 10-fold concentration difference. Blood pH is maintained at 7.35–7.45 — a narrow range. Below 7.35 is acidosis; above 7.45 is alkalosis. Both are life-threatening because enzyme function, protein conformation, and membrane ion transport all depend on pH. The stomach operates at pH 1.5–3.5 (highly acidic, optimal for pepsin); the small intestine at pH 7–8 (optimal for pancreatic enzymes).

#### Buffer Systems
A buffer resists pH changes by neutralizing added acid or base. A buffer consists of a weak acid and its conjugate base in equilibrium. When H⁺ is added, the conjugate base reacts: A⁻ + H⁺ → HA (absorbs acid). When OH⁻ is added, the weak acid reacts: HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O (absorbs base). Buffers are most effective at pH values near their pKa (the pH at which [HA] = [A⁻]). Buffer capacity is greatest within ±1 pH unit of pKa.

#### The Bicarbonate Buffer System
The bicarbonate buffer (H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻) is the primary blood pH regulator. CO₂ produced by cellular respiration dissolves in blood plasma: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. Elevated CO₂ increases H⁺ (lowering pH); decreased CO₂ decreases H⁺ (raising pH). The respiratory system tightly controls blood CO₂ — hyperventilating blows off CO₂, raising pH (respiratory alkalosis); hypoventilating retains CO₂, lowering pH (respiratory acidosis). The kidneys regulate HCO₃⁻ reabsorption over longer timescales, providing a secondary compensation mechanism.

#### Phosphate and Protein Buffers
Inside cells, the phosphate buffer system (H₂PO₄⁻/HPO₄²⁻, pKa 6.86) is important because intracellular pH is ~7.2, within the buffer's effective range. Proteins themselves act as buffers through the ionizable R-groups of histidine (pKa ~6), lysine, aspartate, and glutamate. Hemoglobin buffers blood pH by accepting H⁺ released when O₂ is delivered to tissues (the Bohr effect). This coupling of O₂ delivery and pH buffering elegantly integrates respiratory gas exchange with acid-base homeostasis.

#### Review Questions
1. Why does hyperventilation cause blood pH to rise (alkalosis) rather than fall?
2. A buffer contains 0.1 M acetic acid (pKa 4.76) and 0.1 M sodium acetate. What is the pH of this buffer? At what pH is this buffer most effective?
3. Explain how hemoglobin acts as a buffer and why this function is linked to oxygen delivery.

### Carbon Chemistry: The Molecular Basis of Life
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** carbon-chemistry-molecular-basis-life
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** carbon's bonding capacity · functional groups · isomers
**Summary:** Understand why carbon is the backbone of biomolecules and how functional groups determine chemical behavior.

#### Carbon's Unique Bonding Properties
Carbon (C) has 4 valence electrons and forms 4 covalent bonds — to other carbons, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus. This allows carbon to form chains, branches, and rings of virtually unlimited length and complexity. Carbon-carbon bonds are strong (C-C = 347 kJ/mol; C=C = 614 kJ/mol) yet numerous enough to store large amounts of chemical energy. No other element approaches carbon's structural diversity: silicon, carbon's periodic table neighbor, forms rigid silica frameworks rather than flexible organic molecules.

#### Functional Groups
Functional groups are specific arrangements of atoms that confer consistent chemical properties regardless of the carbon skeleton they're attached to. Key groups: hydroxyl (-OH) makes compounds polar and reactive; carbonyl (C=O) as aldehyde (-CHO) or ketone (C=O in chain) affects reactivity and classification of sugars; carboxyl (-COOH) makes compounds acidic (ionizes to -COO⁻ + H⁺); amino (-NH₂) makes compounds basic (accepts H⁺ to form -NH₃⁺); phosphate (-OPO₃²⁻) allows energy transfer and ionization; sulfhydryl (-SH) forms disulfide bonds in proteins.

#### Isomers
Isomers are molecules with the same molecular formula but different arrangements. Structural isomers have different bonding arrangements (e.g., butane vs. isobutane, both C₄H₁₀). Stereoisomers have the same bonds but different spatial arrangements. Enantiomers are mirror images (like left and right hands) — biologically important because enzymes are stereospecific: L-amino acids are used in proteins, D-sugars in carbohydrates. Cis-trans isomers (geometric isomers) differ in arrangement around a double bond — cis-fatty acids are kinked (healthier); trans-fatty acids are straight (more like saturated fats, associated with cardiovascular disease).

#### Dehydration Synthesis and Hydrolysis
Monomers join into polymers via dehydration synthesis (condensation reactions): each new bond releases a water molecule. Hydrolysis breaks polymers back into monomers by adding water across the bond. These reactions are catalyzed by enzymes. Dehydration synthesis builds carbohydrates (glycosidic bonds), proteins (peptide bonds), nucleic acids (phosphodiester bonds), and triglycerides (ester bonds). Hydrolysis is how your digestive system breaks down food: amylase hydrolyzes starch, proteases hydrolyze proteins, lipases hydrolyze triglycerides.

#### Review Questions
1. Why is carbon uniquely suited to serve as the backbone of biological macromolecules? Compare briefly with silicon.
2. Identify the functional group in each: acetic acid (CH₃COOH), glycine (NH₂-CH₂-COOH), glucose (contains -OH groups and -CHO).
3. What is a dehydration synthesis reaction? Write the general form and name one biological polymer built this way.

### Biological Macromolecules: Carbohydrates and Lipids
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** macromolecules-carbohydrates-lipids
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** monosaccharides · polysaccharides · triglycerides · phospholipids
**Summary:** Master the structure, function, and key examples of carbohydrates and lipids as biological macromolecules.

#### Carbohydrates: Structure
Carbohydrates follow the formula (CH₂O)n. Monosaccharides are the monomers — glucose, fructose, galactose (hexoses, 6C) and ribose, deoxyribose (pentoses, 5C). Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) exists predominantly in ring form in aqueous solution. Disaccharides form by glycosidic bonds between two monosaccharides: sucrose (glucose + fructose), maltose (glucose + glucose), lactose (glucose + galactose). Polysaccharides are long chains: starch (α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic bonds, energy storage in plants), glycogen (more highly branched, energy storage in animals/liver/muscle), cellulose (β-1,4 glycosidic bonds, structural in plant cell walls — humans cannot digest due to lack of cellulase).

#### Carbohydrates: Function
Carbohydrates serve as: (1) primary energy source — glucose is the preferred fuel for cellular respiration; (2) structural materials — cellulose in plant walls, chitin (N-acetylglucosamine polymer) in fungal walls and insect exoskeletons; (3) cell recognition — glycoproteins and glycolipids on cell surfaces serve as identification tags (e.g., ABO blood type antigens are carbohydrate chains). The differences between starch and cellulose demonstrate how monomer orientation (α vs. β glucose) creates completely different macroscopic properties from the same monomer.

#### Lipids: Structure and Diversity
Lipids are defined not by polymer structure (they are not polymers) but by their hydrophobicity — insolubility in water. Major classes: triglycerides (one glycerol + three fatty acid chains joined by ester bonds), phospholipids (glycerol + two fatty acids + phosphate group + polar head), steroids (four fused carbon rings with functional groups), waxes (long-chain fatty acid + long-chain alcohol). Fatty acids can be saturated (no double bonds, straight, tightly packed, solid at room temperature — e.g., lard, butter) or unsaturated (one or more C=C double bonds, kinked, loosely packed, liquid at room temperature — e.g., olive oil). Polyunsaturated fats have multiple double bonds.

#### Phospholipids and the Lipid Bilayer
Phospholipids are amphipathic: the phosphate-containing head is polar and hydrophilic; the two fatty acid tails are nonpolar and hydrophobic. In aqueous solution, phospholipids spontaneously form bilayers: heads face out (toward water), tails face in (away from water). This is thermodynamically driven by the hydrophobic effect — the ordered hydrogen bond network around exposed nonpolar tails is released when tails cluster together, increasing entropy. The bilayer is self-healing and fluid at physiological temperature. Cholesterol inserts between phospholipids, reducing fluidity at high temperatures and preventing solidification at low temperatures — a "fluidity buffer."

#### Steroids and Waxes
Steroids share a four-ring carbon skeleton. Cholesterol is the precursor to all steroid hormones (testosterone, estrogen, cortisol, aldosterone) and bile salts. Despite its cardiovascular disease associations, cholesterol is essential for membrane fluidity and hormone synthesis. Waxes are highly hydrophobic (very long hydrocarbon chains) and serve as waterproofing: leaf cuticle (cutin), insect exoskeleton, earwax (cerumen), whale oil (spermaceti). Wax melts at higher temperatures than most lipids due to its more regular structure.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain why humans can digest starch but not cellulose, even though both are glucose polymers.
2. A phospholipid is described as amphipathic. Define amphipathic and explain why this property is essential for bilayer formation.
3. Compare saturated and unsaturated fatty acids in terms of molecular structure, physical state at room temperature, and health implications.

### Proteins: Structure and Function
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** proteins-structure-function-application
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** amino acid structure · four levels of protein structure · protein folding diseases
**Summary:** Apply your knowledge of protein structure to understand how amino acid sequence determines 3D shape and biological function.

#### Amino Acid Structure and Peptide Bonds
All 20 standard amino acids share a central α-carbon bonded to: an amino group (-NH₂), a carboxyl group (-COOH), a hydrogen, and a variable R-group (side chain) that determines identity and chemical properties. Amino acids are classified by R-group: nonpolar/hydrophobic (alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, proline, phenylalanine, tryptophan, methionine), polar/uncharged (serine, threonine, cysteine, tyrosine, asparagine, glutamine), charged/basic (lysine, arginine, histidine), and charged/acidic (aspartate, glutamate). Peptide bonds form between the carboxyl of one amino acid and the amino of the next via dehydration synthesis, creating the peptide backbone (N-terminus → C-terminus).

#### Four Levels of Protein Structure
Primary structure: the linear amino acid sequence, determined by DNA. All higher structure derives from this sequence. Secondary structure: local regular folding patterns stabilized by hydrogen bonds between backbone atoms (N-H and C=O). α-helices are coiled; β-pleated sheets are extended strands connected by hydrogen bonds laterally. Tertiary structure: the overall 3D shape of a polypeptide, stabilized by interactions among R-groups: hydrophobic interactions (nonpolar R-groups cluster in the interior away from water), disulfide bonds (covalent bonds between cysteines), ionic bonds (between oppositely charged R-groups), and hydrogen bonds. Quaternary structure: assembly of two or more polypeptide subunits (e.g., hemoglobin: 2α + 2β subunits).

#### Structure Determines Function
Hemoglobin demonstrates structure-function relationships beautifully. Its quaternary structure allows cooperative O₂ binding: binding of one O₂ causes conformational changes that increase affinity for subsequent O₂ molecules (sigmoidal binding curve). Sickle cell disease results from a single amino acid substitution (glutamic acid → valine at position 6 of β-globin) — this one change creates a hydrophobic patch that causes deoxygenated hemoglobin to polymerize into rigid fibers, deforming red blood cells. One amino acid = one disease = millions affected. This illustrates how critical primary structure is.

#### Protein Denaturation
Denaturation is the disruption of secondary, tertiary, and/or quaternary structure without breaking peptide bonds. Causes: heat (disrupts non-covalent interactions by increasing kinetic energy), extreme pH (alters ionization of R-groups, disrupts ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds), organic solvents (disrupt hydrophobic interactions), heavy metals (bind sulfhydryl groups, preventing or disrupting disulfide bonds). Denaturation is usually irreversible in vivo. The classic demonstration: a fried egg — heat denatures albumin, turning it from clear (soluble) to white (insoluble aggregates). Prions are misfolded proteins that catalyze the misfolding of normal proteins, causing neurodegenerative diseases (BSE, CJD) — denaturation with catastrophic consequences.

#### Review Questions
1. A mutation changes a hydrophobic leucine to a charged lysine in the interior of a globular protein. Predict what happens to protein stability and function.
2. Explain why an α-helix is a type of secondary structure and what type of chemical interaction stabilizes it.
3. Compare denaturation by heat vs. extreme pH at the molecular level. Which types of bonds/interactions does each disrupt?

### Nucleic Acids: DNA and RNA
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** nucleic-acids-dna-rna-application
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** nucleotide structure · DNA double helix · RNA types
**Summary:** Apply nucleic acid structure knowledge to understand information storage, transfer, and the central dogma.

#### Nucleotide Structure and Polymer Assembly
Nucleotides are the monomers of nucleic acids, each consisting of: a pentose sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), a nitrogenous base, and one to three phosphate groups. Nitrogenous bases: purines (two rings) = adenine and guanine; pyrimidines (one ring) = cytosine, thymine (DNA only), and uracil (RNA only). Nucleotides join via phosphodiester bonds between the 3' hydroxyl of one sugar and the 5' phosphate of the next, creating a backbone with directionality: synthesis always proceeds 5' → 3'. The sequence of bases encodes genetic information.

#### DNA Double Helix
DNA consists of two antiparallel strands wound into a right-handed helix. The sugar-phosphate backbone is on the outside; the bases face inward and pair via hydrogen bonds: A-T (2 hydrogen bonds), G-C (3 hydrogen bonds). G≡C pairs are stronger and higher G+C content raises the melting temperature (Tm) of the duplex — relevant for PCR primer design. Base pairing rules (Chargaff's rules): [A]=[T] and [G]=[C] in any double-stranded DNA. The major groove (wider) and minor groove (narrower) are where DNA-binding proteins make specific contact with base sequences. The helix has a pitch of 3.4 nm per 10 base pairs, with each base pair separated by 0.34 nm.

#### RNA Structure and Types
RNA is single-stranded and uses ribose and uracil. Three major types: messenger RNA (mRNA) carries protein-coding sequence from DNA in the nucleus to ribosomes in the cytoplasm; ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is the catalytic and structural component of ribosomes (23S/16S in prokaryotes, 28S/18S in eukaryotes) — the ribosome is fundamentally an RNA enzyme (ribozyme); transfer RNA (tRNA) carries amino acids to the ribosome during translation, with an anticodon loop that pairs with mRNA codons. RNA's single-stranded nature allows it to fold into complex secondary and tertiary structures via intramolecular base pairing (hairpin loops, stem-loops), enabling its catalytic functions.

#### ATP: The Energy Currency
ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a nucleotide with special energy-storage function. The two phosphoanhydride bonds (connecting the three phosphates) are high-energy bonds releasing ~30.5 kJ/mol when hydrolyzed. ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ + energy. This energy drives endergonic reactions, active transport, muscle contraction, and cell signaling. ATP is not a long-term storage molecule (cells turn over their entire ATP supply every minute during exercise); it is a momentary energy currency coupling catabolic (energy-releasing) to anabolic (energy-requiring) reactions.

#### Review Questions
1. Why does a DNA sequence with 70% G+C content have a higher melting temperature than one with 30% G+C?
2. Describe the differences between mRNA, rRNA, and tRNA in terms of structure and function.
3. Why is ATP described as an "energy currency" rather than an "energy store"? What makes it suitable for this role?

## Subtopic: Cell Structure & Organelle Functions

### Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic Cells
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** prokaryotic-vs-eukaryotic-cells
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** cell theory · membrane-bound organelles · endosymbiotic theory
**Summary:** Understand the fundamental differences between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells and the evolutionary origin of organelles.

#### Cell Theory
Cell theory has three tenets: (1) all living organisms are composed of cells; (2) the cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life; (3) all cells arise from pre-existing cells (Virchow's contribution, disproving spontaneous generation for cells). Extensions: all cells share similar chemical composition; all metabolic reactions occur within cells; genetic information is passed from cell to cell during division.

#### Prokaryotes
Prokaryotic cells lack a membrane-bound nucleus — DNA is in the nucleoid region, an area of the cytoplasm not bounded by membrane. They also lack membrane-bound organelles. Features: plasma membrane, cell wall (peptidoglycan in bacteria, pseudopeptidoglycan or other materials in archaea), ribosomes (70S = 30S + 50S subunits), flagella (different structure from eukaryotic), pili (for attachment and conjugation), plasmids (small circular extra-chromosomal DNA). Average size: 1–10 µm. Two domains: Bacteria and Archaea (archaea have distinct membrane lipids with ether linkages instead of ester linkages, and some thrive in extreme environments — extremophiles).

#### Eukaryotes
Eukaryotic cells contain a membrane-bound nucleus and extensive membrane systems. Average size: 10–100 µm. Key organelles: nucleus (houses DNA, site of transcription); endoplasmic reticulum — rough (studded with ribosomes, synthesizes membrane and secreted proteins) and smooth (lipid synthesis, drug detoxification, Ca²⁺ storage); Golgi apparatus (sorts, modifies, and packages proteins); mitochondria (ATP synthesis via oxidative phosphorylation); lysosomes (hydrolytic enzyme-filled vesicles for intracellular digestion); vacuoles (large storage in plants — central vacuole maintains turgor pressure); chloroplasts (photosynthesis in plant cells); cytoskeleton (microtubules, actin filaments, intermediate filaments).

#### Endosymbiotic Theory
Lynn Margulis proposed that mitochondria and chloroplasts originated as free-living prokaryotes engulfed by a host cell in a symbiotic relationship. Evidence: (1) mitochondria and chloroplasts are bounded by two membranes (inner = prokaryotic membrane; outer = phagocytic vesicle membrane); (2) they contain their own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes (like prokaryotes, not 80S like eukaryotes); (3) they reproduce by binary fission; (4) their genome sequences are most similar to specific bacterial groups (mitochondria to α-proteobacteria; chloroplasts to cyanobacteria); (5) they can be inhibited by antibiotics that target prokaryotic ribosomes (like chloramphenicol).

#### Review Questions
1. List three pieces of evidence that support the endosymbiotic theory for the origin of mitochondria.
2. Compare the ribosomes of prokaryotes and eukaryotes in terms of size (Svedberg units) and location.
3. Why do plant cells contain both mitochondria and chloroplasts, while animal cells contain only mitochondria?

### Endomembrane System and Protein Trafficking
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** endomembrane-protein-trafficking
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** rough ER · Golgi apparatus · vesicle trafficking · signal peptides
**Summary:** Trace the path of a secreted protein from ribosome synthesis to exocytosis, through the endomembrane system.

#### The Secretory Pathway
Proteins destined for the endomembrane system, secretion, or plasma membrane insertion are synthesized on ribosomes associated with the rough endoplasmic reticulum (rER). A signal peptide at the N-terminus directs the ribosome to the rER membrane; the signal peptide is cleaved during translation. The growing polypeptide threads into the rER lumen, where chaperone proteins assist folding and disulfide bonds form. N-linked glycosylation (addition of oligosaccharide chains to asparagine) begins in the rER. Misfolded proteins are retained and eventually degraded via ER-associated degradation (ERAD).

#### Golgi Apparatus: Processing and Sorting
Vesicles bud off the rER and fuse with the cis face of the Golgi apparatus (the entry face). Proteins move through the Golgi's stacked cisternae (cis → medial → trans) via vesicular transport. Within the Golgi: N-linked glycans are trimmed and modified; O-linked glycans are added; proteins are phosphorylated or sulfated; sorting signals are added. At the trans-Golgi network (TGN), proteins are sorted into vesicles destined for the plasma membrane (constitutive secretion), secretory vesicles (regulated secretion, e.g., neurotransmitters), or lysosomes (tagged with mannose-6-phosphate). The Golgi is the cell's postal distribution center.

#### Lysosomes and Autophagy
Lysosomes contain ~50 hydrolytic enzymes (lipases, proteases, nucleases, glycosidases) active at pH 5. The lysosome membrane maintains this acidic interior using a proton pump (V-type ATPase). Lysosomes fuse with endosomes (from endocytosis) or phagosomes (from phagocytosis) to digest macromolecules and pathogens. Autophagy ("self-eating") is the sequestration of damaged organelles or proteins in autophagosomes, which fuse with lysosomes for degradation and recycling. Lysosomal storage diseases (e.g., Tay-Sachs) result from deficiency of specific lysosomal enzymes, causing accumulation of undigested substrates.

#### Vesicle Budding and Fusion
Vesicular transport is mediated by coat proteins. COPI coats retrieve escaped proteins from Golgi back to rER (retrograde). COPII coats move cargo from ER to Golgi (anterograde). Clathrin coats mediate endocytosis at the plasma membrane. Vesicle fusion requires SNARE proteins: v-SNAREs on the vesicle pair with t-SNAREs on the target membrane, providing specificity and driving membrane fusion. Rab GTPases serve as molecular "addresses" — each organelle has characteristic Rab proteins that recruit docking factors.

#### Review Questions
1. Trace the path of an antibody (a secreted protein) from its synthesis on a ribosome to its exocytosis from a B cell. Name each organelle or compartment the protein passes through.
2. What is the role of mannose-6-phosphate in protein sorting within the Golgi?
3. How do SNARE proteins ensure that vesicles fuse with the correct target membrane?

### The Cytoskeleton: Structure, Dynamics, and Function
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** cytoskeleton-structure-dynamics-function
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** microtubules · actin filaments · intermediate filaments · motor proteins
**Summary:** Understand the three types of cytoskeletal filaments, their assembly dynamics, and their roles in cell shape, movement, and division.

#### Three Cytoskeletal Networks
The cytoskeleton is a dynamic network of protein filaments that gives cells shape, enables movement, and organizes intracellular space. Three main types: (1) Microtubules (MTs): hollow tubes of α/β-tubulin heterodimers, 25 nm diameter, most rigid, longest-lived, function in chromosome segregation, organelle positioning, and flagellar/ciliary movement; (2) Actin filaments (microfilaments): actin monomers (G-actin) polymerize into double-stranded helical filaments (F-actin), 7 nm diameter, most flexible, function in cell shape, crawling, cytokinesis, and muscle contraction; (3) Intermediate filaments: diverse proteins (keratin, vimentin, desmin, lamins, neurofilaments), 8–12 nm diameter, most stable, provide mechanical strength and anchor organelles.

#### Microtubule Dynamics
Microtubules exhibit dynamic instability: they alternate between phases of polymerization (growth) and depolymerization (catastrophe). GTP-tubulin polymerizes at the plus end (+); if GTP is hydrolyzed to GDP before the next dimer is added, the MT catastrophically depolymerizes. This dynamic behavior allows rapid reorganization of the MT network during mitosis. The mitotic spindle forms from MTs nucleated at centrosomes (MTOC). Kinetochore MTs attach to chromosomes at kinetochores; interpolar MTs overlap and push spindle poles apart; astral MTs anchor the spindle to the cell cortex. Drugs targeting MTs are major cancer chemotherapeutics: taxol stabilizes MTs (prevents depolymerization), vinblastine/vincristine prevent polymerization — both arrest cells in mitosis.

#### Actin Dynamics and Cell Motility
Actin polymerizes preferentially at its plus ("barbed") end and depolymerizes at its minus ("pointed") end — a process called treadmilling. ARP2/3 complex nucleates new actin branches at 70° angles, creating the dendritic network that pushes the leading edge of migrating cells forward (lamellipodia and filopodia). Formins nucleate straight actin cables. In muscle, actin and myosin II interact in a sliding filament mechanism: myosin heads (using ATP hydrolysis) walk toward the plus end of actin, generating force that slides actin past myosin, shortening the sarcomere. The same principle drives cytokinesis, where an actin-myosin ring constricts to pinch cells apart.

#### Motor Proteins
Kinesin walks toward microtubule plus ends (typically toward cell periphery, away from nucleus), transporting vesicles, organelles, and chromosomes. Dynein walks toward MT minus ends (toward nucleus/centrosome), powering retrograde transport and flagellar/ciliary beating. Myosin walks toward actin plus ends (varied directionality by class). All motor proteins use ATP hydrolysis to generate conformational changes that translate into directional stepping. Mutations in dynein cause primary ciliary dyskinesia (Kartagener syndrome), where immotile cilia lead to chronic respiratory infections, infertility (immotile sperm), and situs inversus (reversed organ positioning).

### Cell Organelles in Action: Case Studies
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** cell-organelles-case-studies
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** organelle dysfunction · disease connections · experimental tools
**Summary:** Apply knowledge of organelle function to explain disease mechanisms and understand how researchers study organelles.

#### Mitochondrial Disease
Mitochondria produce ~90% of a cell's ATP via oxidative phosphorylation. Mitochondrial diseases can result from mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) or nuclear DNA encoding mitochondrial proteins. mtDNA is maternally inherited (from the egg cell's mitochondria); sperm mitochondria are typically degraded. mtDNA has 37 genes (13 encode oxidative phosphorylation subunits, 22 tRNAs, 2 rRNAs). MELAS syndrome (mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, stroke-like episodes) results from mtDNA mutation. Because multiple mtDNA copies exist per cell (heteroplasmy), disease severity correlates with the proportion of mutant copies.

#### Lysosomal Storage Diseases
Tay-Sachs disease: lack of hexosaminidase A enzyme leads to accumulation of GM₂ ganglioside in lysosomes, particularly in neurons. Progressive neurological deterioration begins at ~6 months, with death typically by age 4. It is more prevalent in Ashkenazi Jewish populations due to a founder effect. Gaucher disease: deficiency of glucocerebrosidase leads to glucocerebroside accumulation in macrophages; milder forms are treatable with enzyme replacement therapy (imiglucerase) — a landmark in recombinant protein therapeutics. I-cell disease: failure to add mannose-6-phosphate to lysosomal enzymes means they are secreted outside the cell instead of being delivered to lysosomes, demonstrating the sorting signal's critical importance.

#### The Nucleus and Nuclear Pore Complex
The nucleus is bounded by a double membrane (nuclear envelope) perforated by nuclear pore complexes (NPCs), each containing ~1000 proteins (nucleoporins). NPCs mediate transport of: proteins into the nucleus (import, using nuclear localization signals, NLS), RNA out of the nucleus (export, using nuclear export signals, NES). The NPC is gated — small molecules (<40 kDa) diffuse passively; larger molecules require active transport coupled to Ran GTPase. The nuclear lamina (lamin intermediate filaments) underlies the inner nuclear membrane, providing structural support and anchoring chromatin. Mutations in lamins cause laminopathies including Hutchinson-Gilford progeria (accelerated aging syndrome).

#### Tools for Studying Organelles
Differential centrifugation separates organelles by size and density: low speed pellets nuclei and cell debris; 10,000×g pellets mitochondria, chloroplasts, and lysosomes; 100,000×g pellets ribosomes and vesicles (microsomal fraction). Fluorescent proteins (GFP, RFP) fused to organelle-targeting sequences enable real-time visualization. Electron microscopy reveals ultrastructure. CRISPR-Cas9 can knock out organelle-specific genes to study function. Organoid technology allows 3D culturing of tissue-specific stem cells into mini-organs with relevant organelle complexity (e.g., intestinal organoids reveal lysosomal biology in enterocytes).

2. Explain the molecular defect in I-cell disease and predict the consequences for lysosomal function.

## Subtopic: Membrane Structure & Transport

### The Fluid Mosaic Model
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** fluid-mosaic-model
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** Singer-Nicolson model · membrane fluidity · lipid rafts
**Summary:** Understand the structure of the plasma membrane and how its fluid, mosaic nature enables diverse cell functions.

#### The Fluid Mosaic Model (1972)
Singer and Nicolson proposed the fluid mosaic model: the plasma membrane is a fluid phospholipid bilayer in which proteins float and move laterally ("mosaic" of proteins in a "fluid" lipid sea). The bilayer is ~7–8 nm thick. Phospholipids diffuse laterally (10⁷ times/second) but rarely flip-flop between leaflets (transverse diffusion, <once per month without enzymes). FRAP (fluorescence recovery after photobleaching) demonstrates membrane fluidity: bleach a patch of fluorescent lipids → fluorescence recovers as unbleached lipids diffuse in.

#### Membrane Proteins
Integral (transmembrane) proteins span the bilayer — they contain hydrophobic α-helical segments that interact with the lipid interior. Examples: ion channels, transporters, receptor tyrosine kinases, G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). Peripheral proteins are associated with membrane surfaces via electrostatic or hydrogen bond interactions, easily removed by high salt or pH changes. Lipid-anchored proteins are covalently attached to lipid molecules embedded in the bilayer. The protein-to-lipid ratio varies by membrane: the mitochondrial inner membrane is ~76% protein (very active) while myelin sheaths are ~18% protein (insulating, metabolically quiet).

#### Membrane Fluidity Factors
Fluidity depends on: (1) Temperature — higher temperature = more fluid; (2) Fatty acid saturation — unsaturated fatty acids (kinked tails, don't pack tightly) increase fluidity; (3) Cholesterol — at high temperatures, cholesterol restricts phospholipid movement (reduces fluidity); at low temperatures, it prevents tight packing (prevents solidification). This dual role makes cholesterol a "fluidity buffer." Poikilotherms (cold-blooded animals) adjust their membrane fatty acid composition seasonally — increasing unsaturated fats in winter to maintain membrane fluidity in the cold.

#### Lipid Rafts and Membrane Asymmetry
Lipid rafts are cholesterol and sphingolipid-enriched microdomains that are more ordered (less fluid) than surrounding membrane. They serve as platforms for signaling complexes, organizing signaling molecules spatially. Membrane asymmetry: the two leaflets have different lipid compositions. Phosphatidylserine (negative charge) is normally restricted to the inner leaflet — when it flips to the outer leaflet, it signals apoptosis and triggers platelet aggregation. Glycolipids and glycoproteins face only the extracellular space, contributing to the glycocalyx — a carbohydrate-rich layer involved in cell recognition and protection.

### Membrane Transport: Passive and Active
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** membrane-transport-passive-active
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** simple diffusion · facilitated diffusion · active transport · Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase
**Summary:** Master the mechanisms, energetics, and examples of all major membrane transport processes.

#### Selective Permeability
The plasma membrane is selectively permeable — it allows some substances to cross freely while restricting others. Small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, N₂, fatty acids) diffuse freely across the lipid bilayer. Small polar molecules (H₂O, urea) cross slowly. Large polar molecules (glucose, amino acids) and ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺) cannot cross the lipid bilayer without assistance. This selectivity is essential for maintaining concentration gradients that drive cellular processes.

#### Passive Transport
Passive transport requires no energy (no ATP) — it moves substances down their electrochemical gradient (from high to low concentration for neutral solutes; for ions, the gradient combines concentration and electrical potential). Simple diffusion: nonpolar molecules directly diffuse through the bilayer. Facilitated diffusion uses membrane proteins: channel proteins (ion channels) form pores — gated (open in response to voltage, ligand, or mechanical stimulus) or ungated (always open, like aquaporins for H₂O). Carrier proteins (transporters) bind specific solutes, undergo conformational change, and release them on the other side — slower than channels. Aquaporins are water channels that increase water permeability ~100×; they explain why some cells are highly water-permeable (red blood cells, renal tubules) while others are not.

#### Active Transport
Active transport moves substances against their electrochemical gradient, requiring energy (usually ATP hydrolysis). Primary active transport directly couples ATP hydrolysis to transport. The Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase (sodium-potassium pump) exports 3 Na⁺ out and imports 2 K⁺ in per ATP hydrolyzed, maintaining low intracellular Na⁺ and high intracellular K⁺. This gradient drives the resting membrane potential (~−70 mV) and is used for secondary active transport. The Ca²⁺ ATPase pumps Ca²⁺ into the ER lumen or out of the cell, maintaining very low cytoplasmic [Ca²⁺] (~100 nM at rest vs. ~1 mM outside). Secondary active transport couples the movement of one ion (usually Na⁺) down its gradient to drive another solute against its gradient (cotransport/symport: both in same direction; antiport: opposite directions).

#### Bulk Transport
Endocytosis and exocytosis move large particles or volumes in membrane-bounded vesicles. Phagocytosis ("cell eating"): pseudopods engulf large particles (bacteria, dead cells) → phagosome → fuses with lysosome for digestion. Pinocytosis ("cell drinking"): invagination of the membrane creates small vesicles of extracellular fluid. Receptor-mediated endocytosis: clathrin-coated pits bind specific extracellular ligands via surface receptors → internalize the receptor-ligand complex → early endosome → late endosome → lysosomal degradation or receptor recycling. LDL (cholesterol) is taken up by receptor-mediated endocytosis; defects cause familial hypercholesterolemia.

### Osmosis, Tonicity, and Water Potential
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** osmosis-tonicity-water-potential
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** osmotic pressure · tonicity · water potential · turgor pressure
**Summary:** Master osmosis and tonicity, then apply water potential concepts to plant cell behavior.

#### Osmosis
Osmosis is the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane from a region of lower solute concentration (higher water concentration) to a region of higher solute concentration (lower water concentration). Water moves to equalize water potential across the membrane. Osmotic pressure is the pressure that would need to be applied to prevent osmotic flow — it is proportional to solute concentration (Van't Hoff equation: π = iMRT, where i = Van't Hoff factor, M = molarity, R = gas constant, T = temperature in Kelvin).

#### Tonicity
Tonicity describes the effect of a solution on cell volume. Isotonic: same solute concentration as the cell → no net water movement → cell volume unchanged. Hypotonic: lower solute concentration than cell → water enters → cell swells (animal cells may lyse/undergo cytolysis; plant cells become turgid against cell wall). Hypertonic: higher solute concentration than cell → water leaves → cell shrinks (animal cells undergo crenation; plant cells undergo plasmolysis — membrane pulls away from wall). The specific terms crenation and plasmolysis are tested in Science Bowl.

#### Water Potential in Plants
Water potential (Ψ) = osmotic potential (Ψₛ) + pressure potential (Ψₚ). Ψₛ is always negative (solutes lower water potential). Ψₚ (turgor pressure) is positive in turgid plant cells (wall pushes back on water). Water moves from high Ψ to low Ψ. In a turgid plant cell in water: Ψₛ (negative) + Ψₚ (positive) = Ψ (may be ~0 at full turgor). Wilting occurs when Ψₚ drops to 0 (incipient plasmolysis). Water potential drives flow through the SPAC (soil-plant-atmosphere continuum): soil Ψ is high, leaf Ψ is very negative (due to low Ψₛ and transpiration pulling water up), so water flows from soil → roots → stems → leaves → air.

### Transport Across Membranes: Applied Cases
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** membrane-transport-applied-cases
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** glucose transport · kidney tubule reabsorption · drug delivery
**Summary:** Apply membrane transport mechanisms to physiological contexts including intestinal absorption, kidney function, and pharmacology.

#### Glucose Transport in Enterocytes
Glucose absorption from the intestinal lumen into enterocytes uses secondary active transport (SGLT1, sodium-glucose linked transporter 1): the Na⁺ gradient (maintained by Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase on the basolateral side) drives glucose into the cell against its gradient. Inside the enterocyte, glucose exits via facilitated diffusion through GLUT2 on the basolateral side into the bloodstream. This is an example of transcellular transport and secondary active transport. SGLT2 inhibitors (gliflozins) prevent glucose reabsorption in the kidney tubule, used as type 2 diabetes drugs — a pharmacological application of transport knowledge.

#### Kidney Tubule: Reabsorption and Secretion
The nephron relies on selective membrane transport to reclaim ~99% of filtered water and solutes. In the proximal tubule: glucose, amino acids, Na⁺, HCO₃⁻, and water are reabsorbed. Na⁺/glucose cotransport (SGLT1/2) uses the Na⁺ gradient to reclaim glucose. The thick ascending limb of the Loop of Henle has NKCC2 (Na-K-2Cl cotransporter) — the target of loop diuretics like furosemide. Aquaporin 2 (AQP2) in the collecting duct controls water reabsorption in response to ADH (antidiuretic hormone, vasopressin) — ADH inserts AQP2 into the apical membrane, increasing water permeability and conserving water.

#### Ion Channels in Nerve Function
Action potentials depend on voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels. At rest, Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase maintains low intracellular Na⁺ and high intracellular K⁺; K⁺ leak channels allow K⁺ to leave, creating the resting potential (~−70 mV). Depolarization triggers voltage-gated Na⁺ channels to open → Na⁺ flows in → potential rises to +30 mV. Then Na⁺ channels inactivate (absolute refractory period) and K⁺ channels open → K⁺ flows out → repolarization. Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase restores the gradient. Local anesthetics (lidocaine) block voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, preventing action potential propagation. Tetrodotoxin (TTX, puffer fish toxin) is a specific Na⁺ channel blocker used in research.
