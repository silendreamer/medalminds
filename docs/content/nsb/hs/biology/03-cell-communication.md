## Subtopic: Cell Signaling & Receptors

### Types of Cell Signaling: An Overview
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** types-cell-signaling-overview
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** autocrine · paracrine · endocrine · juxtacrine
**Summary:** Cells communicate through four major signaling modes that differ in distance, speed, and mechanism.

#### Signaling Distance Defines Communication Mode
Cells don't act in isolation — they constantly send and receive chemical messages. The four major signaling modes are classified by the distance between sender and receiver. In **autocrine** signaling, a cell secretes a molecule that binds its own receptors (common in immune cells and cancer). **Paracrine** signaling targets nearby cells via local diffusion (e.g., neurotransmitters crossing synapses). **Endocrine** signaling uses the bloodstream to carry hormones over long distances to distant target organs. **Juxtacrine** (contact-dependent) signaling requires direct cell-to-cell contact via membrane-bound ligands — critical in embryonic development.

#### Ligands and Receptors
Every signal requires a ligand (the signaling molecule) and a complementary receptor. The receptor's shape determines which cells can respond — a cell must express the correct receptor to "hear" a signal. Ligands include proteins, peptides, steroid hormones, gases (like nitric oxide), and small organic molecules. Binding is typically reversible and highly specific — a lock-and-key fit.

#### Signal Relay and Amplification
One of the most powerful features of cell signaling is amplification. A single ligand binding one receptor can activate dozens of intracellular proteins, which each activate hundreds more downstream. This cascade means that a tiny hormonal signal in the blood can trigger massive cellular responses without needing a large concentration of the original hormone.

#### Cell Surface vs. Intracellular Receptors
Hydrophilic ligands (proteins, peptides) cannot cross the plasma membrane — they bind cell-surface receptors. Hydrophobic ligands (steroid hormones like estrogen and testosterone, thyroid hormones) diffuse through the membrane and bind intracellular receptors that directly regulate gene transcription. This distinction is critical: steroids act slowly but persistently (changing gene expression), while peptide hormones act fast via surface receptors.

#### Clinical Connection: Receptor Mutations in Disease
When signaling goes wrong, disease follows. Overactive growth factor receptors (like HER2 in breast cancer) drive uncontrolled proliferation. Defective insulin receptors cause type 2 diabetes. Understanding receptor biology is the foundation of modern drug development — most targeted cancer therapies work by blocking specific receptors or their downstream signals.

#### Review Questions
1. A hydrophobic hormone like cortisol reaches a liver cell. Where does it bind its receptor, and what happens next?
2. Distinguish paracrine from endocrine signaling with one example each.
3. Why does signal amplification require so little ligand to produce a large cellular effect?

### G-Protein Coupled Receptors (GPCRs)
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** gpcrs-mechanism
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** GPCR · G-protein (Gα, Gβ, Gγ) · adenylyl cyclase · cAMP · protein kinase A
**Summary:** GPCRs are the largest family of cell-surface receptors, coupling extracellular signals to intracellular G-proteins to amplify and transduce the signal.

#### GPCR Structure and Activation
G-protein coupled receptors are transmembrane proteins with seven hydrophobic helices that span the lipid bilayer. When a ligand binds the extracellular face, the receptor changes conformation, activating the associated heterotrimeric G-protein on the cytoplasmic face. The G-protein has three subunits: Gα, Gβ, and Gγ. In the resting state, Gα is bound to GDP. Ligand binding causes Gα to swap GDP for GTP and dissociate from Gβγ.

#### The cAMP Second Messenger Pathway
Activated Gα (bound to GTP) diffuses along the membrane and activates **adenylyl cyclase**, which converts ATP into **cyclic AMP (cAMP)**. cAMP is a second messenger — it carries the signal deeper into the cell. cAMP binds and activates **protein kinase A (PKA)**, which phosphorylates serine and threonine residues on target proteins, changing their activity. PKA can activate enzymes, regulate ion channels, or enter the nucleus to phosphorylate transcription factors.

#### Signal Termination
The G-protein is self-inactivating: Gα has intrinsic GTPase activity, converting GTP back to GDP, which returns it to the inactive state. Phosphodiesterase degrades cAMP back to AMP, shutting off PKA. This built-in "off switch" prevents signals from running indefinitely.

#### Examples of GPCR Signaling
Epinephrine (adrenaline) binds β-adrenergic receptors (GPCRs) on heart muscle, raising cAMP and increasing heart rate. The same hormone binds α-adrenergic receptors (different GPCRs) on blood vessels, causing constriction. The diversity of GPCR types allows one hormone to have different effects in different tissues depending on which GPCR type is expressed.

#### Pharmacology of GPCRs
Over 30% of all FDA-approved drugs target GPCRs. Beta-blockers (like propranolol) block β-adrenergic GPCRs, slowing heart rate in hypertension. Opioid painkillers activate GPCR-coupled receptors in the nervous system. Antihistamines block histamine GPCRs. GPCRs are among the most druggable targets in medicine.

#### Review Questions
1. Trace the GPCR signaling pathway from epinephrine binding to PKA activation.
2. Why does Gα's GTPase activity matter for signal termination?
3. How can epinephrine produce opposite effects (heart acceleration vs. blood vessel constriction) in different tissues?

### Receptor Tyrosine Kinases (RTKs)
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** rtk-signaling
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** RTK · dimerization · autophosphorylation · RAS · MAP kinase cascade
**Summary:** Receptor tyrosine kinases transduce growth factor signals through dimerization and phosphorylation cascades that regulate cell growth and division.

#### RTK Structure and Activation
Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) are single-pass transmembrane proteins with an extracellular ligand-binding domain and an intracellular kinase domain. Unlike GPCRs, RTKs are monomers in the resting state. When a growth factor (like EGF or insulin) binds, it causes **dimerization** — two RTK monomers come together. Dimerization allows the kinase domains to **transphosphorylate** each other on tyrosine residues, activating the receptor.

#### Downstream Signaling: The RAS-MAP Kinase Pathway
Phosphorylated tyrosines on activated RTKs serve as docking sites for adaptor proteins like GRB2, which recruits SOS. SOS activates **RAS** (a small GTP-binding protein) by promoting GTP binding. Active RAS triggers a **MAP kinase cascade**: RAS → RAF → MEK → ERK. ERK (a MAP kinase) phosphorylates transcription factors in the nucleus, turning on genes for cell growth and proliferation.

#### RTKs vs. GPCRs: A Comparison
While GPCRs use cAMP as a second messenger and act transiently, RTKs use phosphotyrosine as a docking signal and activate longer-lasting transcriptional changes. GPCRs are ideal for rapid physiological responses (heart rate, smooth muscle tone); RTKs drive sustained decisions about growth and differentiation.

#### Oncogenic RAS Mutations
RAS is mutated in ~30% of all human cancers. A point mutation that destroys RAS's GTPase activity locks RAS in the GTP-bound (active) state permanently — the cell proliferates without needing a growth signal. This is why RAS is one of the most studied oncogenes. Understanding the RAS pathway led to the development of MEK and RAF inhibitors used in melanoma treatment.

#### Insulin Receptor: An RTK in Metabolism
The insulin receptor is an RTK that triggers glucose uptake. When insulin binds, the receptor autophosphorylates and activates PI3K → AKT signaling, which drives GLUT4 glucose transporters to the cell surface. In type 2 diabetes, downstream signaling is impaired despite normal insulin binding — this "insulin resistance" at the signaling level is why cells can't import glucose properly.

#### Review Questions
1. What structural change in RTKs occurs upon growth factor binding, and what does this enable?
2. Describe the RAS-MAP kinase cascade from active RAS to gene transcription.
3. Why does a RAS GTPase mutation cause uncontrolled cell growth?

### Ligand-Gated Ion Channels
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ligand-gated-ion-channels
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** ionotropic receptor · neurotransmitter · membrane potential · acetylcholine · nicotinic receptor
**Summary:** Ligand-gated ion channels convert chemical signals directly into electrical signals by opening ion-selective pores in the membrane.

#### Ionotropic vs. Metabotropic Receptors
Neurotransmitter receptors come in two major types. **Ionotropic receptors** (ligand-gated ion channels) are themselves ion channels — binding the ligand directly opens the pore, producing an electrical response within milliseconds. **Metabotropic receptors** (GPCRs) are coupled to G-proteins and produce slower, longer-lasting effects. At the neuromuscular junction, the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor is ionotropic: acetylcholine binding opens a cation channel, depolarizing the muscle cell and triggering contraction.

#### The Nicotinic Acetylcholine Receptor
The nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR) is a pentameric channel with five subunits arranged around a central pore. Each subunit spans the membrane. The pore is normally closed; when two acetylcholine molecules bind (one per α-subunit), a conformational change opens the pore to Na⁺ and K⁺. The net influx of Na⁺ depolarizes the membrane, initiating an action potential in muscle. The drug curare blocks nAChRs, causing paralysis — historically used on arrows in South America and now medically in surgery.

#### GABA-A Receptors and Inhibition
Not all ligand-gated channels are excitatory. GABA-A receptors are chloride (Cl⁻) channels. When GABA binds, Cl⁻ flows in, hyperpolarizing the membrane and making the neuron less likely to fire. Benzodiazepines (like Valium) and alcohol enhance GABA-A activity, producing sedation and anxiolysis. This is a direct pharmacological consequence of changing ion channel gating kinetics.

#### Speed Advantage of Ion Channels
The direct mechanical coupling between ligand binding and channel opening makes ionotropic receptors extraordinarily fast — responses occur in <1 millisecond. This speed is essential for rapid neural processing: hearing, reflexes, and vision all depend on fast ionotropic signaling. GPCRs, which use second messengers, are far slower (seconds to minutes) but produce more sustained and diverse effects.

#### Review Questions
1. Why does acetylcholine binding to nAChRs cause muscle depolarization?
2. Compare the speed and duration of signaling through ionotropic vs. metabotropic receptors.
3. How does benzodiazepine action on GABA-A receptors explain the sedative effect?

### Signal Integration: How Cells Make Decisions
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** signal-integration-cell-decisions
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** signal integration · synergy · antagonism · threshold · combinatorial control
**Summary:** Cells integrate multiple simultaneous signals to make all-or-nothing decisions about growth, survival, and differentiation.

#### Cells Receive Many Signals Simultaneously
At any moment, a cell receives dozens of signals through multiple receptor types. The cell's response is not simply additive — signals interact through crosstalk, competition, and convergence. Two signals that each produce a weak response can together produce a much stronger effect (**synergy**), while competing signals can cancel each other (**antagonism**).

#### Threshold Responses and Switch-Like Behavior
Many cellular decisions are switch-like: below a threshold signal strength, nothing happens; above it, the full response is triggered. This "all-or-nothing" behavior is often achieved by positive feedback loops. For example, MAP kinase cascades include feedback phosphorylation steps that amplify weak signals — once activated past a threshold, the full kinase cascade fires even if the original signal fades.

#### Combinatorial Control of Gene Expression
Signal integration is especially important in development. A muscle cell expresses different genes than a neuron, even though both contain the same genome. Different signals activate different combinations of transcription factors, and it is the specific combination — not any one factor — that specifies cell identity. This combinatorial logic allows a genome of ~20,000 genes to produce over 200 distinct cell types.

#### Review Questions
1. Define signal synergy and give a biological example.
2. Why do threshold responses produce more reliable cellular decisions than graded responses?
3. How can the same genome produce 200+ different cell types via combinatorial signaling?

### Hormones as Long-Range Signals
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** hormones-long-range-signals
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** steroid hormone · peptide hormone · receptor location · gene regulation · rapid vs. slow response
**Summary:** Steroid and peptide hormones use fundamentally different receptor locations and mechanisms to regulate target cells across the body.

#### Two Classes, Two Mechanisms
Steroid hormones (cortisol, estrogen, testosterone, aldosterone) are lipid-soluble: they cross membranes freely and bind nuclear receptors. The hormone-receptor complex acts as a transcription factor, directly binding DNA and altering gene expression. Effects are slow (hours) but persistent. Peptide hormones (insulin, glucagon, GH, ADH) are water-soluble: they bind cell-surface receptors (RTKs or GPCRs) and work through second messengers. Effects are rapid (seconds to minutes) but transient.

#### Cortisol and the Stress Response
Cortisol is a steroid secreted by the adrenal cortex during stress. It crosses cell membranes, binds the glucocorticoid receptor (GR), and the complex translocates to the nucleus. There, GR binds glucocorticoid response elements (GREs) in DNA, upregulating genes for gluconeogenesis and suppressing immune genes. This is why high-dose steroids are immunosuppressive — they literally turn off immune gene transcription.

#### Review Questions
1. Why can cortisol cross the plasma membrane but insulin cannot?
2. Compare the time course of cortisol action vs. epinephrine action in a stress response.
3. A student claims "hormones work by entering cells." When is this correct, and when is it wrong?

### Comparing Signal Transduction Pathways
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** comparing-signal-transduction
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** GPCR · RTK · ion channel · cAMP · phosphotyrosine · second messengers
**Summary:** A systematic comparison of the three major receptor types and their downstream pathways.

#### Side-by-Side Comparison
| Feature | GPCR | RTK | Ligand-gated channel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ligand type | Peptides, amines, odorants | Growth factors | Neurotransmitters |
| Second messenger | cAMP, IP₃, DAG | Phosphotyrosine | None (direct) |
| Speed | Seconds | Minutes–hours | Milliseconds |
| Key effector | PKA, PKC | RAS, PI3K | Ion flux |

#### Convergence on Shared Effectors
Different receptor types often converge on the same downstream effector. Both RTKs and GPCRs can activate PI3K, which produces PIP₃ and activates AKT. AKT promotes cell survival, growth, and glucose uptake. This convergence means that multiple extracellular signals can reinforce the same intracellular decision — a robust system for critical cellular choices.

## Subtopic: Signal Transduction Pathways

### The IP₃/DAG Pathway
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ip3-dag-pathway
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** phospholipase C · IP₃ · DAG · calcium signaling · protein kinase C
**Summary:** The IP₃/DAG pathway releases intracellular calcium and activates PKC, producing diverse cellular responses including secretion and muscle contraction.

#### Phospholipase C and PIP₂ Cleavage
When certain GPCRs (coupled to Gq proteins) are activated, Gα activates **phospholipase C-β (PLCβ)**. PLCβ cleaves the membrane phospholipid **PIP₂ (phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate)** into two second messengers: **IP₃ (inositol trisphosphate)** and **DAG (diacylglycerol)**. This single cleavage event launches two parallel signaling branches simultaneously.

#### IP₃ Branch: Calcium Release
IP₃ is water-soluble and diffuses to the **endoplasmic reticulum (ER)**, where it binds IP₃ receptors — calcium channels in the ER membrane. This opens the channels and releases Ca²⁺ from ER stores into the cytoplasm, raising cytoplasmic [Ca²⁺] 10–100 fold. Calcium ions then bind **calmodulin**, a calcium-sensing protein that activates calmodulin-dependent kinases (CaM kinases), triggering responses from muscle contraction to neurotransmitter release.

#### DAG Branch: Protein Kinase C
DAG remains in the membrane and, together with calcium, activates **protein kinase C (PKC)**. PKC phosphorylates serine/threonine residues on diverse target proteins, influencing gene expression, cytoskeletal rearrangement, and secretion. Phorbol esters (tumor-promoting compounds from plants) mimic DAG, constitutively activating PKC — this is how they promote cancer.

#### Calcium as a Versatile Second Messenger
Calcium is one of the most versatile intracellular messengers. Beyond PKC and CaM kinases, Ca²⁺ directly regulates: muscle troponin (triggering contraction), synaptotagmin (triggering vesicle fusion for neurotransmitter release), calcineurin (regulating T-cell activation, target of the immunosuppressant cyclosporine), and fertilization responses in eggs. Cells invest enormous energy in pumps (SERCA, plasma membrane Ca²⁺-ATPase) to maintain low cytoplasmic [Ca²⁺] so signals remain precise.

#### Review Questions
1. What enzyme cleaves PIP₂, and what are the two products?
2. How does IP₃ raise cytoplasmic calcium concentration?
3. Why is DAG a membrane-anchored signal while IP₃ acts in the cytoplasm?

### The PI3K–AKT–mTOR Pathway
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** pi3k-akt-mtor-pathway
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** PI3K · PIP₃ · AKT · mTOR · PTEN tumor suppressor
**Summary:** The PI3K–AKT pathway integrates growth factor signals to promote cell survival, growth, and metabolism — and is mutated in many cancers.

#### PI3K Activation by RTKs
Phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) is recruited to activated RTKs (like the insulin receptor or EGF receptor) via SH2-domain interactions with phosphotyrosine residues. Once activated, PI3K phosphorylates PIP₂ in the membrane at the 3-position, producing **PIP₃**. PIP₃ serves as a docking site, recruiting proteins with PH (pleckstrin homology) domains to the membrane.

#### AKT: The Central Survival Kinase
AKT (also called PKB) is recruited to the membrane by PIP₃ and activated by phosphorylation. Activated AKT phosphorylates dozens of downstream targets:
- **BAD** → inhibits apoptosis (cell survival)
- **GSK3** → stimulates glycogen synthesis
- **FOXO transcription factors** → inhibits them (preventing pro-death gene expression)
- **mTORC1** → promotes cell growth and protein synthesis

The net effect: AKT is a pro-survival, pro-growth kinase that integrates growth factor and nutrient signals.

#### mTOR: Sensing Nutrients and Growth
mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) is a serine/threonine kinase that integrates signals about nutrient availability, energy status, and growth factors. When activated by AKT (and by amino acids via the Ragulator complex), mTOR promotes ribosome biogenesis, protein synthesis, and cell growth. **Rapamycin** (an antibiotic from Easter Island bacteria) inhibits mTOR and is used as an immunosuppressant in organ transplant patients — it prevents T-cell proliferation.

#### PTEN: The Tumor Suppressor That Reverses PI3K
**PTEN** is a phosphatase that degrades PIP₃ back to PIP₂, opposing PI3K. PTEN is one of the most commonly mutated tumor suppressors in cancer — when PTEN is lost, PIP₃ accumulates, AKT is constitutively active, and cells survive and proliferate without growth signals. PTEN mutations occur in glioblastoma, prostate cancer, and endometrial cancer.

#### Review Questions
1. Trace the pathway from RTK activation to AKT activation in four steps.
2. How does PTEN oppose the PI3K pathway, and why is PTEN mutation oncogenic?
3. Why does rapamycin suppress the immune system?

### The JAK-STAT Pathway
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** jak-stat-pathway
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** cytokine receptor · JAK · STAT · transcription factor · interferon
**Summary:** The JAK-STAT pathway transmits cytokine signals directly from cell surface to nucleus with minimal intermediate steps, enabling rapid changes in gene expression.

#### Cytokine Receptors and JAK Association
Many cytokines (interferons, interleukins, erythropoietin, growth hormone) signal through receptors that lack intrinsic kinase activity. Instead, these receptors are constitutively associated with **Janus kinases (JAKs)** on their cytoplasmic face. Ligand binding causes receptor dimerization, bringing two JAKs into proximity. The JAKs transphosphorylate each other (like RTKs) and then phosphorylate tyrosines on the receptor cytoplasmic tail.

#### STAT Recruitment and Activation
**STAT proteins (Signal Transducers and Activators of Transcription)** are latent cytoplasmic transcription factors. They bind phosphotyrosines on the receptor via SH2 domains, become phosphorylated by JAKs, dimerize with another STAT, and translocate to the nucleus. In the nucleus, STAT dimers bind specific DNA sequences and activate target gene transcription.

#### Interferons and Antiviral Defense
Type I interferons (IFN-α, IFN-β) signal through JAK1 and TYK2 to activate STAT1–STAT2 heterodimers, which upregulate antiviral genes (MHC I, OAS, PKR). This is a primary defense against viral infection. Hepatitis C treatment with pegylated interferon-α works by activating JAK-STAT signaling in liver cells to fight the virus. SARS-CoV-2 has evolved strategies to suppress JAK-STAT signaling in infected cells.

#### Diseases of Dysregulated JAK-STAT
Gain-of-function JAK2 mutations (JAK2 V617F) cause polycythemia vera — uncontrolled red blood cell production because erythropoietin signaling is constitutively on. JAK inhibitors (ruxolitinib) are approved treatments. Loss-of-function STAT3 mutations cause the immunodeficiency Hyper-IgE syndrome. Understanding JAK-STAT biology enabled a whole new class of targeted immunosuppressant drugs used in rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease.

#### Review Questions
1. How does JAK-STAT differ from RTK signaling in the number of steps between ligand binding and gene activation?
2. Why are STAT proteins considered both signal transducers and transcription factors?
3. How does interferon-α protect cells from viral infection via JAK-STAT?

### Signal Termination and Feedback
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** signal-termination-feedback
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** phosphatase · receptor downregulation · SOCS protein · feedback inhibition · desensitization
**Summary:** Cells terminate signals through phosphatases, receptor internalization, and inhibitory feedback proteins to prevent pathological overactivation.

#### Why Termination Is as Important as Activation
A signal that cannot be turned off is as dangerous as no signal at all — cancer is often the consequence of constitutively active signaling. Cells terminate signals through multiple mechanisms: (1) ligand removal/degradation, (2) receptor inactivation or internalization, (3) phosphatase activity reverting phosphorylation, (4) GTPase activity inactivating G-proteins, and (5) inhibitory feedback proteins.

#### Receptor Downregulation
Sustained activation of a receptor often triggers its own removal from the cell surface — **downregulation**. Phosphorylation of the receptor's cytoplasmic tail recruits β-arrestin, which blocks further G-protein coupling (desensitization) and promotes receptor internalization via clathrin-coated vesicles. Internalized receptors are either recycled to the surface or degraded. This is why prolonged drug use leads to tolerance — opioid receptor downregulation reduces the number of available receptors.

#### SOCS Proteins: Feedback Inhibitors of JAK-STAT
JAK-STAT activation transcriptionally induces **SOCS (Suppressors of Cytokine Signaling)** proteins, which then bind JAKs and block their kinase activity. This is a classic **negative feedback loop** — the signal produces the very proteins that shut it off. Pathogens like Mycobacterium tuberculosis exploit this by inducing SOCS to dampen immune JAK-STAT responses.

#### Review Questions
1. Describe three distinct mechanisms a cell uses to terminate a receptor tyrosine kinase signal.
2. How does β-arrestin-mediated receptor downregulation contribute to opioid tolerance?
3. Why is induction of SOCS proteins by STAT signaling an example of negative feedback?

### Comparing Signal Pathways: Applications
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** comparing-signal-pathways-review
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** GPCR · RTK · JAK-STAT · second messenger · effector kinase
**Summary:** Review of all major signaling pathways with emphasis on distinguishing features for exam and bowl questions.

#### Pathway Identification Decision Tree
Identifying the correct pathway from a description:
1. Does the receptor have 7 transmembrane helices? → **GPCR**
2. Does the receptor dimerize and cross-phosphorylate on tyrosines? → **RTK**
3. Does the receptor have no intrinsic kinase but associates with JAKs? → **JAK-STAT**
4. Does ligand binding directly open an ion channel? → **Ligand-gated ion channel**
5. Does the hormone enter the cell and bind a nuclear receptor? → **Steroid/nuclear receptor**

#### The Central Role of Phosphorylation
Across all non-steroid pathways, **phosphorylation** is the universal currency of signal transduction. Kinases add phosphate groups; phosphatases remove them. The reversibility of phosphorylation makes it perfect for rapid signaling — a single enzyme can toggle a protein's activity on and off without synthesizing or degrading it.

## Subtopic: Cell Cycle Regulation

### The Cell Cycle: Phases and Control Points
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** cell-cycle-phases-control
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** G1 · S phase · G2 · mitosis · restriction point · checkpoint
**Summary:** The cell cycle is divided into precise phases with molecular checkpoints that ensure DNA is accurately replicated and distributed before division.

#### Four Phases of the Cell Cycle
The eukaryotic cell cycle has four phases:
- **G1 (Gap 1):** Cell grows, synthesizes proteins, and prepares for DNA replication. The restriction point (R point) in late G1 is the key commitment checkpoint.
- **S phase:** DNA replication occurs — each chromosome is duplicated to form two sister chromatids joined at the centromere.
- **G2 (Gap 2):** Cell continues to grow, duplicates organelles, and checks for replication errors.
- **M phase (Mitosis):** Chromosome segregation and cytokinesis. Subdivided into prophase, prometaphase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase.

Cells can also exit the cycle into **G0**, a quiescent state (common in neurons, muscle cells, liver cells).

#### Cyclins and Cyclin-Dependent Kinases (CDKs)
Cell cycle transitions are driven by **cyclin-CDK complexes**. CDKs are kinases that require a binding partner (cyclin) for activity. Cyclin levels oscillate through the cycle:
- **Cyclin D–CDK4/6:** Active in G1; promotes passage through the restriction point by phosphorylating Rb
- **Cyclin E–CDK2:** Drives G1→S transition
- **Cyclin B–CDK1 (MPF):** Drives G2→M transition; triggers nuclear envelope breakdown and chromosome condensation

#### The Restriction Point and Rb
The restriction point is controlled by the **retinoblastoma protein (Rb)**. In early G1, Rb is unphosphorylated and binds E2F transcription factors, keeping them inactive. Growth factors activate Cyclin D–CDK4/6, which phosphorylates Rb. Phospho-Rb releases E2F, which then activates genes needed for S phase (including DNA polymerases and Cyclin E). Once past the restriction point, the cell is committed to dividing regardless of subsequent growth factor presence.

#### The Three Major Checkpoints
1. **G1/S checkpoint (restriction point):** Is the cell large enough? Is DNA undamaged? Are growth factors present?
2. **G2/M checkpoint:** Is DNA replication complete? Is DNA undamaged?
3. **Spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC):** Are all chromosomes attached to spindle microtubules at their kinetochores? (Prevents anaphase until every chromosome is attached)

#### Review Questions
1. What happens to E2F when Rb is phosphorylated by Cyclin D–CDK4/6?
2. Why is the spindle assembly checkpoint critical for preventing aneuploidy?
3. A cell lacks functional Cyclin B. Predict the consequence for the cell cycle.

### Tumor Suppressors and Oncogenes
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** tumor-suppressors-oncogenes
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** proto-oncogene · oncogene · tumor suppressor · Rb · p53 · two-hit hypothesis
**Summary:** Cancer arises when mutations disable tumor suppressors or hyperactivate proto-oncogenes, releasing cells from normal growth constraints.

#### Proto-Oncogenes and Oncogenes
**Proto-oncogenes** are normal genes encoding proteins that promote growth (growth factors, receptors, signal transducers, transcription factors). A gain-of-function mutation converting a proto-oncogene into an **oncogene** causes constitutive activity — the cell grows without a signal. Examples:
- **RAS**: point mutation → locked in GTP-bound state
- **HER2**: amplification → too many growth factor receptors
- **BCR-ABL**: chromosomal translocation → constitutively active tyrosine kinase (cause of CML; treated by imatinib/Gleevec)
- **MYC**: amplification → constitutive transcription of growth genes

Only **one mutation** in one allele is needed (dominant).

#### Tumor Suppressors
**Tumor suppressors** encode proteins that inhibit proliferation or promote apoptosis. Loss-of-function mutations remove the brake. Classic examples:
- **Rb**: regulates G1/S checkpoint (mutated in retinoblastoma, osteosarcoma)
- **p53**: "guardian of the genome" — responds to DNA damage by halting the cell cycle or triggering apoptosis (mutated in >50% of all cancers)
- **PTEN**: degrades PIP₃, limiting AKT signaling
- **BRCA1/2**: DNA repair; mutations dramatically increase breast/ovarian cancer risk

**Two-hit hypothesis (Knudson):** Both alleles of a tumor suppressor must be inactivated for loss of function. Inherited cancer syndromes (familial retinoblastoma) carry one germline mutation — only one somatic "hit" is needed to lose the second copy.

#### p53: The Guardian of the Genome
p53 is a transcription factor activated by DNA damage (via ATM/ATR kinases), oncogene activation, and hypoxia. Active p53 induces:
1. **p21** → CDK inhibitor → halts cell cycle at G1/S
2. **GADD45** → promotes DNA repair
3. **BAX/PUMA** → promotes apoptosis if damage is irreparable

MDM2 normally ubiquitinates p53 for degradation, keeping levels low. DNA damage activates ATM, which phosphorylates p53 to block MDM2 binding, stabilizing p53.

### CDK Inhibitors and Cell Cycle Arrest
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** cdk-inhibitors-cell-cycle-arrest
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** CDK inhibitor · p21 · p16 · INK4 proteins · CIP/KIP family · senescence
**Summary:** CDK inhibitor proteins act as brakes on the cell cycle, and their loss contributes to cancer while their activation mediates tumor suppression.

#### Two Families of CDK Inhibitors
CDK inhibitors (CKIs) are proteins that bind and inhibit cyclin-CDK complexes. Two major families:
- **INK4 family (p16, p15, p18, p19):** Inhibit CDK4/6 specifically, preventing Rb phosphorylation and keeping cells in G1
- **CIP/KIP family (p21, p27, p57):** Broader inhibitors of multiple CDKs; p21 is induced by p53; p27 responds to TGF-β and cell-cell contact inhibition

#### p16 and the INK4/ARF Locus
The **CDKN2A** locus encodes both **p16** (INK4a) and **p14ARF** from overlapping reading frames — a remarkable example of genomic economy. p16 inhibits CDK4/6 (blocking Rb phosphorylation). p14ARF binds MDM2, preventing p53 degradation. Loss of CDKN2A simultaneously disables both the Rb and p53 pathways — making it one of the most commonly deleted loci in human cancer (including melanoma, pancreatic cancer, and leukemias).

#### Cellular Senescence
When cells experience chronic CDK inhibitor signaling (e.g., from oncogene activation or telomere shortening), they can enter **senescence** — permanent cell cycle arrest. Senescent cells are metabolically active but non-dividing. They secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines (the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, SASP). Senescence is tumor-suppressive in young organisms (preventing cancer) but may promote inflammation and age-related disease in older organisms.

#### CDK4/6 Inhibitors in Cancer Therapy
Because CDK4/6 hyperactivation (via Cyclin D overexpression or p16 loss) is so common in cancer, CDK4/6 inhibitors (palbociclib, ribociclib) have become important breast cancer drugs. They block Rb phosphorylation, keeping cancer cells in G1 arrest. Combined with anti-estrogen therapy, CDK4/6 inhibitors significantly improve survival in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer.

3. Explain why palbociclib (a CDK4/6 inhibitor) would be ineffective in a tumor that has lost Rb.

### Cell Cycle in Development and Cancer
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** cell-cycle-development-cancer
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** contact inhibition · anchorage dependence · hallmarks of cancer · cancer stem cells
**Summary:** Normal cells exit the cycle in response to density and substrate cues that cancer cells ignore — understanding these controls explains cancer's growth advantage.

#### Normal Growth Controls Lost in Cancer
Normal cells exhibit two key growth controls: **contact inhibition** (cells stop dividing when they touch each other) and **anchorage dependence** (cells require attachment to a substrate to divide). Cancer cells lose both — they pile up in culture (loss of contact inhibition) and can grow in suspension (loss of anchorage dependence). These properties reflect changes in cell signaling: contact inhibition involves Hippo pathway activation (YAP inhibition) at cell-cell contacts.

#### Hallmarks of Cancer (Hanahan & Weinberg)
The six classic hallmarks of cancer are:
1. Self-sufficiency in growth signals (oncogene activation)
2. Insensitivity to anti-growth signals (tumor suppressor loss)
3. Evasion of apoptosis (Bcl-2 overexpression, p53 loss)
4. Limitless replicative potential (telomerase reactivation)
5. Sustained angiogenesis (VEGF production)
6. Tissue invasion and metastasis (E-cadherin loss)

Later additions include: evading immune destruction, reprogramming metabolism (Warburg effect), and genome instability.

1. Define contact inhibition and explain which signaling pathway mediates it.
2. How does telomerase reactivation contribute to "limitless replicative potential" in cancer cells?
3. Rank the six classic hallmarks by which you think is most fundamental, and justify your answer.

## Subtopic: Apoptosis (Programmed Cell Death)

### Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Apoptosis Pathways
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** apoptosis-intrinsic-extrinsic
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** caspase · Bcl-2 family · cytochrome c · death receptor · FADD · apoptosome
**Summary:** Apoptosis can be triggered from within the cell by mitochondrial signals or from outside by death receptors, both converging on caspase activation.

#### Why Apoptosis Matters
Apoptosis — programmed, orderly cell death — is essential for normal development and homeostasis. In human embryos, apoptosis sculpts fingers (by killing webbing cells) and eliminates autoreactive T cells. Adults remove ~100 billion cells daily via apoptosis. Insufficient apoptosis contributes to cancer and autoimmunity; excessive apoptosis causes neurodegeneration. Apoptosis differs from **necrosis** (accidental cell death): apoptotic cells shrink, chromatin condenses, DNA is laddered, and the cell fragments into "apoptotic bodies" without releasing inflammatory contents.

#### Intrinsic Pathway: The Mitochondrial Route
The intrinsic pathway is triggered by internal stress signals: DNA damage, oxidative stress, ER stress, growth factor withdrawal. The key decision is made at the mitochondrial outer membrane by the **Bcl-2 family**:
- **Pro-survival:** Bcl-2, Bcl-XL, Mcl-1 (keep mitochondria intact)
- **Pro-apoptotic:** Bax, Bak (form pores in the outer membrane)
- **BH3-only proteins:** Bad, Bim, Bid, Puma, Noxa (activated by stress; neutralize pro-survival Bcl-2 proteins)

When pro-apoptotic signals overcome pro-survival proteins, Bax and Bak oligomerize and permeabilize the outer mitochondrial membrane, releasing **cytochrome c** into the cytoplasm.

#### The Apoptosome and Caspase Cascade
Released cytochrome c binds **Apaf-1**, triggering the assembly of a wheel-shaped heptameric complex called the **apoptosome**. The apoptosome activates **caspase-9** (an initiator caspase), which activates **caspase-3 and caspase-7** (executioner caspases). Executioner caspases cleave hundreds of cellular proteins: structural proteins (lamins collapse the nucleus), DNase inhibitors (ICAD is cleaved, releasing CAD to fragment DNA), and cytoskeletal proteins.

#### Extrinsic Pathway: Death Receptors
External signals can also trigger apoptosis via **death receptors** (TNFR1, Fas/CD95) on the cell surface. Ligand binding (TNF, FasL) causes receptor clustering and recruitment of adaptor protein **FADD**, which recruits and activates **caspase-8** (initiator caspase). Caspase-8 then directly activates caspase-3, bypassing the mitochondria. In some cells, caspase-8 also cleaves Bid to Truncated Bid (tBid), amplifying the signal through the intrinsic pathway.

### Caspases: The Execution Machinery
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** caspases-execution-machinery
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** caspase · zymogen · initiator caspase · executioner caspase · ICAD · lamin
**Summary:** Caspases are proteases that exist as inactive zymogens and are activated in a cascade to systematically dismantle the dying cell.

#### Caspase Structure and Activation
Caspases (cysteine-aspartate proteases) cleave after aspartate residues and are produced as inactive **zymogens** (procaspases). Initiator caspases (caspase-8, -9, -10) are activated by dimerization in large signaling complexes (DISC for extrinsic, apoptosome for intrinsic). Once active, initiator caspases cleave and activate executioner caspases (caspase-3, -6, -7) by removing their prodomain.

#### Key Substrates of Executioner Caspases
The power of caspases is their broad substrate range:
- **ICAD** → releases DNase (CAD) → DNA laddering at nucleosomal intervals (a hallmark of apoptosis)
- **Lamins** → nuclear envelope disintegration and chromatin condensation
- **PARP** → DNA repair inhibited (cells stop trying to survive)
- **Cytoskeletal proteins** (gelsolin, fodrin) → cell rounding and blebbing
- **Caspase-activated scramblase** → flips phosphatidylserine to outer leaflet → "eat me" signal for macrophages

#### IAP Proteins: Caspase Inhibitors
**Inhibitor of Apoptosis Proteins (IAPs)** (including XIAP, survivin) directly bind and inhibit caspase-3, -7, and -9. Cancer cells frequently overexpress IAPs to escape apoptosis. Smac/DIABLO is a mitochondrial protein released alongside cytochrome c that neutralizes IAPs, committing the cell to death. IAP antagonist drugs (Smac mimetics) are being developed to restore apoptosis sensitivity in cancer.

#### Phosphatidylserine as an "Eat Me" Signal
One of the cleverest features of apoptosis is the "eat me" signal. Healthy cells maintain phosphatidylserine (PS) exclusively in the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane. Caspase-activated scramblase flips PS to the outer leaflet, where it is recognized by macrophage receptors (TIM4, BAI1, stabilin-2). This triggers phagocytosis of the apoptotic body without releasing inflammatory contents — a "clean" death that doesn't trigger inflammation (unlike necrosis).

### Apoptosis in Cancer and Therapy
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** apoptosis-cancer-therapy
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** Bcl-2 overexpression · venetoclax · BH3 mimetic · p53 and apoptosis · immunotherapy
**Summary:** Cancer cells survive by blocking apoptosis through Bcl-2 overexpression and other mechanisms — drugs that restore apoptosis are a major class of cancer therapy.

#### How Cancer Cells Evade Apoptosis
Cancer cells evolve multiple strategies to resist apoptosis:
- **Bcl-2 overexpression**: The t(14;18) translocation in follicular lymphoma places BCL2 under the immunoglobulin promoter, driving Bcl-2 overexpression. Bcl-2 sequesters Bax and Bak, preventing cytochrome c release.
- **p53 loss**: Without p53 to activate Puma and Noxa, DNA-damaged cells fail to undergo apoptosis.
- **XIAP overexpression**: Directly inhibits caspase-3 and -9.
- **FasL/TRAIL resistance**: Downregulation of death receptors or overexpression of FLIP (which blocks caspase-8 activation).

#### Venetoclax: A BH3 Mimetic
**Venetoclax (ABT-199)** is a BH3 mimetic — a small molecule that mimics BH3-only proteins by binding Bcl-2 and displacing Bax/Bak, restoring their ability to permeabilize the mitochondria. Venetoclax is approved for chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and AML, producing responses in patients previously resistant to chemotherapy. It is one of the first drugs designed from understanding apoptosis biology — a triumph of bench-to-bedside research.

#### Chemotherapy and Apoptosis
Most traditional chemotherapy drugs (cisplatin, doxorubicin, paclitaxel) work by inducing DNA damage or spindle disruption, ultimately activating p53 to trigger apoptosis. This is why tumors with p53 mutations are often chemotherapy-resistant — they can't apoptose in response to DNA damage. Understanding the apoptosis pathway explains both why chemotherapy works and why it fails.

2. How does venetoclax restore apoptosis in cancer cells that overexpress Bcl-2?
3. Why are p53-mutant tumors often resistant to DNA-damaging chemotherapy?

### Apoptosis vs. Necrosis vs. Autophagy
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** apoptosis-necrosis-autophagy-comparison
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** apoptosis · necrosis · autophagy · inflammation · morphological features
**Summary:** Three distinct cell death pathways differ in mechanism, morphology, and consequences for surrounding tissue.

#### Three Fates for a Dying Cell
| Feature | Apoptosis | Necrosis | Autophagy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Programmed, regulated | Injury, toxin, ischemia | Starvation, stress |
| Morphology | Cell shrinks, DNA ladders, buds | Cell swells, membrane ruptures | Autophagosomes, gradual |
| Membrane | Intact until phagocytosis | Ruptures early | Intact |
| Inflammation | No (anti-inflammatory "eat me" signal) | Yes (DAMP release) | Minimal |
| Energy requirement | Yes (ATP needed for caspase activation) | No | Yes |
| Outcome | Clean removal, no damage to neighbors | Tissue damage, inflammation | Survival or death |

#### Autophagy: Self-Eating for Survival
Autophagy is not primarily a death pathway — it's a survival mechanism. Under starvation, cells sequester cytoplasm and organelles in double-membrane **autophagosomes** that fuse with lysosomes for degradation and recycling. This provides amino acids and energy when external nutrients are scarce. Autophagy also clears damaged organelles (mitophagy clears damaged mitochondria). In cancer, autophagy can be both tumor-suppressive (by removing damaged organelles) and tumor-promoting (by sustaining cancer cells under hypoxic stress).
