#### Three Fates for a Dying Cell
| Feature | Apoptosis | Necrosis | Autophagy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Programmed, regulated | Injury, toxin, ischemia | Starvation, stress |
| Morphology | Cell shrinks, DNA ladders, buds | Cell swells, membrane ruptures | Autophagosomes, gradual |
| Membrane | Intact until phagocytosis | Ruptures early | Intact |
| Inflammation | No (anti-inflammatory "eat me" signal) | Yes (DAMP release) | Minimal |
| Energy requirement | Yes (ATP needed for caspase activation) | No | Yes |
| Outcome | Clean removal, no damage to neighbors | Tissue damage, inflammation | Survival or death |

#### Autophagy: Self-Eating for Survival
Autophagy is not primarily a death pathway — it's a survival mechanism. Under starvation, cells sequester cytoplasm and organelles in double-membrane **autophagosomes** that fuse with lysosomes for degradation and recycling. This provides amino acids and energy when external nutrients are scarce. Autophagy also clears damaged organelles (mitophagy clears damaged mitochondria). In cancer, autophagy can be both tumor-suppressive (by removing damaged organelles) and tumor-promoting (by sustaining cancer cells under hypoxic stress).

#### Review Questions
1. Why does necrosis cause inflammation while apoptosis does not?
2. Under what conditions is autophagy protective, and when might it promote cell death?
3. A cell in an oxygen-deprived environment loses ATP and ruptures. Is this apoptosis or necrosis? Justify your answer.
