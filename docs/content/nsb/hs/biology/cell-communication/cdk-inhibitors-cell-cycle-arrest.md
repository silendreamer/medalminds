#### Two Families of CDK Inhibitors
CDK inhibitors (CKIs) are proteins that bind and inhibit cyclin-CDK complexes. Two major families:
- **INK4 family (p16, p15, p18, p19):** Inhibit CDK4/6 specifically, preventing Rb phosphorylation and keeping cells in G1
- **CIP/KIP family (p21, p27, p57):** Broader inhibitors of multiple CDKs; p21 is induced by p53; p27 responds to TGF-β and cell-cell contact inhibition

#### p16 and the INK4/ARF Locus
The **CDKN2A** locus encodes both **p16** (INK4a) and **p14ARF** from overlapping reading frames — a remarkable example of genomic economy. p16 inhibits CDK4/6 (blocking Rb phosphorylation). p14ARF binds MDM2, preventing p53 degradation. Loss of CDKN2A simultaneously disables both the Rb and p53 pathways — making it one of the most commonly deleted loci in human cancer (including melanoma, pancreatic cancer, and leukemias).

#### Cellular Senescence
When cells experience chronic CDK inhibitor signaling (e.g., from oncogene activation or telomere shortening), they can enter **senescence** — permanent cell cycle arrest. Senescent cells are metabolically active but non-dividing. They secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines (the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, SASP). Senescence is tumor-suppressive in young organisms (preventing cancer) but may promote inflammation and age-related disease in older organisms.

#### CDK4/6 Inhibitors in Cancer Therapy
Because CDK4/6 hyperactivation (via Cyclin D overexpression or p16 loss) is so common in cancer, CDK4/6 inhibitors (palbociclib, ribociclib) have become important breast cancer drugs. They block Rb phosphorylation, keeping cancer cells in G1 arrest. Combined with anti-estrogen therapy, CDK4/6 inhibitors significantly improve survival in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer.
