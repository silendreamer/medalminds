#### Leaf Parts and Basic Structure
A leaf typically consists of a blade (flat photosynthetic surface) and a petiole (stalk) connecting to the stem. The blade has a network of veins (vascular tissue) that conduct water, minerals, and sugars. The epidermis is a single-cell layer on both upper and lower surfaces; it's often coated with a waxy cuticle that reduces water loss. The mesophyll between upper and lower epidermis contains the photosynthetic tissues.

#### Mesophyll Layers: Palisade and Spongy
The upper mesophyll is typically palisade parenchyma — tall, columnar cells tightly packed and rich in chloroplasts, positioned to intercept light. The lower mesophyll is spongy parenchyma — loosely packed cells with large intercellular air spaces, allowing gas diffusion. This stratification maximizes light capture while allowing gas exchange. The spongy mesophyll connects to stomata (pores) on the lower epidermis, enabling CO₂ entry for photosynthesis and water loss via transpiration.

#### Stomata: Guard Cells and Gas Exchange
Stomata are small openings in the epidermis, each surrounded by two guard cells. Guard cells have differential cell wall thickness and contain chloroplasts. When light stimulates photosynthesis in guard cells, they accumulate potassium ions and organic acids, lowering water potential and absorbing water. Turgor pressure increases, and the guard cells expand, opening the stoma. At night, guard cells lose potassium and water, wilting and closing the stoma. This is an elegant system: stoma opens when light is available for photosynthesis and closes at night or during drought to save water.

#### C3 vs C4 Leaf Anatomy
C3 plants (wheat, rice) have typical mesophyll structure. C4 plants (corn, sugarcane) have a modified anatomy with a layer of bundle sheath cells around the vein. C4 photosynthesis concentrates CO₂ around Rubisco in bundle sheath cells, reducing photorespiration. This adaptation is advantageous in hot, dry climates where CO₂ is limiting. C4 leaves have more compact structure and smaller stomata.

#### Xerophyte Adaptations: Drought-Resistant Leaves
Plants in arid environments (xerophytes) have leaves modified to conserve water: thick, waxy cuticles; sunken stomata (in pits, reducing air movement around the opening); reduced leaf surface area (smaller, rolled, or needle-like leaves); and thicker mesophyll cells with fewer air spaces. Examples: desert shrubs, cacti, pines. These adaptations reduce transpiration but also reduce CO₂ uptake, making photosynthesis less efficient. Xerophytes sacrifice productivity for survival in water-limited environments.
