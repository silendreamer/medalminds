#### Scenario 1: Chemical Spill Response
You're working with 100 mL of 6 M H₂SO₄ and accidentally knock the container off the bench, spilling the acid on the floor and on your lab partner's arm. Step 1: immediately direct partner to the safety shower — rinse for 15 minutes. Step 2: alert the instructor. Step 3: do NOT try to clean the acid spill yourself until the instructor arrives — the instructor determines if sodium bicarbonate is appropriate. Step 4: document the incident.

Key point: safety shower use comes BEFORE any other action when acid contacts skin. Removing contaminated clothing while at the shower (not before reaching it) is also appropriate.

#### Scenario 2: Graph Interpretation
A student plots [NO₂] concentration (y-axis) vs. time (x-axis) during the decomposition reaction 2NO₂ → 2NO + O₂. The curve decreases steeply at first, then levels off. Questions: (a) Why does the curve level off? (NO₂ concentration decreases, slowing the rate as the reaction progresses.) (b) What does the slope at any point represent? (The instantaneous rate of reaction at that time.) (c) How would doubling the initial concentration change the graph's appearance? (Steeper initial slope, same eventual plateau if reaction goes to completion.)

#### Scenario 3: Reading an SDS
An SDS for benzene (C₆H₆) shows: Flash point 11°F (−11°C), NFPA hazard rating 3-1-0-carcinogen. Interpret: Flash point 11°F means benzene ignites at temperatures as low as −11°C — extremely flammable, must keep away from all ignition sources. NFPA 3-1-0: health = 3 (serious — carcinogen), flammability = 1 (slight), reactivity = 0 (stable). What PPE is required? Full PPE: goggles, nitrile gloves (latex doesn't protect against benzene), lab coat, and use only in fume hood.

#### Review Questions
1. A student sees the GHS flammable pictogram (flame) and the exclamation mark on a chemical. What are the minimum PPE requirements?
2. A graph shows that product concentration increases then plateaus. What does this tell you about the reaction's progress?
3. Why is "wear gloves" insufficient safety guidance for a chemical lab?
