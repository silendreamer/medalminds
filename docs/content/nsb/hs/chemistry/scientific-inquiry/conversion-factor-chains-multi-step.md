#### When One Step Isn't Enough
Many real chemistry problems require chaining multiple conversion factors. The rule: write all factors in a row, with units that should cancel arranged to cancel. Never start calculating until you've written out the complete unit chain and verified that only the target unit remains.

Example: Convert 2.50 g/mL to kg/L:
2.50 g/mL × (1 kg / 1000 g) × (1000 mL / 1 L) = 2.50 kg/L

The two conversions (g→kg and mL→L) happen to cancel, giving 2.50 kg/L — numerically the same! This is a useful sanity check: g/cm³ numerically equals kg/L equals 1000 kg/m³.

#### Chemistry's Most Common Conversion Chain
Grams → moles → particles (for single substances):
g × (1 mol / molar mass g) × (6.022 × 10²³ / 1 mol) = particles

Example: How many molecules in 18.0 g of water (M = 18.02 g/mol)?
18.0 g × (1 mol / 18.02 g) × (6.022 × 10²³ molecules / 1 mol) = **6.01 × 10²³ molecules** — exactly 1 mol, as expected.

#### Stoichiometry Preview: Mole Ratios as Conversion Factors
In chemical equations, coefficients act as conversion factors between moles. For N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, the ratio (2 mol NH₃ / 1 mol N₂) is a conversion factor. How many moles of NH₃ form from 0.50 mol N₂?
0.50 mol N₂ × (2 mol NH₃ / 1 mol N₂) = 1.0 mol NH₃.

#### Compound Unit Conversions
Convert 65 mi/hr to ft/s:
65 mi/hr × (5280 ft / 1 mi) × (1 hr / 3600 s) = 65 × 5280 / 3600 ft/s = **95.3 ft/s**

Always write out the full chain before calculating. This prevents upside-down conversion factors.
