## Subtopic: Mole Concept & Avogadro's Number

### Understanding the Mole
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** understanding-the-mole
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** mole · Avogadro's number · atomic mass unit · molar mass
**Summary:** The mole is a unit that relates the atomic scale to the macroscopic scale we can measure.

#### What is a Mole?
A mole is defined as exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number). This definition bridges the submicroscopic world of atoms and molecules to laboratory-scale quantities. One mole of carbon-12 has a mass of exactly 12 grams by definition—this is why molar mass (grams per mole) is numerically equal to atomic mass (in atomic mass units). Understanding this connection is foundational to all stoichiometry.

#### Avogadro's Number and Scaling
Avogadro's number arose historically because scientists needed a conversion factor. The atomic mass unit (amu) is defined so that one carbon-12 atom weighs 12 amu. To convert this to the laboratory scale, Avogadro's number is exactly the number of amu per gram. This is not coincidence—it's by definition. One mole of any element contains Avogadro's number of atoms.

#### Molar Mass
Molar mass (M) is the mass of one mole of a substance, expressed in g/mol. For elements, it's numerically equal to the atomic mass. For compounds, add the atomic masses of all atoms in the formula. Water (H₂O) has molar mass 18.0 g/mol (2×1.008 + 15.999).

#### Particle-to-Gram Conversions
Use dimensional analysis with Avogadro's number to convert between counts of particles and mass. Example: 2.5 × 10²⁴ oxygen atoms = (2.5 × 10²⁴ atoms) × (1 mol / 6.022 × 10²³ atoms) × (16.0 g/mol) = 66.4 g of oxygen.

#### Review Questions
1. How many moles are in 4.8 grams of carbon (atomic mass = 12)?
2. What is the molar mass of calcium chloride (CaCl₂)?
3. How many atoms are in 0.50 moles of nitrogen gas (N₂)?

### Molar Mass Calculations
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** molar-mass-calculations
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** molar mass · atomic mass · empirical formula · molecular formula
**Summary:** Calculating molar mass from atomic masses is straightforward addition.

#### Finding Molar Mass from a Formula
Add the atomic mass of each element, accounting for subscripts. For ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃): N (14.0) + H (1.008×4) + N (14.0) + O (16.0×3) = 14.0 + 4.032 + 14.0 + 48.0 = 80.0 g/mol.

#### Atomic Mass from the Periodic Table
The periodic table lists average atomic masses based on isotope abundances. These are the values used in molar mass calculations. Hydrogen averages 1.008 amu (not exactly 1), oxygen averages 16.00 amu (not exactly 16), and carbon averages 12.01 amu.

#### Compounds with Multiple Elements
For compounds with repeated elements, multiply the atomic mass by the subscript. In ethane (C₂H₆): 2×(12.01) + 6×(1.008) = 24.02 + 6.048 = 30.1 g/mol.

#### Connection to Stoichiometry
Molar mass is the bridge between grams (what we measure) and moles (what balanced equations use). It appears in every stoichiometric calculation.

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate the molar mass of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆).
2. A compound contains 32% sulfur and 68% oxygen by mass. What is its empirical formula? (Hint: this prepares for next topic.)
3. What is the molar mass of Al₂(SO₄)₃?

### Conversions: Moles, Grams, Particles
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** mole-conversions
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** dimensional analysis · molar mass · Avogadro's number · conversion factors
**Summary:** Three key conversions connect moles, grams, and particle counts.

#### Moles ↔ Grams
Multiply moles by molar mass to get grams: grams = moles × (g/mol). Reverse: moles = grams / (g/mol). Example: 0.250 mol H₂SO₄ × 98.1 g/mol = 24.5 g.

#### Moles ↔ Particles
Multiply moles by Avogadro's number to get particle count: particles = moles × (6.022 × 10²³). Reverse: moles = particles / (6.022 × 10²³).

#### Grams ↔ Particles
Chain two conversions: grams → moles (divide by molar mass) → particles (multiply by Avogadro's). Example: 11.2 g of CO₂ = (11.2 g) / (44.0 g/mol) × (6.022 × 10²³) = 1.53 × 10²³ molecules.

#### Dimensional Analysis Strategy
Write conversion factors as fractions so units cancel. (X g) × (1 mol / M g) × (6.022 × 10²³ / 1 mol) = particles. Arrows and labels make it clear which direction you're converting.

#### Review Questions
1. How many grams of O₂ contain 2.0 × 10²⁴ molecules?
2. Convert 0.50 g of NaCl to moles (molar mass = 58.5 g/mol).
3. How many Fe atoms are in 5.6 g of iron metal? (Atomic mass Fe = 55.8)

### Application: Mole Calculations in Real Contexts
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** mole-applications
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** stoichiometry · pharmaceutical dosing · industrial production · molar conversions
**Summary:** Mole conversions are used in dosing, production, and environmental monitoring.

#### Pharmaceutical Example
An antacid tablet contains 500 mg of CaCO₃ (molar mass 100.1 g/mol). How many moles is this? (500 mg = 0.500 g) / (100.1 g/mol) = 0.00499 mol ≈ 5.0 × 10⁻³ mol. Doctors use molar amounts to calculate how much stomach acid is neutralized.

#### Industrial Production
A factory produces 2.5 metric tons of ammonia (NH₃, molar mass 17.0 g/mol) per day. How many moles is this? (2.5 × 10⁶ g) / (17.0 g/mol) = 1.47 × 10⁵ mol/day. This number helps estimate energy use, reactor size, and production costs.

#### Atmospheric Chemistry
Air contains about 21% O₂ by volume. In a sealed room of 50 m³, how many moles of O₂ are present? Using ideal gas law (covered later) or molar volume (22.4 L/mol at STP), we can calculate respiration rates and ventilation needs.

#### Environmental Monitoring
A water sample contains 0.010 M (molar) lead ions. What mass of lead is in 1.0 L of water? (0.010 mol/L) × (1.0 L) × (207.2 g/mol) = 2.1 g of Pb—well above EPA safety limits.

#### Review Questions
1. A vitamin tablet contains 100 mg of vitamin C (C₆H₈O₆, molar mass 176.1 g/mol). How many moles?
2. A factory needs to produce 5,000 g of table salt (NaCl). How many moles must they make?
3. Air in a room contains 400 ppm CO₂. In a 300 m³ room, how many moles of CO₂ are present?

### Application: Percent Composition and Empirical Formulas
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** percent-composition-empirical
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** percent composition · empirical formula · molar mass · molecular formula
**Summary:** Finding what percentage each element contributes to a compound's mass.

#### Calculating Percent Composition
For glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), molar mass = 180.2 g/mol. % C = (6 × 12.01 / 180.2) × 100% = 40.0%. % H = (12 × 1.008 / 180.2) × 100% = 6.7%. % O = (6 × 16.00 / 180.2) × 100% = 53.3%. Note: 40.0 + 6.7 + 53.3 = 100%.

#### Finding Empirical Formula from Percent Composition
If a compound is 40.0% C, 6.7% H, and 53.3% O, assume 100 g sample. Convert to moles: C: 40.0 g / 12.01 g/mol = 3.33 mol. H: 6.7 g / 1.008 g/mol = 6.65 mol. O: 53.3 g / 16.00 g/mol = 3.33 mol. Divide by smallest: C: 1, H: 2, O: 1. Empirical formula is CH₂O.

#### Molecular Formula from Molar Mass
The empirical formula mass of CH₂O is 30.0 g/mol. If the true molar mass is 180 g/mol, the molecular formula is (180/30) = 6 times the empirical: C₆H₁₂O₆.

#### Worked Example
A compound contains 85.6% C and 14.4% H by mass. Its molar mass is 28.0 g/mol. Find the molecular formula. Assume 100 g: C: 85.6/12.01 = 7.13 mol, H: 14.4/1.008 = 14.3 mol. Ratio: C:H = 7.13:14.3 = 1:2. Empirical formula CH₂, empirical mass 14.0. Molecular formula is (28.0/14.0) × CH₂ = C₂H₄ (ethylene).

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate % composition of Ca(OH)₂.
2. A compound is 92% C and 8% H. Its molar mass is 78 g/mol. Find the molecular formula.
3. Aspirin (C₉H₈O₄) contains what % acetyl group (C₂H₃O)?

### Mixed Review: Mole and Mass Problems
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** mole-mass-review
**Estimated time:** 10 min
**Key concepts:** molar mass · Avogadro's number · percent composition · dimensional analysis
**Summary:** Multi-step problems connecting grams, moles, and particles.

#### Multi-Step Conversion
How many carbon atoms are in 5.0 g of ethanol (C₂H₆O)? Step 1: Molar mass of C₂H₆O = 2(12.01) + 6(1.008) + 16.00 = 46.07 g/mol. Step 2: Moles = 5.0 g / 46.07 g/mol = 0.109 mol. Step 3: Moles of C atoms = 0.109 mol × 2 = 0.218 mol C. Step 4: Number of C atoms = 0.218 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.31 × 10²³ atoms.

#### Density Bridge
A liquid has density 0.87 g/mL and molar mass 58.1 g/mol. What is its molar volume (L/mol)? For 1 mole: mass = 58.1 g, volume = 58.1 g / (0.87 g/mL) = 66.8 mL = 0.0668 L. Molar volume = 0.0668 L/mol.

- 64 g of oxygen gas (O₂) equals how many moles? (Answer: 2 mol)
- What's the molar mass of Ca₃(PO₄)₂? (Answer: 310.2 g/mol)

#### Review Questions
1. How many H atoms are in 11.0 g of CH₄?
2. A solution has 0.50 mol/L of CuSO₄. What mass is in 2.0 L?
3. Order by number of molecules: 10 g CO₂, 10 g H₂O, 10 g O₂.

## Subtopic: Balancing Chemical Equations

### Introduction to Chemical Equations
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** chemical-equations-intro
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** reactants · products · subscripts · coefficients · conservation of mass
**Summary:** Chemical equations describe which atoms rearrange in a reaction and must balance—same number of each atom on both sides.

#### Anatomy of an Equation
In the reaction: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the "2" before H₂ and H₂O are coefficients (tell how many molecules). The "2" in H₂ is a subscript (tells atoms per molecule). Reactants (left side) transform into products (right side). Arrow shows reaction direction (→) or equilibrium (⇌).

#### The Law of Conservation of Mass
Atoms are not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction—they're just rearranged. The equation must show the same number of each atom on both sides. H₂ + O₂ is NOT balanced (2 H and 2 O on left, 2 H and 1 O on right). Add coefficient: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O balances (4 H and 2 O on each side).

#### States of Matter
Notation tells you the physical state: (s) = solid, (l) = liquid, (g) = gas, (aq) = aqueous (dissolved in water). Example: Zn(s) + HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g) specifies that hydrogen gas is released.

#### Symbols You Need to Know
→ forward reaction; ⇌ reversible/equilibrium; ↑ gas escapes; ↓ precipitate forms; Δ means heat applied; ⚡ means electric current applied.

#### Review Questions
1. What does the coefficient "3" mean in 3CO₂?
2. In the equation 2Al + 3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃, how many chlorine atoms react?
3. What does (aq) mean? Give an example compound.

### Balancing Simple Equations
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** balancing-equations-simple
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** coefficient · atom count · trial and error · inspection method
**Summary:** Balance equations by adjusting coefficients so atom counts match on both sides.

#### The Balancing Process
Start with the unbalanced equation. Count atoms of each element on left and right. Adjust coefficients (not subscripts!) to balance. Check your work. Example: C + O₂ → CO₂. Left: 1 C, 2 O. Right: 1 C, 2 O. Already balanced.

#### Balancing by Inspection
For Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃: Count oxygen (even numbers are easier). On the right, 3 oxygen atoms per Fe₂O₃. Try coefficient 2 on the product: Fe + O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃. Now balance iron: need 4 Fe on the left. 4Fe + O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃. Check oxygen: 3 O per Fe₂O₃ × 2 = 6 O needed, so 3O₂ on left. Final: 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃.

#### Common Mistakes
- Changing subscripts (wrong—it changes the compound itself)
- Forgetting to count all atoms (especially in polyatomic ions)
- Over-balancing (using fractional coefficients then forgetting to convert to whole numbers)

#### Fractional Coefficients
Sometimes trial-and-error gives fractions (e.g., ½O₂). Clear fractions by multiplying all coefficients by 2. Example: 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O (multiply everything by 2 if you started with 1 and 3.5).

#### Review Questions
1. Balance: N₂ + H₂ → NH₃
2. Balance: Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂
3. Balance: C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

### Balancing Complex Equations
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** balancing-complex-equations
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** polyatomic ions · multistep balancing · half-reaction method · subscripts
**Summary:** Larger equations and those with polyatomic ions need systematic approaches.

#### Preserving Polyatomic Ions
When balancing equations with polyatomic ions like NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, or PO₄³⁻, treat the ion as a single unit if it appears unchanged on both sides. In Fe(NO₃)₃ + NaOH → Fe(OH)₃ + NaNO₃, the NO₃⁻ ion is preserved, so balance it as a block rather than separating N and O.

#### Multistep Balancing Strategy
Balance the most complex species first (usually compounds with multiple elements). Then balance single elements. Example: C₂H₅OH + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Start with carbon (C₂H₅OH has 2 C, so use 2CO₂). Then hydrogen (C₂H₅OH has 6 H, so use 3H₂O). Finally, oxygen: need 2 + 3 = 5 atoms from O₂, so use 3O₂. Check: C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O. (1+6=7 C, 2+6=8 H, 6+3=9 O on each side.)

#### Oxidation-Reduction Equations (Preview)
Redox reactions involve electron transfer. The half-reaction method (balance separately for oxidation and reduction) handles these systematically. Example: Fe + Cu²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + Cu requires tracking electron loss and gain.

#### Common Complex Equations
Combustion: CₓHᵧ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Acid-base: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O (simple, straightforward). Precipitation: AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃ (spectator ions).

#### Review Questions
1. Balance: Ca(OH)₂ + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + H₂O
2. Balance: Mg + N₂ → Mg₃N₂
3. Balance: C₆H₁₂O₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

### Application: Combustion and Real Reactions
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** combustion-reactions
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** combustion · hydrocarbon oxidation · stoichiometric ratio · energy release
**Summary:** Combustion is the most common reaction—fuel burns in oxygen to produce CO₂ and H₂O.

#### General Combustion Equation
Hydrocarbon CₓHᵧ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Ethane example: C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Balance carbon: 2CO₂. Balance hydrogen: 3H₂O. Balance oxygen: need 4 + 3 = 7, so 3.5O₂ or multiply by 2: 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O.

#### Real-World Application: Fuel Economy
Gasoline is approximately C₈H₁₈. Complete combustion: 2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O. For every 1 mole of octane (114 g), 16 moles (704 g) of CO₂ are produced—why cars contribute to climate change.

#### Incomplete Combustion
When oxygen is limited, products include CO and C (soot) in addition to CO₂: 2C₂H₆ + 5O₂ → 4CO + 6H₂O. Incomplete combustion produces less energy and more pollution.

#### Thermochemistry Connection
Combustion releases energy (exothermic, ΔH < 0). The equation 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O ΔH = -572 kJ tells you that 572 kJ is released per 2 moles of H₂ burned. This is why combustion powers vehicles and electricity generation.

#### Review Questions
1. Balance the combustion of propane (C₃H₈).
2. How many moles of O₂ are needed to completely burn 1 mole of benzene (C₆H₆)?
3. When 2 g of H₂ burns in O₂, releasing -285.8 kJ/mol, how much energy is released?

### Application: Acid-Base Neutralization Reactions
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** acid-base-neutralization
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** strong acid · strong base · salt · neutralization · stoichiometry
**Summary:** Acid-base reactions produce salt and water; equations are straightforward to balance.

#### Basic Acid-Base Equation
Acid + Base → Salt + Water. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. Check: 1 H, 1 Cl, 1 Na, 1 O on each side—balanced.

#### Polyprotic Acids
Some acids donate more than one proton. H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid) can react in two stages: H₂SO₄ + NaOH → NaHSO₄ + H₂O (first proton) or H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O (both protons). The stoichiometry depends on how many H⁺ are available and how many OH⁻ are added.

#### Worked Example: Ammonia Neutralization
Ammonia (NH₃) is a weak base. When 2 mol of HCl reacts with 2 mol of NH₃: 2HCl + 2NH₃ → 2NH₄Cl. This is a 1:1 stoichiometry—equal moles react.

#### Titration Preparation
Neutralization equations are the foundation for titration calculations. If you know the concentration and volume of acid, and the stoichiometry (1:1 or 1:2 etc.), you can find how much base is needed to reach the equivalence point.

#### Review Questions
1. Balance: H₃PO₄ + Ca(OH)₂ → Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + H₂O
2. What mass of NaOH is needed to neutralize 100 mL of 0.50 M HCl?
3. A diprotic acid H₂A reacts with NaOH. Write and balance both neutralization equations.

## Subtopic: Reaction Types & Stoichiometric Ratios

### Types of Chemical Reactions
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** reaction-types
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** synthesis · decomposition · combustion · displacement · acid-base · precipitation
**Summary:** Reactions fall into categories based on the pattern of reactant and product changes.

#### Synthesis (Combination)
Two or more substances combine to form one product. 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl. Usually: A + B → AB. Often exothermic (release heat). Examples: rusting (Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃), formation of ammonia (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃).

#### Decomposition
One compound breaks into two or more substances. 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. Usually: AB → A + B. Many are endothermic (require heat). Examples: thermal decomposition of limestone (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂), electrolysis of water (2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ with electricity).

#### Combustion
A substance reacts with oxygen, usually releasing heat and light. CₓHᵧ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Nearly always exothermic. Examples: burning wood, candles, fossil fuels. Characterized by rapid oxidation and visible flames or heat.

#### Single Displacement (Replacement)
One element displaces another from a compound. Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. Pattern: A + BC → AC + B. Must use the activity series to predict if the reaction occurs—more active elements displace less active ones.

#### Review Questions
1. Classify: 2KMnO₄ → K₂MnO₄ + MnO₂ + O₂
2. Classify: AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃
3. Write a synthesis reaction and a decomposition reaction.

### Single and Double Displacement
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** displacement-reactions
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** activity series · single displacement · double displacement · aqueous reactions
**Summary:** Displacement reactions involve one or both reactants swapping partners.

#### Single Displacement Rules
Use the activity series (a ranked list of elements by reactivity). More active elements displace less active ones from their compounds. Metal activity order (high to low): K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Ni > Sn > Pb > H > Cu > Ag > Au. Example: Fe + Cu²⁺ → Fe²⁺ + Cu (Fe is more active, displaces Cu).

#### Halogen Activity Series
F > Cl > Br > I. So Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂ (Cl displaces Br because Cl is more active).

#### Double Displacement
Two compounds exchange ions. AB + CD → AD + CB. Driving force is often formation of an insoluble precipitate, weak acid, or gas. Example: AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃ (AgCl precipitate drives reaction). Another: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O (weak acid H₂O forms).

#### Predicting Products
For double displacement, use the cation from one compound and anion from the other. For AgNO₃ + NaCl: Ag⁺ with Cl⁻ = AgCl; Na⁺ with NO₃⁻ = NaNO₃. Use solubility rules to predict if a precipitate forms.

### Acid-Base and Precipitation Reactions
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** acid-base-precipitation
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** acid-base reaction · salt formation · precipitation · solubility rules
**Summary:** These double displacement reactions form water or insoluble solids.

#### Acid-Base (Neutralization)
An acid reacts with a base to form a salt and water. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. The H⁺ from acid and OH⁻ from base combine: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. Strong acid + strong base → neutral salt. Weak acid + strong base → basic salt. Strong acid + weak base → acidic salt.

#### Precipitation Reactions
Two aqueous solutions mix, and an insoluble salt forms. AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃. The arrow ↓ indicates a precipitate. Solubility rules predict which salts precipitate: salts of Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, and Ba²⁺ with specific anions are often insoluble; salts of Na⁺, K⁺, and NH₄⁺ are usually soluble.

#### Solubility Rules Summary
- Nitrates (NO₃⁻), perchlorates, and ammonium salts: always soluble
- Halides (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻): soluble except with Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺
- Sulfates (SO₄²⁻): soluble except with Ba²⁺, Pb²⁺, Ca²⁺ (some)
- Carbonates, phosphates, sulfides: generally insoluble except with Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺
- Hydroxides: soluble only for alkali metals and Ba(OH)₂

#### Spectator Ions and Net Ionic Equations
In AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃, the Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ don't participate—they're spectator ions. The net ionic equation shows only the reacting ions: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).

### Application: Stoichiometric Ratios in Reactions
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** stoichiometric-ratios-app
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** mole ratio · limiting reagent · stoichiometric calculations · balanced equation
**Summary:** Balanced equations give the mole ratio—how many moles of each reactant and product.

#### Reading Mole Ratios from Equations
In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the coefficients tell you the mole ratio: 2 mol H₂ : 1 mol O₂ : 2 mol H₂O. This ratio holds regardless of the absolute amounts. So 4 mol H₂ requires 2 mol O₂ and produces 4 mol H₂O (scaled by 2).

#### Worked Example: Nitrogen Fixation
The Haber process makes ammonia: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. If 10 mol of H₂ reacts, how much NH₃ forms? Use the mole ratio 3 H₂ : 2 NH₃. (10 mol H₂) × (2 mol NH₃ / 3 mol H₂) = 6.67 mol NH₃.

#### Gram-to-Gram Conversions
If 28 g of N₂ reacts with excess H₂, how much NH₃ forms? Step 1: Moles of N₂ = 28 g / 28 g/mol = 1 mol. Step 2: From equation, 1 mol N₂ → 2 mol NH₃. Step 3: Mass of NH₃ = 2 mol × 17 g/mol = 34 g.

#### Scaling Recipes
The stoichiometric ratio is like a recipe. Want to make twice as much product? Use twice as much of each reactant. The ratio stays constant.

#### Review Questions
1. In 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O, how many moles of CO₂ form from 3 mol of C₂H₆?
2. How many grams of CO₂ are produced when 10 g of C₂H₆ burn?
3. In the reaction Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu, how many grams of Cu form from 5.6 g of Fe?

### Application: Percent Yield and Limiting Reagents
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** percent-yield-limiting
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** theoretical yield · actual yield · percent yield · limiting reagent · excess reagent
**Summary:** Real reactions don't produce 100% of the theoretical amount—percent yield measures efficiency.

#### Theoretical vs. Actual Yield
Theoretical yield is the amount of product calculated from stoichiometry, assuming perfect conditions. Actual yield is what you actually get in the lab. Percent yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100%. Example: if theory predicts 10 g of product but you collect 9.2 g, percent yield = 92%.

#### Limiting Reagent Concept
In a reaction, one reactant runs out first—that's the limiting reagent. Others are in excess. Example: to make 2 sandwiches, you need 2 slices bread and 4 slices cheese. If you have 5 slices bread and 6 slices cheese, bread is limiting (need 4 slices, only have 5 is plenty, but need 2 sandwiches × 2 slices = only 4 slices cheese to match 2 pieces; actually cheese is limiting). Wait, let me redo: 1 sandwich = 1 slice bread + 2 slices cheese. With 5 slices bread, you can make 5 sandwiches. With 6 slices cheese, you can make 3 sandwiches. Cheese is limiting; you make 3 sandwiches (limiting reagent determines yield).

#### Worked Example: Find Limiting Reagent
Reaction: 3H₂ + N₂ → 2NH₃. You have 10 mol H₂ and 5 mol N₂. Which limits the reaction? H₂ needs ratio 3:1 with N₂. You have 10:5 = 2:1, so H₂ is in relative excess and N₂ is limiting. 5 mol N₂ × (2 mol NH₃ / 1 mol N₂) = 10 mol NH₃ produced.

#### Calculating Percent Yield
If the theoretical yield is 10 mol NH₃ but you isolate 8.5 mol, percent yield = (8.5 / 10) × 100% = 85%. Losses occur from side reactions, incomplete reactions, or product lost during isolation.

#### Review Questions
1. If 5.0 g of Fe reacts with excess O₂ to form Fe₂O₃, and you collect 6.5 g of product, what's the percent yield? (Molar mass Fe₂O₃ = 160 g/mol)
2. In 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO, you start with 5.0 g Mg and 2.0 g O₂. Which is limiting? How much MgO forms?
3. A reaction has a theoretical yield of 45 g but an actual yield of 36 g. What is the percent yield?

#### Multi-Step Stoichiometry Problem
Copper(II) oxide reacts with hydrogen gas: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O. If 4.0 g of CuO reacts with 1.0 g H₂, which is limiting? CuO: molar mass 80 g/mol, so 4.0 g = 0.050 mol. ### Competition Extension: Stoichiometry Speed and Strategy
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** stoichiometry-speed
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** mole ratio · dimensional analysis · limiting reagent · percent yield
**Summary:** Bowl-style stoichiometry questions test quick calculation and correct unit handling.

#### Bowl-Style Toss-Up #1
"In the combustion of ethane (C₂H₆), how many moles of O₂ are needed to burn 3 moles of ethane? 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O." (Answer: 10.5 mol, from 3 × 7/2.)

#### Bowl-Style Toss-Up #2
"A reaction produces a theoretical yield of 75 g but an actual yield of 60 g. What is the percent yield?" (Answer: 80%.)

#### Dimensional Analysis Strategy
Always line up units to cancel. Example: (X g reactant) × (1 mol / molar mass) × (mole ratio from equation) × (molar mass of product) = g of product. Write the conversion factors as fractions so unwanted units cancel and desired units remain.

#### Common Traps in Bowl Competition
- Forgetting to balance the equation first (wrong stoichiometry = wrong answer)
- Using the wrong molar mass (check the periodic table carefully)
- Mixing up which reactant is limiting in a two-reagent problem
- Forgetting that percent yield is not the same as percent composition
