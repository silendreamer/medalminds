## Subtopic: Chemical Formulas & Nomenclature

### Naming Ionic Compounds
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** naming-ionic-compounds
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** ionic compound · cation · anion · Stock system · polyatomic ions
**Summary:** Master the rules for naming binary ionic compounds and compounds with polyatomic ions using the Stock naming system.

#### Binary Ionic Compounds (Metal + Nonmetal)
Binary ionic compounds consist of a metal cation and a nonmetal anion. Naming rules: (1) Name the cation first (element name, unmodified). (2) Name the anion second, changing the ending to *-ide*. Examples: NaCl = sodium chloride; MgO = magnesium oxide; CaBr₂ = calcium bromide.

#### Transition Metal Cations: The Stock System
Transition metals can form multiple cation charges. Use Roman numerals (Stock system) to specify the charge. Fe²⁺ = iron(II); Fe³⁺ = iron(III). FeCl₂ = iron(II) chloride; FeCl₃ = iron(III) chloride. The charge is determined from the known anion charge and electrical neutrality: if Cl⁻ and there are 3 Cl, cation must be 3+.

#### Polyatomic Ion Compounds
Many anions are polyatomic (multi-atom, with charge). Must-know anions: NO₃⁻ (nitrate), SO₄²⁻ (sulfate), PO₄³⁻ (phosphate), CO₃²⁻ (carbonate), OH⁻ (hydroxide), NH₄⁺ (ammonium, cation). Naming: Ca(NO₃)₂ = calcium nitrate; (NH₄)₂SO₄ = ammonium sulfate.

#### Naming from Formula: Worked Examples
- Cu₂O: copper has 2 atoms (each 1+) bonding to O²⁻ → copper(I) oxide
- Fe₂O₃: O is 2−, two Fe balance three O (total 6−) → each Fe is 3+ → iron(III) oxide
- Al₂(SO₄)₃: Al³⁺, SO₄²⁻ → aluminum sulfate (Al is always 3+, no Roman numeral needed)

#### Review Questions
1. Name the following compounds: FeBr₃, Na₂SO₄, Pb(NO₃)₂, Cu₃PO₄.
2. Write formulas for: iron(III) hydroxide, calcium phosphate, ammonium carbonate.
3. Why do we use Roman numerals when naming compounds with transition metals like iron or copper, but not when naming compounds with calcium or aluminum?

### Naming Covalent Compounds and Acids
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** naming-covalent-acids
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** covalent compound · IUPAC prefixes · binary acid · oxyacid
**Summary:** Name molecular (covalent) compounds using Greek prefixes and name acids using the -ic/-ous and hydro- conventions.

#### Naming Binary Covalent Compounds
Binary covalent compounds (nonmetal + nonmetal) use Greek prefixes for atom counts: mono(1), di(2), tri(3), tetra(4), penta(5), hexa(6), hepta(7), octa(8). Rule: name first nonmetal element, then second with -ide suffix. Drop "mono-" for the first element only.
- N₂O₄ = dinitrogen tetroxide
- PCl₅ = phosphorus pentachloride
- SO₃ = sulfur trioxide
- CO = carbon monoxide

#### Binary Acids (HX dissolved in water)
Binary acids (hydrogen + one nonmetal element, dissolved in water): prefix *hydro-*, root of element, suffix *-ic acid*. HCl(aq) = hydrochloric acid; HBr(aq) = hydrobromic acid; H₂S(aq) = hydrosulfuric acid.

#### Oxyacids
Oxyacids contain hydrogen, oxygen, and another element. Naming based on the polyatomic ion: if anion ends in *-ate*, acid ends in *-ic acid*; if anion ends in *-ite*, acid ends in *-ous acid*.
- NO₃⁻ (nitrate) → HNO₃ = nitric acid
- NO₂⁻ (nitrite) → HNO₂ = nitrous acid
- SO₄²⁻ (sulfate) → H₂SO₄ = sulfuric acid
- H₃PO₄ = phosphoric acid (PO₄³⁻ = phosphate)

#### Review Questions
1. Name: N₂O₅, H₂SO₃, HClO₄, PCl₃.
2. Write formulas for: phosphorus trichloride, nitrous acid, dinitrogen monoxide, carbonic acid.
3. What is the difference in naming an oxyacid whose ion ends in -ate versus one whose ion ends in -ite?

## Subtopic: Ionic & Covalent Bonding

### Ionic Bonding: Electron Transfer and Lattice Formation
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ionic-bonding-fundamentals
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** ionic bond · lattice energy · cation · anion · Born-Haber cycle
**Summary:** Ionic bonds form by complete electron transfer from metal to nonmetal; lattice energy quantifies the stability of the resulting ionic solid.

#### Electron Transfer and Ion Formation
Ionic bonds form when the electronegativity difference between two atoms is large (typically ΔEN > 1.7), causing complete electron transfer. The metal loses electrons to form a cation; the nonmetal gains electrons to form an anion. Example: Na (EN 0.93) + Cl (EN 3.16) → Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl. Na reaches the noble gas configuration of Ne; Cl reaches the configuration of Ar.

#### Lattice Energy
Lattice energy is the energy released when gaseous ions combine to form one mole of solid ionic compound. Highly exothermic (released → negative ΔH for lattice formation). Magnitude depends on ion charge and size: higher charge → greater lattice energy; smaller ions → greater lattice energy. NaCl: −787 kJ/mol; MgO: −3,791 kJ/mol (Mg²⁺O²⁻ vs Na⁺Cl⁻ — higher charges and smaller size).

#### Properties of Ionic Compounds
Ionic compounds form crystal lattices with alternating cations and anions. Properties: high melting/boiling points (strong electrostatic attraction in lattice); conduct electricity when dissolved in water or melted (ions free to move); hard but brittle (lattice shatters along slip planes when ions of like charge align).

#### The Ionic Bond Is Not Directional
Unlike covalent bonds, ionic bonds have no preferred direction — the attraction between ions is electrostatic and acts equally in all directions. This allows the formation of extended lattice structures rather than discrete molecules.

#### Review Questions
1. Why does MgO have a much higher lattice energy than NaCl?
2. Explain why NaCl conducts electricity when dissolved in water but not in solid form.
3. Which has the higher melting point: NaF or NaCl? Explain using lattice energy arguments.

### Covalent Bonding: Sharing Electrons
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** covalent-bonding-fundamentals
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** covalent bond · single/double/triple bonds · bond length · bond energy · polar vs. nonpolar
**Summary:** Covalent bonds form by electron sharing between nonmetals; bond order determines bond length and energy.

#### Why Atoms Share Electrons
When two nonmetals approach, neither has a strong enough tendency to completely take electrons from the other. Instead, they share electrons in a covalent bond — both nuclei attract the shared electron pair, lowering the potential energy of the system. The resulting shared pair occupies the space between the nuclei.

#### Bond Order, Length, and Energy
| Bond type | Example | Bond length | Bond energy |
|-----------|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Single (order 1) | C−C | 154 pm | 347 kJ/mol |
| Double (order 2) | C=C | 134 pm | 614 kJ/mol |
| Triple (order 3) | C≡C | 120 pm | 839 kJ/mol |

Higher bond order → shorter bond, stronger bond (higher energy to break). Bond energy is always positive (energy input required to break a bond).

#### Polar vs. Nonpolar Covalent Bonds
If both atoms are identical (H₂, Cl₂): electrons shared equally → nonpolar covalent. If atoms differ in electronegativity (ΔEN 0.4–1.7): electrons pulled toward the more electronegative atom → polar covalent bond. The more electronegative atom has partial negative charge (δ−); the other has partial positive charge (δ+).

#### Octet Rule
Most atoms form bonds to achieve 8 electrons in their valence shell (noble gas configuration). Hydrogen is an exception: seeks 2 electrons (helium configuration). Exceptions to the octet rule also include electron-deficient compounds (BF₃, 6 electrons on B) and expanded octets (PCl₅, SF₆ — period 3+ elements can use d orbitals).

#### Review Questions
1. Why is a C=C double bond shorter than a C−C single bond?
2. Classify each bond as ionic, polar covalent, or nonpolar covalent: H−F, O=O, Mg−O, N−Cl.
3. Boron trifluoride (BF₃) violates the octet rule. Draw its Lewis structure and identify the "deficiency."

## Subtopic: Lewis Structures, Formal Charge & Resonance

### Drawing Lewis Structures
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** drawing-lewis-structures
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** Lewis structure · valence electrons · octet rule · lone pairs
**Summary:** Lewis structures show how valence electrons are distributed in molecules — as bonding pairs between atoms and lone pairs on atoms.

#### Steps to Draw a Lewis Structure
1. Count total valence electrons (sum over all atoms; add 1e⁻ per negative charge; subtract 1e⁻ per positive charge).
2. Draw the skeleton: least electronegative atom is usually central (H is always terminal); connect with single bonds.
3. Complete octets on terminal atoms with lone pairs first.
4. Place remaining electrons on the central atom.
5. If central atom has fewer than 8 electrons, form multiple bonds (move lone pairs from terminal atoms to form double/triple bonds).

#### Worked Example: SO₂
Valence electrons: S(6) + O(6) + O(6) = 18. Skeleton: O−S−O. Place single bonds (use 4e⁻, 14 remaining). Complete octets on O: use 12e⁻ (3 pairs each, 2e⁻ left). Place 2e⁻ on S → S has only 6 electrons; move one lone pair from one O to form S=O double bond → both O octets satisfied, S has 8.

#### Worked Example: CO₂
Valence e⁻: C(4) + 2×O(6) = 16. Single bonds O−C−O use 4e⁻, 12 remain. Each O gets 3 lone pairs (6e⁻ each) → uses 12. C has 4 electrons (2 from each bond) — that's an octet ✓. Wait: 2 single bonds give C only 4 electrons. Form double bonds: O=C=O → C has 4 shared pairs = 8 ✓. Total e⁻: 4 (double bonds × 2) + 4 (4 lone pairs on each O) × 2 = 8 + 8 = 16 ✓.

#### Review Questions
1. Draw the Lewis structure for NH₃. Count total valence electrons and identify the lone pair on nitrogen.
2. Draw the Lewis structure for H₂O₂. How many lone pairs are in the molecule?
3. What does it mean when a central atom has an "expanded octet"? Give an example.

### Formal Charge and Resonance
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** formal-charge-resonance
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** formal charge · resonance structures · resonance hybrid · best Lewis structure
**Summary:** Formal charge identifies the most stable Lewis structure; resonance structures describe molecules whose bonds are delocalized.

#### Formal Charge Formula
Formal charge = (valence e⁻ of neutral atom) − (lone pair e⁻) − ½(bonding e⁻). The best Lewis structure minimizes formal charges (closest to zero) and places negative formal charges on more electronegative atoms.

#### Worked Example: SO₄²⁻
Two valid Lewis structures exist for SO₄²⁻: one with all single bonds (formal charge on S = 6 − 0 − ½(8) = +2; each O = 6 − 6 − ½(2) = −1; sum = +2 + 4(−1) = −2 ✓) and one with two S=O double bonds and two S−O single bonds (reduces formal charges: S formal charge = +2 − 2 = 0 depending on arrangement). The structure with formal charges closer to zero is preferred.

#### Resonance Structures
Some molecules cannot be represented by a single Lewis structure. Ozone (O₃): the central O is bonded to one terminal O by single bond and one by double bond. But experimental data shows both O−O bonds are identical length (128 pm, between single 148 pm and double 121 pm). Solution: draw both structures as resonance contributors; the real molecule is the **resonance hybrid** — a blend of both structures. The bonds are delocalized across all contributing structures.

#### Benzene: The Classic Resonance Molecule
Benzene (C₆H₆) has two resonance structures (alternating single and double bonds in the ring). The hybrid has all equivalent C−C bonds (140 pm, between single and double), and the π electrons are delocalized around the entire ring, giving benzene exceptional stability.

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate the formal charge on nitrogen in NO₃⁻ if N has one double bond to O, one single bond to O, and one single bond to O (each terminal O has 3 lone pairs).
2. Draw two resonance structures for NO₂⁻. Explain what the resonance hybrid predicts about the N−O bond lengths.
3. Why do we say resonance structures are "contributors" rather than real structures that the molecule "switches between"?

### Lewis Structures Application: Complex Molecules
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** lewis-structures-complex-application
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** expanded octet · formal charge optimization · resonance in practice
**Summary:** Apply Lewis structure rules to molecules with expanded octets, formal charges, and resonance to interpret real chemical behavior.

#### Expanded Octet Species
Period 3+ elements (P, S, Cl, etc.) can have more than 8 electrons around them because they have accessible d orbitals. PCl₅: P has 5 bonds → 10 electrons (expanded octet). SF₆: S has 6 bonds → 12 electrons. XeF₄: Xe has 4 bonds + 2 lone pairs → 12 electrons. For these species, the octet rule must be abandoned.

#### Choosing the Best Structure for SO₃
SO₃ has multiple valid Lewis structures. Structure 1: three S−O single bonds (S formal charge = +3−0−1.5 = wait, formal charge on S = 6 − 0 − ½(6) = +3; on each O: 6−6−1 = −1; net = +3 + (−3) = 0). Structure 2: two S−O single bonds and one S=O double bond — reduces formal charges. Structure 3: three S=O double bonds (S has 12 electrons, all formal charges = 0). Experimental data: all S−O bonds equal (142 pm) → resonance hybrid of all contributing structures (primarily 3-double-bond character when expanded octet is allowed).

#### Nitrate Ion Formal Charge
NO₃⁻: N has one double bond, two single bonds; each O has lone pairs. Best structure: each resonance form has one N=O (formal charge on N = 5−0−4 = +1 if N has 8e⁻), and two N−O single bonds. The three equivalent resonance structures average to 1⅓ bond order for each N−O bond.

#### Review Questions
1. Draw the Lewis structure for XeF₄, including any lone pairs on Xe. Does Xe obey the octet rule?
2. Draw the three resonance structures for NO₃⁻ and determine the N−O bond order.
3. For SO₂, draw the two resonance structures, calculate formal charges for each, and describe the expected S−O bond lengths relative to pure single and double bonds.

## Subtopic: VSEPR Theory & Molecular Geometry

### VSEPR: Predicting Molecular Shapes
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** vsepr-predicting-shapes
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** VSEPR theory · electron domain · molecular geometry · electron geometry
**Summary:** VSEPR theory uses electron domain repulsion to predict the three-dimensional shape of molecules.

#### The VSEPR Principle
Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR): electron domains (bonding pairs AND lone pairs) around a central atom repel each other and arrange to maximize their distance. This determines the geometry of the electron domains (electron geometry) and the arrangement of atoms (molecular geometry).

#### Electron Geometry vs. Molecular Geometry
Electron geometry includes lone pairs; molecular geometry describes only where the atoms are. With 2 domains: linear (180°). With 3: trigonal planar (120°). With 4: tetrahedral (109.5°). With 5: trigonal bipyramidal (90°/120°). With 6: octahedral (90°).

#### Lone Pairs Change the Shape
Lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs (greater repulsion). For 4 electron domains: 0 lone pairs → tetrahedral (CH₄, 109.5°); 1 lone pair → trigonal pyramidal (NH₃, ~107°); 2 lone pairs → bent (H₂O, ~104.5°). The molecular geometry changes even though the electron geometry is still tetrahedral.

| 2 | 1 | Trig. planar | Bent | SO₂ |
| 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | Tetrahedral | CH₄ |
| 3 | 1 | Tetrahedral | Trig. pyramidal | NH₃ |
| 2 | 2 | Tetrahedral | Bent | H₂O |

| 4 | 1 | Trig. bipyramidal | See-saw | SF₄ |
| 6 | 0 | Octahedral | Octahedral | SF₆ |
| 4 | 2 | Octahedral | Square planar | XeF₄ |

### Molecular Polarity from VSEPR
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** molecular-polarity-vsepr
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** dipole moment · polar molecule · vector addition · symmetry
**Summary:** A molecule is polar if it has polar bonds arranged asymmetrically; symmetric arrangements of polar bonds produce nonpolar molecules.

#### From Bond Polarity to Molecular Polarity
Each polar bond is a vector (points from δ+ to δ−). Molecular polarity = vector sum of all bond dipoles. If the vectors cancel completely → nonpolar molecule (even if bonds are polar). If vectors don't cancel → polar molecule with a net dipole moment (μ, measured in Debye, D).

#### Symmetric Nonpolar Molecules with Polar Bonds
CO₂ (linear): two C=O dipoles point in opposite directions → cancel → nonpolar (μ = 0).
BF₃ (trigonal planar): three B−F dipoles at 120° → cancel → nonpolar.
CCl₄ (tetrahedral): four C−Cl dipoles at 109.5° → cancel symmetrically → nonpolar.
SF₆ (octahedral): six S−F dipoles → cancel → nonpolar.

#### Asymmetric Polar Molecules
NH₃ (trigonal pyramidal): three N−H dipoles point down-and-out + lone pair points up → net dipole upward → polar (μ = 1.47 D).
H₂O (bent): two O−H dipoles + two lone pairs → net dipole toward oxygen → polar (μ = 1.85 D, highest of common molecules).
SO₂ (bent): two S−O dipoles don't cancel → polar.

#### Review Questions

2. Predict whether each molecule is polar or nonpolar: CHCl₃, BCl₃, H₂S, PCl₅.
3. Which would you expect to have the higher boiling point: CCl₄ or CHCl₃? Explain using polarity.

## Subtopic: Hybridization

### sp, sp², and sp³ Hybridization
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** hybridization-sp-sp2-sp3
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** hybridization · sp · sp² · sp³ · sigma bond · pi bond
**Summary:** Hybridization explains how atomic orbitals mix to form bonds; sp, sp², and sp³ hybridization correspond to linear, trigonal planar, and tetrahedral geometries respectively.

#### Why Hybridization?
Carbon's ground-state configuration is [He]2s²2p². It has two unpaired p electrons — so it should form only 2 bonds. But methane (CH₄) has 4 equivalent C−H bonds. Hybridization resolves this: the 2s and all three 2p orbitals combine to form four equivalent sp³ hybrid orbitals, each forming one σ bond.

#### sp³ Hybridization
1 s + 3 p orbitals → 4 sp³ hybrids; tetrahedral geometry, 109.5°. All 4 bonds are equivalent σ bonds. Examples: CH₄, NH₃, H₂O (lone pairs occupy sp³ orbitals).

#### sp² Hybridization
1 s + 2 p orbitals → 3 sp² hybrids; trigonal planar, 120°; one unhybridized p orbital perpendicular to the plane forms a π bond with a neighboring atom. Examples: C₂H₄ (ethylene), BF₃, SO₃. Double bond = 1 σ (sp²–sp²) + 1 π (p–p).

#### sp Hybridization
1 s + 1 p orbital → 2 sp hybrids; linear, 180°; two unhybridized p orbitals form two π bonds. Examples: C₂H₂ (acetylene), CO₂, BeCl₂. Triple bond = 1 σ + 2 π.

#### Hybridization Shortcut
Count electron domains around central atom: 2 domains → sp; 3 domains → sp²; 4 domains → sp³; 5 domains → sp³d; 6 domains → sp³d².

#### Review Questions

2. Why does a carbon atom in a C=C double bond have sp² hybridization rather than sp³?
3. Describe the bonding in acetylene (HC≡CH): what types of bonds are present and what hybridization does each C have?

### Sigma and Pi Bonds in Hybridization
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** sigma-pi-bonds-hybridization
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** sigma bond · pi bond · bond rotation · conjugation
**Summary:** All bonds have one sigma bond; double bonds add a pi bond; triple bonds add two pi bonds. Pi bonds restrict rotation and enable delocalization.

#### Sigma Bonds
Sigma (σ) bonds: electron density concentrated directly between the two nuclei (head-on overlap). Every bond (single, double, triple) contains exactly one σ bond. σ bonds allow free rotation around the bond axis (in single bonds), because the electron density is symmetric.

#### Pi Bonds
Pi (π) bonds: electron density concentrated above and below the bond axis (side-on p orbital overlap). Double bond = 1σ + 1π. Triple bond = 1σ + 2π. π bonds do NOT allow free rotation — rotating breaks the side-on p orbital overlap, requiring energy. This is why cis-trans isomers exist in alkenes (C=C) but not in alkanes (C−C).

#### Counting σ and π Bonds
N₂ (triple bond): 1σ + 2π.
Ethylene C₂H₄: C=C (1σ+1π) + 4 C−H bonds (4σ) = 5σ + 1π total.
Benzene C₆H₆: 6 C−C bonds (each 1σ) + 6 C−H bonds (6σ) + 3 π bonds (delocalized) = 12σ + 3π.

#### Review Questions

3. A molecule has 2 carbon atoms connected by a triple bond, with one hydrogen on each carbon. Identify the hybridization of each carbon and count all σ and π bonds.

## Subtopic: Intermolecular Forces

### Types of Intermolecular Forces
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** intermolecular-forces-types
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** London dispersion · dipole-dipole · hydrogen bonding · IMF strength
**Summary:** Three key intermolecular forces — London dispersion, dipole-dipole, and hydrogen bonding — explain the physical properties of all substances.

#### London Dispersion Forces (LDF)
Present in ALL substances (polar and nonpolar). Arise from temporary induced dipoles caused by electron cloud fluctuations. Strength increases with molar mass (more electrons → more polarizable → stronger LDF). Strongest type for large nonpolar molecules (like I₂, large hydrocarbons). Only force in noble gases, nonpolar molecules (N₂, CH₄, Cl₂).

#### Dipole-Dipole Forces
Present in polar molecules. Arise from permanent dipoles attracting each other (δ+ end of one molecule attracted to δ− end of another). Stronger than LDF for similar molar mass. Example: HCl, SO₂, NH₃ (without H-bonding criterion met separately).

#### Hydrogen Bonding
Special, extra-strong dipole-dipole force when H is bonded directly to F, O, or N (which are highly electronegative and small). The H (partially positive) is attracted to a lone pair on F, O, or N of a neighboring molecule. Strength: 5–30 kJ/mol (much stronger than regular dipole-dipole). Examples: H₂O (strongest, two H-bond donors + two lone-pair acceptors), HF, NH₃, DNA base pairs.

#### Ranking IMF Strength
LDF < dipole-dipole < hydrogen bonding (roughly). But a large nonpolar molecule (octane, C₈H₁₈) may have stronger LDF than a small polar molecule (HF) due to size/polarizability. Context matters — compare similar-sized molecules: for roughly equal molecular weight, H-bond > dipole-dipole > LDF.

#### Review Questions
1. Which intermolecular force is present in ALL molecules regardless of polarity?
2. Explain why HF has a higher boiling point than HCl, even though HCl is larger.
3. List all IMFs present in each substance: CH₃OH, CO₂, NH₃, CCl₄.

### IMF and Physical Properties
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** imf-physical-properties
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** boiling point · viscosity · surface tension · vapor pressure
**Summary:** Stronger IMFs produce higher boiling points, lower vapor pressures, greater viscosity, and higher surface tension.

#### Boiling Point Trends
More energy is required to vaporize liquids with stronger IMFs (must overcome attractions between molecules). For nonpolar molecules increasing in size: CH₄ (−161°C) < C₂H₆ (−89°C) < C₄H₁₀ (−1°C) < C₈H₁₈ (126°C) — increasing LDF with mass. For similar-mass molecules: CH₄ (nonpolar, −161°C) vs NH₃ (H-bonding, −33°C) — NH₃ much higher BP despite lower mass.

#### Group 16 Hydrides Anomaly
Expected trend by mass: H₂S < H₂Se < H₂Te (increasing LDF → increasing BP). But H₂O breaks the trend massively (BP = 100°C vs predicted ~−80°C) because of strong H-bonding network. Same anomaly in Group 15 (NH₃) and Group 17 (HF).

#### Viscosity and Surface Tension
Higher IMF → higher viscosity (resistance to flow) and higher surface tension (energy per unit area of surface). Water's high surface tension (allows insects to walk on water) and high viscosity relative to its small size are due to H-bonding. Glycerol (three OH groups) has even higher viscosity than water.

#### Vapor Pressure
Vapor pressure = pressure of vapor above a liquid at equilibrium. Stronger IMFs → fewer molecules escape to vapor → lower vapor pressure → higher normal boiling point. Ether (weak LDF) has high vapor pressure; water (strong H-bonding) has low vapor pressure.
