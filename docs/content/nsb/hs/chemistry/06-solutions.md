## Subtopic: Solution Formation & Solubility

### What Makes a Solution
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** solution-definition
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** solute · solvent · homogeneous mixture · dissolution · aqueous solutions
**Summary:** A solution is a homogeneous mixture where one substance dissolves completely in another.

#### Definition and Components
A solution consists of a solute (the substance being dissolved) and a solvent (the substance doing the dissolving). Saltwater is a solution: salt is the solute, water is the solvent. Most common solutions use water as the solvent (aqueous solutions). The solute dissolves at the molecular or ionic level—individual particles of solute are evenly distributed throughout the solvent, creating a homogeneous mixture (uniform appearance and properties everywhere).

#### Dissolution Process
When an ionic compound like NaCl dissolves in water, the polar water molecules surround and separate the ions. Hydration occurs—water molecules orient around each ion with partial charges facing the opposite charge on the ion. This breaks the crystal lattice and allows ions to move freely throughout the solution.

#### Saturated vs. Unsaturated Solutions
An unsaturated solution has less solute than could dissolve at that temperature. Adding more solute will cause it to dissolve. A saturated solution contains the maximum amount of solute at a given temperature—if you add more solute, it won't dissolve; it settles as a precipitate or solid.

#### Factors Affecting Solubility
- **Temperature**: Most salts dissolve more as temperature increases (endothermic dissolution). Gases dissolve less at higher temperatures.
- **Pressure**: Affects gas solubility significantly (Henry's Law); affects liquids and solids minimally.
- **Polarity**: "Like dissolves like"—polar solvents dissolve polar and ionic solutes; nonpolar solvents dissolve nonpolar solutes.

#### Review Questions
1. In a salt water solution, which is the solute and which is the solvent?
2. Why does sugar dissolve better in hot water than cold water?
3. What is the difference between a saturated and unsaturated solution?

### Concentration Measurements: Molarity
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** molarity-definition
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** molarity · molar concentration · moles per liter · solution preparation
**Summary:** Molarity (M) is the most common way to express solution concentration: moles of solute per liter of solution.

#### Molarity Formula
Molarity (M) = moles of solute / liters of solution. Example: If 0.5 mol of NaCl is dissolved to make 1.0 L of total solution, molarity = 0.5 M. A 1.0 M solution contains 1 mole of solute dissolved in enough solvent to make 1 liter total volume.

#### Preparing a Molarity Solution
To prepare a 0.50 M solution of CaCl₂: Calculate mass needed: 0.50 mol × 110.98 g/mol = 55.5 g. Dissolve 55.5 g of CaCl₂ in a beaker with some water. Transfer to a 1 L volumetric flask and rinse the beaker. Add water to bring the level to the 1 L mark on the flask. Stopper and mix thoroughly.

#### Interpreting Molarity
A 2 M solution is twice as concentrated as 1 M. It contains twice as many moles of solute per liter. The label "0.1 M HCl" means 0.1 mol of HCl per 1 L of solution—or equivalently, 0.05 mol per 500 mL.

#### Advantage Over Other Units
Molarity is used in chemistry because it directly relates to mole ratios in balanced equations. If a reaction requires a 2:1 mole ratio of reactants, and both are in solution, you can use molarity to find the volume ratio: V₁M₁ = n₁ moles and V₂M₂ = n₂ moles. Setting n₁:n₂ = 2:1 solves for volume ratio.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the molarity of a solution containing 2.0 mol NaCl in 500 mL of solution?
2. How many moles of H₂SO₄ are in 250 mL of 0.40 M H₂SO₄?
3. To prepare 100 mL of 1.5 M KCl, how many grams of KCl do you need?

### Concentration Measurements: Molality and Mass Percent
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** molality-mass-percent
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** molality · mass percent · molarity vs. molality · colligative properties
**Summary:** Molality and mass percent are alternative ways to express concentration, independent of temperature.

#### Molality Definition
Molality (m) = moles of solute / kilograms of solvent. Example: 0.5 mol of NaCl dissolved in 1.0 kg of water gives 0.5 m NaCl. Unlike molarity (which depends on solution volume), molality depends only on mass—it doesn't change with temperature.

#### Mass Percent Definition
Mass percent = (mass of solute / mass of solution) × 100%. For 10 g of NaCl dissolved in 90 g of water (100 g total solution), mass percent = (10 / 100) × 100% = 10%. A 10% NaCl solution contains 10 g NaCl per 100 g solution.

#### Why Molality Matters
Molality is used for colligative properties (properties that depend on particle count, not identity)—freezing point depression, boiling point elevation, osmotic pressure. These properties are proportional to molality because they depend on the number of solute particles relative to solvent mass, not solvent volume.

#### Worked Example: Converting Between Units
A 0.50 M NaCl solution has a density of 1.02 g/mL. What is its molality? In 1 L (1000 mL) of solution: moles of NaCl = 0.50 mol. Mass of solution = 1000 mL × 1.02 g/mL = 1020 g. Mass of NaCl = 0.50 mol × 58.5 g/mol = 29.25 g. Mass of water = 1020 - 29.25 = 990.75 g = 0.99075 kg. Molality = 0.50 mol / 0.99075 kg = 0.505 m.

#### Review Questions
1. What is the molality of a solution containing 3.0 mol of ethanol (C₂H₅OH) dissolved in 5.0 kg of water?
2. Calculate the mass percent of a 12 g salt in 88 g water.
3. A 0.25 M solution has a density of 1.01 g/mL. Is it more or less concentrated in molality than molarity? (Hint: compare the two values.)

### Application: Dilution and Mixing Solutions
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** dilution-calculations
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** dilution equation · M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ · serial dilutions · stock solutions
**Summary:** Diluting a solution decreases concentration; the dilution equation relates initial and final concentrations and volumes.

#### Dilution Equation
When you add solvent to a solution, the number of moles of solute stays the same, but volume increases. moles = M × V (constant). So M₁V₁ = M₂V₂. If you dilute a 2.0 M solution from 100 mL to 500 mL: (2.0 M)(100 mL) = (M₂)(500 mL), so M₂ = 0.40 M.

#### Worked Example: Diluting Concentrated Acid
You need 500 mL of 0.10 M HCl. You have 12 M HCl (concentrated). Use the dilution equation: (12 M)(V₁) = (0.10 M)(500 mL), so V₁ = 4.2 mL. Dilute 4.2 mL of concentrated HCl to 500 mL with water (slowly, with care—mixing concentrated acid is exothermic!).

#### Serial Dilutions
Preparing very dilute solutions often requires serial dilutions. Example: prepare 10⁻⁶ M solution from 1 M stock. First dilution: 1:100 → 0.01 M. Second dilution: take 0.01 M and dilute 1:100 → 10⁻⁴ M. Third dilution: 1:100 → 10⁻⁶ M. Each step is a factor of 100 dilution.

#### Mixing Solutions of Different Concentrations
If you mix V₁ liters of M₁ solution with V₂ liters of M₂ solution, the final concentration is: M_final = (M₁V₁ + M₂V₂) / (V₁ + V₂). Example: Mix 200 mL of 0.50 M NaCl with 300 mL of 0.20 M NaCl. M_final = (0.50 × 0.2 + 0.20 × 0.3) / 0.5 = (0.10 + 0.06) / 0.5 = 0.32 M.

#### Review Questions
1. How would you prepare 250 mL of 0.15 M H₂SO₄ from 18 M H₂SO₄?
2. If you mix 150 mL of 0.40 M KCl with 100 mL of 0.60 M KCl, what is the final concentration?
3. You need a 10⁻⁵ M solution. You have 0.01 M stock. Describe a two-step serial dilution to achieve this.

### Application: Titration and Standardization
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** titration-standardization
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** titration · analyte · titrant · equivalence point · molarity determination
**Summary:** Titration is a precise method to determine the concentration of a solution by reaction with a standard solution.

#### Titration Setup
A known concentration (titrant, in a burette) is slowly added to an unknown concentration solution (analyte, in a flask) until the reaction is complete (equivalence point), indicated by a color change (indicator). At equivalence point: moles of titrant = moles of analyte (from stoichiometry).

#### Acid-Base Titration Example
Titrate 20.0 mL of unknown HCl with 0.100 M NaOH. The indicator (phenolphthalein) turns pink at equivalence point when 18.5 mL of NaOH is added. Moles of NaOH = 0.0185 L × 0.100 M = 0.00185 mol. From HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O (1:1 ratio), moles of HCl = 0.00185 mol. Molarity of HCl = 0.00185 mol / 0.0200 L = 0.0925 M.

#### Polyprotic Acid Titration
If titrating a diprotic acid (like H₂SO₄), the stoichiometry is different. H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O. If 25 mL of 0.50 M H₂SO₄ requires 50 mL of NaOH: (0.50 M)(25 mL) × (2 mol NaOH / 1 mol H₂SO₄) = 25 mmol NaOH needed. Molarity of NaOH = 25 mmol / 50 mL = 0.50 M. Check: (0.50 M)(50 mL) = 25 mmol ✓.

#### Standardization
Many reagents (like HCl) aren't exactly the molarity printed on the bottle due to evaporation, impurities, etc. To standardize: titrate a primary standard (a pure, known compound) of the reagent's counterpart. Example: titrate NaOH with pure oxalic acid (H₂C₂O₄) to determine NaOH's exact molarity.

#### Review Questions
1. In an acid-base titration, 25.0 mL of unknown HNO₃ requires 30.0 mL of 0.125 M KOH. What is the molarity of HNO₃?
2. A 50 mL sample of vinegar (acetic acid) requires 40 mL of 0.50 M NaOH to neutralize. What is the molarity of acetic acid?
3. For a diprotic acid (H₂A), 20 mL of unknown solution requires 35 mL of 0.20 M NaOH. Find the molarity of H₂A.

### Mixed Review: Solutions and Concentration
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** solutions-review
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** molarity · molality · dilution · titration · mass percent
**Summary:** Integrate concentration calculations and solution preparation.

#### Multi-Step Concentration Problem
Prepare a dilute solution and calculate its concentration. Step 1: You have 5.0 M NaCl. Step 2: Dilute 50 mL to 250 mL with water. Step 3: Final molarity = (5.0 M)(50 mL) / (250 mL) = 1.0 M. Step 4: This 1.0 M solution can be used in titrations or other applications.

#### Titration Problem
Unknown HCl is titrated with 0.10 M NaOH. 30 mL of HCl requires 25 mL NaOH. Molarity of HCl = (0.10 M × 25 mL) / 30 mL = 0.083 M.

#### Mixed Units Problem
A 0.50 M NaCl solution is diluted by adding 200 mL of water to 100 mL of original solution. New concentration: (0.50 M)(100 mL) / (300 mL) = 0.167 M. Also express as mass percent if density ≈ 1.00 g/mL: mass of NaCl = 0.167 mol × 58.5 g/mol = 9.8 g (in 300 mL). Mass percent = (9.8 g / 300 g) × 100% = 3.3%.

#### Review Questions
1. 2.0 M H₂SO₄ (100 mL) is diluted to 500 mL. What is the new concentration?
2. In a titration, 15 mL of unknown Ba(OH)₂ requires 30 mL of 0.20 M HCl. Find the molarity of Ba(OH)₂.
3. Calculate the mass percent of 0.75 M KBr in a solution with density 1.05 g/mL.

### Competition Extension: Concentration and Titration Mastery
**Type:** Competition Extension
**Slug:** concentration-competition
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** molarity · dilution · titration · stoichiometry · precision
**Summary:** Science Bowl questions on solutions test quick calculation and conceptual understanding.

#### Bowl-Style Toss-Up #1
"A 0.50 M solution of NaCl is diluted by adding water to double the volume. What is the new molarity?" (Answer: 0.25 M.)

#### Bowl-Style Toss-Up #2
"In a titration, 20 mL of unknown HCl requires 25 mL of 0.40 M NaOH. What is the molarity of the HCl?" (Answer: 0.50 M.)

#### Common Traps
- Forgetting that M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ applies to initial/final conditions, not mixing two solutions
- Confusing molarity with molality
- Making errors in stoichiometry during titration (forgetting 2:1 ratio for diprotic acids)
- Incorrect molar mass lookups

#### Speed Strategies
- Dilution always reduces molarity (if volume increases, M decreases)
- In titration, moles of titrant at equivalence point = moles of analyte (from stoichiometry)
- Cross-multiply for dilution: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂

#### Review Questions
1. For 10 points: How many mL of 6.0 M H₂SO₄ are needed to prepare 500 mL of 0.50 M H₂SO₄?
2. For 15 points: A 30 mL sample of unknown acid requires 24 mL of 0.15 M KOH. If the acid is diprotic, what is its molarity?
3. Bonus (20 points): Serial dilution from 2.0 M to 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ M. Minimum number of 1:100 dilution steps needed?
