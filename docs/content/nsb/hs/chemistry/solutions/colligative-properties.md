#### What Are Colligative Properties?
**Colligative properties** are physical properties of solutions that depend on the **number** of dissolved solute particles, not on their chemical identity. The four classic colligative properties are **vapor-pressure lowering**, **boiling-point elevation**, **freezing-point depression**, and **osmotic pressure**. Adding any nonvolatile solute to a pure solvent always raises the boiling point, lowers the freezing point, and generates osmotic pressure — regardless of what the solute is.

#### The van't Hoff Factor
The **van't Hoff factor** (**i**) accounts for the number of particles a solute produces per formula unit in solution. For a nonelectrolyte such as glucose, i = 1. For a strong electrolyte, i equals the number of ions: NaCl → i = 2 (Na+ and Cl−); CaCl2 → i = 3 (Ca2+ and 2 Cl−). In practice, ion pairing in concentrated solutions makes the effective i slightly lower than the ideal integer, but for competition problems use ideal values unless told otherwise.

#### Boiling-Point Elevation and Freezing-Point Depression
Dissolving a solute lowers solvent vapor pressure (Raoult's law), which means a higher temperature is required to reach atmospheric pressure — the boiling point rises. Conversely, the solute disrupts crystal-lattice formation, depressing the freezing point. The governing equations use molality (m) because mass-based concentration is temperature-independent:

- **Boiling-point elevation:** delta-Tb = i × Kb × m
- **Freezing-point depression:** delta-Tf = i × Kf × m

Here **Kb** and **Kf** are the **ebullioscopic** and **cryoscopic constants** of the solvent (for water: Kb = 0.512 °C·kg/mol, Kf = 1.86 °C·kg/mol). A solution of 1 m NaCl (i = 2) in water freezes at approximately −3.72 °C and boils at approximately 101.02 °C.

#### Osmotic Pressure
**Osmosis** is the net flow of solvent through a **semipermeable membrane** from a region of lower solute concentration to higher solute concentration. The pressure required to stop this flow is the **osmotic pressure** (Pi): **Pi = iMRT**, where M is molarity (mol/L), R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K), and T is absolute temperature in Kelvin. Osmotic pressure is extremely sensitive to solute concentration — even dilute solutions can generate substantial pressure — making it important in biological systems (cells, kidneys) and industrial processes (reverse osmosis desalination).

#### Applications and Common Examples
- **Antifreeze** (ethylene glycol in water): increases Kf × m depression to protect engines at sub-zero temperatures.
- **Salting roads**: NaCl or CaCl2 depresses the freezing point of water on pavement; CaCl2 is more effective per gram because i = 3.
- **Intravenous saline** (0.9% NaCl): matched to blood osmotic pressure (~7.7 atm at 37 °C) to prevent cell lysis or crenation.

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate the freezing point of a solution made by dissolving 90 g of glucose (MM = 180 g/mol, i = 1) in 500 g of water (Kf = 1.86 °C·kg/mol).
2. A 0.10 m aqueous CaCl2 solution has a measured boiling-point elevation of 0.49 °C. Using Kb = 0.512 °C·kg/mol, calculate the effective van't Hoff factor and explain why it differs from the ideal value of 3.
3. Why must molality (not molarity) be used in the boiling-point elevation and freezing-point depression equations, but molarity is used in the osmotic pressure equation?
