## Subtopic: History of Atomic Theory

### From Philosophy to Science: The Roots of Atomic Theory
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** history-atomic-theory-roots
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** Democritus · Dalton's atomic theory · law of definite proportions
**Summary:** Trace how atomic theory evolved from ancient Greek philosophy to Dalton's postulates grounded in quantitative chemistry.

#### Ancient Ideas About Matter
The idea that matter is made of tiny, indivisible particles dates to the ancient Greeks. Democritus (~400 BCE) coined the term *atomos* (uncuttable) to describe the smallest unit of matter. However, Aristotle rejected this view in favor of continuous matter made of earth, water, fire, and air — and Aristotle's authority dominated European thought for nearly 2,000 years.

#### Dalton's Atomic Theory (1803)
John Dalton gave atomism its scientific foundation by grounding it in measurable chemistry. His postulates: (1) all matter is made of indivisible atoms; (2) all atoms of a given element are identical in mass and properties; (3) compounds form when atoms of different elements combine in fixed, whole-number ratios; (4) chemical reactions rearrange atoms — they are neither created nor destroyed. These postulates explained the law of conservation of mass and the law of definite proportions.

#### What Dalton Got Right — and Wrong
Dalton was essentially correct about conservation and fixed ratios. He was wrong that atoms are indivisible (they contain subatomic particles) and wrong that all atoms of an element are identical (isotopes exist). But his model was powerful enough to guide chemistry for a century.

#### The Law of Definite Proportions
Joseph Proust's law of definite proportions (1799) states that a compound always contains the same elements in the same ratio by mass. Water is always 88.8% oxygen and 11.2% hydrogen by mass, regardless of source. This empirical regularity demanded an atomic explanation — which Dalton provided.

#### Why the History Matters for Bowl Competition
Understanding the sequence of atomic models helps you answer questions about who discovered what. Know: Dalton = postulates + definite proportions; Thomson = electrons; Rutherford = nucleus; Bohr = energy levels; Schrödinger = orbitals/wave functions.

#### Review Questions
1. State two postulates of Dalton's atomic theory and identify one that was later revised.
2. How does the law of definite proportions support an atomic model of matter?
3. Why did Aristotle's continuous-matter model persist so long, and what type of evidence eventually overturned it?

### Thomson's Discovery of the Electron
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** thomson-electron-discovery
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** cathode ray tube · electron · charge-to-mass ratio · plum pudding model
**Summary:** J.J. Thomson's cathode ray experiments established that atoms contain negatively charged subatomic particles called electrons.

#### Cathode Ray Tubes
In the late 1800s, physicists studied cathode rays — beams that travel from the negative electrode (cathode) to the positive electrode (anode) in evacuated glass tubes. The rays could be deflected by electric and magnetic fields. Thomson (1897) measured how much the beam deflected under known field strengths and calculated the charge-to-mass ratio (e/m) of the particles: about 1.76 × 10¹¹ C/kg.

#### What the Deflection Proved
The cathode rays deflected toward the positive plate, proving the particles carry negative charge. Thomson showed the e/m ratio was the same regardless of the cathode metal or the gas in the tube — proving the particles were a universal constituent of all matter, not specific to one element.

#### The Plum Pudding Model
Thomson proposed that atoms are spheres of diffuse positive charge with electrons embedded throughout — like raisins in a pudding. This model correctly incorporated electrons but incorrectly placed the positive charge throughout the atom rather than concentrated in a nucleus.

#### Millikan's Oil Drop Experiment
Robert Millikan (1909) measured the charge of a single electron (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C). Combined with Thomson's e/m ratio, this gave the electron's mass: ~9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg — about 1/1836 of a hydrogen atom.

#### Review Questions
1. What did Thomson observe in cathode ray experiments that indicated electrons are negatively charged?
2. Why did Thomson conclude electrons are universal rather than element-specific?
3. Describe the plum pudding model and explain why it was later rejected.

### Rutherford's Nuclear Model
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** rutherford-nuclear-model
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** gold foil experiment · nucleus · alpha particles · Geiger-Marsden experiment
**Summary:** Rutherford's gold foil experiment overturned the plum pudding model and revealed that almost all atomic mass is concentrated in a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.

#### The Gold Foil Experiment (1909)
Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, under Rutherford's direction, fired alpha particles (He²⁺ nuclei) at a thin gold foil. Fluorescent screens surrounded the foil to detect scattered particles. If the plum pudding model were correct, all alpha particles should pass straight through with minor deflections — the positive charge was too diffuse to scatter them significantly.

#### The Surprising Results
Most alpha particles did pass straight through. But about 1 in 8,000 bounced back at angles greater than 90°. Rutherford famously compared this to firing artillery shells at tissue paper and having them bounce back. This meant the positive charge and most of the atom's mass were concentrated in a tiny region — the nucleus.

#### The Nuclear Model
Rutherford proposed: atoms are mostly empty space; a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus contains most of the mass; electrons orbit the nucleus at relatively large distances. The nucleus diameter is ~10⁻¹⁵ m (femtometers); the atom's diameter is ~10⁻¹⁰ m (angstroms) — so the nucleus is roughly 1/100,000 the diameter of the atom.

#### Limitations of Rutherford's Model
Classical physics predicted that orbiting electrons should continuously radiate energy, spiral into the nucleus, and cause the atom to collapse in about 10⁻¹¹ seconds. This did not happen — Bohr's model resolved this by introducing quantized orbits.

#### Review Questions
1. What experimental result from the gold foil experiment could NOT be explained by the plum pudding model?
2. Calculate the ratio of nuclear diameter to atomic diameter and explain what this implies about atomic structure.
3. Why did Rutherford's nuclear model require a further revision by Bohr?

### The Periodic Table and Atomic Models Timeline: Application Lab
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** atomic-models-timeline-lab
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** model progression · experimental evidence · paradigm shift
**Summary:** Connect each atomic model to its experimental evidence, mapping how each experiment forced a specific revision.

#### Reading the Evidence Chain
Scientific models advance when experiments produce results that existing models cannot explain. In atomic theory, the chain is: Dalton's postulates → cathode rays (Thomson adds electrons) → gold foil (Rutherford adds nucleus) → hydrogen spectrum (Bohr adds quantized orbits) → wave-particle duality (Schrödinger adds orbitals). Each step was *forced* by specific data.

#### Worked Example: Which Model Explains It?
Experiment: Hydrogen gas emits light at specific wavelengths (656 nm red, 486 nm blue-green, 434 nm violet, etc.) — not a continuous spectrum. Which model explains this? Dalton's model: cannot explain it. Thomson's model: cannot explain it. Rutherford's model: cannot explain it. Bohr's model: YES — electrons occupy specific energy levels; emission occurs when electrons drop from higher to lower levels, releasing a photon of specific energy E = hf.

#### The Balmer Series
Balmer (1885) showed that visible hydrogen emission lines fit the formula 1/λ = R(1/4 − 1/n²) for n = 3, 4, 5, 6. Bohr's model explained this mathematically by quantizing electron energy as Eₙ = −13.6 eV/n².

#### Connecting Evidence to Models
Build the map: cathode rays + deflection → electron exists (Thomson); gold foil scattering → nucleus exists (Rutherford); discrete emission lines → quantized energy levels (Bohr); electron diffraction → wave-particle duality → orbitals, not orbits (Schrödinger/quantum model).

#### Review Questions
1. What specific experimental data motivated Bohr's revision of Rutherford's model?
2. Why does a hydrogen emission spectrum consist of discrete lines rather than a continuous rainbow?
3. In the Balmer series, which value of n produces the longest-wavelength visible emission line?

### Bohr vs. Quantum Model: Strengths and Limits
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** bohr-vs-quantum-model
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** Bohr model · quantum mechanical model · orbital vs. orbit · Heisenberg uncertainty
**Summary:** Compare what Bohr's model predicted correctly with what it failed to explain, and how the quantum mechanical model resolved those failures.

#### Bohr's Model: Successes
Bohr (1913) quantized electron orbits: electrons can only occupy orbits where angular momentum = nℏ (n = 1, 2, 3, …). This correctly predicted the hydrogen emission spectrum frequencies and calculated the ionization energy of hydrogen (13.6 eV). It also introduced the concept of the ground state (n=1) and excited states.

#### Bohr's Failures
The model failed for multi-electron atoms — it could not predict helium's spectrum. It also violated Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, which states Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2: you cannot simultaneously know both the exact position and momentum of an electron. Electrons cannot follow precise orbits because measuring their position disturbs their momentum.

#### The Quantum Mechanical Model
Schrödinger's wave equation (1926) describes electrons as wave functions ψ. The square of the wave function, |ψ|², gives the probability of finding an electron at any given location. Instead of orbits, electrons occupy *orbitals* — three-dimensional probability distributions around the nucleus. Orbitals have shapes: s (spherical), p (dumbbell), d (four-leaf clover), f (complex).

#### Key Conceptual Shift
The shift from Bohr to Schrödinger is a shift from certainty to probability. We cannot say "the electron is at this point in this orbit." We can only say "there is a 90% probability of finding the electron within this orbital boundary."

#### Review Questions
1. Name two predictions Bohr's model made correctly and two it failed to make.
2. State Heisenberg's uncertainty principle and explain why it rules out well-defined electron orbits.
3. What does |ψ|² represent in the quantum mechanical model of the atom?

### Comparing Atomic Models: Key Distinctions
**Type:** Mixed/Review
**Slug:** atomic-models-key-distinctions
**Estimated time:** 9 min
**Key concepts:** orbit vs. orbital · continuous vs. quantized · model limitations
**Summary:** Target the most commonly confused distinctions between atomic models for rapid review before competition.

#### Orbit vs. Orbital
An **orbit** (Bohr) is a defined circular or elliptical path — like a planet's trajectory. An **orbital** (quantum model) is a three-dimensional region of space where there is a high probability (usually 90%) of finding an electron. Orbitals have distinct shapes: s (sphere), p (dumbbell), d (cloverleaf). The key distinction: orbits are deterministic; orbitals are probabilistic.

#### Continuous vs. Quantized Energy
Classical physics allows any energy value for an orbiting object. Bohr quantized electron energies: Eₙ = −13.6 eV/n². The quantum model further specifies that energy is quantized and described by four quantum numbers. Only certain energy states are allowed — which is why emission spectra are discrete, not continuous.

#### What "Ground State" Means
Ground state (n=1 for hydrogen) is the lowest allowed energy level. Electrons occupy the lowest available energy state unless excited. Ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron from the ground state to n = ∞.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain the difference between an orbit and an orbital in your own words.
2. Why do atoms emit light at discrete wavelengths (rather than a continuous spectrum) when electrons return to lower energy levels?
3. The first ionization energy of hydrogen is 13.6 eV. Using Bohr's formula Eₙ = −13.6/n² eV, calculate the energy of the n=2 level and determine the energy of a photon emitted when an electron drops from n=2 to n=1.

## Subtopic: Atomic Structure (Protons, Neutrons, Electrons)

### Inside the Atom: Subatomic Particles
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** subatomic-particles-basics
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** proton · neutron · electron · atomic number · mass number
**Summary:** Master the properties of protons, neutrons, and electrons and learn how they define atomic identity and mass.

#### The Three Subatomic Particles
| Particle | Location | Charge | Mass |
|----------|----------|--------|------|
| Proton | Nucleus | +1 (1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C) | 1.673×10⁻²⁷ kg (~1 amu) |
| Neutron | Nucleus | 0 | 1.675×10⁻²⁷ kg (~1 amu) |
| Electron | Electron cloud | −1 (−1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C) | 9.109×10⁻³¹ kg (~0.00055 amu) |

Protons and neutrons together form the nucleus; electrons occupy the space around it.

#### Atomic Number and Mass Number
The **atomic number (Z)** = number of protons. It uniquely identifies the element — changing protons changes the element. The **mass number (A)** = protons + neutrons. The number of neutrons = A − Z. For a neutral atom, electrons = protons.

#### Notation: ᴬ_Z X
The standard nuclear notation writes the mass number as superscript and atomic number as subscript before the element symbol. ¹⁴_6 C has 6 protons, 8 neutrons, 6 electrons (if neutral). ²³_11 Na has 11 protons, 12 neutrons, 11 electrons.

#### Ions: Gaining and Losing Electrons
Ions form when atoms gain or lose electrons. Na⁺ has lost one electron (10 electrons, 11 protons). Cl⁻ has gained one electron (18 electrons, 17 protons). Ion charge = protons − electrons. The nucleus (protons and neutrons) does not change when ions form.

#### Review Questions
1. An atom has 17 protons, 18 neutrons, and 17 electrons. Give its element symbol, mass number, and nuclear notation.
2. What is the charge and approximate mass (in amu) of a neutron?
3. A Cl⁻ ion has 18 electrons. How many protons does it have, and what is the mass number if it has 18 neutrons?

### Isotopes: Same Element, Different Neutrons
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** isotopes-explained
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** isotope · atomic mass · weighted average · natural abundance
**Summary:** Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons; the periodic table's atomic mass is a weighted average of all naturally occurring isotopes.

#### What Are Isotopes?
Isotopes are atoms with the same atomic number (same element) but different mass numbers (different neutron counts). Carbon-12 (⁶¹²C) has 6 protons and 6 neutrons. Carbon-13 (⁶¹³C) has 6 protons and 7 neutrons. Carbon-14 (⁶¹⁴C) has 6 protons and 8 neutrons. All are carbon (6 protons) but differ in mass.

#### Natural Abundance and Atomic Mass
The atomic mass on the periodic table is not the mass of one isotope — it is a weighted average: atomic mass = Σ(mass of isotope × fractional abundance). For chlorine: ³⁵Cl (34.969 amu, 75.77%) and ³⁷Cl (36.966 amu, 24.23%). Atomic mass = (34.969)(0.7577) + (36.966)(0.2423) = 35.45 amu.

#### Why Isotopes Have the Same Chemistry
Chemical behavior is determined by electrons (and therefore by protons). Since isotopes have the same number of protons, they have the same electron configuration and virtually identical chemical properties. Physical properties like mass and nuclear stability can differ significantly.

#### Radioactive Isotopes
Some isotopes are unstable and decay by emitting radiation. Carbon-14 (half-life 5,730 years) decays by beta emission and is used in radiocarbon dating of organic material up to ~50,000 years old. Uranium-238 (half-life 4.5 billion years) decays by alpha emission.

#### Review Questions
1. Chlorine has two stable isotopes: ³⁵Cl (34.969 amu, 75.77%) and ³⁷Cl (36.966 amu, 24.23%). Calculate the atomic mass of chlorine.
2. How do isotopes differ from each other, and how are they similar?
3. Why do different isotopes of the same element have nearly identical chemical reactivity?

### Nuclear Notation and Ion Practice
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** nuclear-notation-ion-practice
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** nuclear notation · ions · isotope identification · electron counting
**Summary:** Develop fluency with nuclear notation and ion problems — the arithmetic of subatomic particles.

#### Reading Nuclear Notation Fluently
Given ⁵⁶_26 Fe²⁺: mass number A = 56, atomic number Z = 26, charge = 2+. Protons = 26 (iron). Neutrons = 56 − 26 = 30. Electrons = 26 − 2 = 24 (lost 2 electrons to form 2+ ion).

#### Writing Nuclear Notation
Given "a sulfur ion with 18 electrons and 16 neutrons": sulfur has Z = 16, so protons = 16. Electrons = 18 → charge = 16 − 18 = −2. Mass number = 16 + 16 = 32. Symbol: ³²_16 S²⁻.

#### Worked Examples
1. ⁶³_29 Cu⁺ → protons = 29, neutrons = 63 − 29 = 34, electrons = 29 − 1 = 28
2. ¹⁹_9 F⁻ → protons = 9, neutrons = 19 − 9 = 10, electrons = 9 + 1 = 10
3. ²⁷_13 Al³⁺ → protons = 13, neutrons = 27 − 13 = 14, electrons = 13 − 3 = 10

#### Common Trap: Mass Number vs. Atomic Mass
Mass number is always a whole number (count of nucleons). Atomic mass (on periodic table) is a decimal weighted average of all isotopes. If a question says "how many neutrons," use mass number − atomic number with integer mass number, not the decimal atomic mass.

#### Review Questions
1. An ion has 35 protons, 44 neutrons, and 36 electrons. Write its nuclear notation including charge.
2. What is the difference between mass number and atomic mass?
3. An element X has atomic number 26 and its most common isotope has mass number 56. Write the nuclear notation for the neutral atom and for the Fe³⁺ ion.

## Subtopic: Electron Configuration & Orbital Theory

### Quantum Numbers: The Address of Every Electron
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** quantum-numbers-explained
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** principal quantum number · angular momentum quantum number · magnetic quantum number · spin quantum number
**Summary:** Four quantum numbers uniquely specify the state of every electron in an atom; understanding them is the foundation of electron configuration.

#### The Four Quantum Numbers
Every electron in an atom is described by four quantum numbers:
1. **n (principal):** Energy level (shell). n = 1, 2, 3, … Higher n = higher energy, farther from nucleus.
2. **l (angular momentum):** Sublevel shape. l = 0 to n−1. l=0 → s, l=1 → p, l=2 → d, l=3 → f.
3. **mₗ (magnetic):** Orbital orientation. mₗ = −l to +l (2l+1 values). For l=1 (p): mₗ = −1, 0, +1 → three p orbitals.
4. **mₛ (spin):** Electron spin. mₛ = +½ or −½.

#### Pauli Exclusion Principle
No two electrons in the same atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers. Since two electrons in the same orbital have the same n, l, and mₗ, they must have opposite spins (+½ and −½). Each orbital holds at most two electrons.

#### Number of Orbitals and Electrons Per Level
- n=1: 1 sublevel (1s), 1 orbital, 2 electrons max
- n=2: 2 sublevels (2s, 2p), 4 orbitals (1+3), 8 electrons max
- n=3: 3 sublevels (3s, 3p, 3d), 9 orbitals (1+3+5), 18 electrons max
- General: n² orbitals, 2n² electrons per shell

#### Review Questions
1. What are the allowed values of l for n=4? List the corresponding sublevel names.
2. How many orbitals are in the 3d sublevel, and what are the allowed mₗ values?
3. State the Pauli exclusion principle and explain why each orbital holds at most two electrons.

### Writing Electron Configurations
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** writing-electron-configurations
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** aufbau principle · Hund's rule · electron configuration · noble gas shorthand
**Summary:** Apply the three rules — aufbau, Pauli exclusion, and Hund's rule — to write electron configurations for any element.

#### The Three Rules
1. **Aufbau principle:** Electrons fill the lowest available energy sublevel first. Filling order: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d. Remember: 4s fills before 3d.
2. **Pauli exclusion:** Each orbital holds at most 2 electrons with opposite spins.
3. **Hund's rule:** Within a sublevel, electrons occupy separate orbitals before pairing, all with the same spin (maximizes spin).

#### Writing Configurations
Oxygen (Z=8): 1s²2s²2p⁴ — the four 2p electrons fill: first three go one each into 2pₓ, 2p_y, 2p_z (Hund), then the fourth pairs with one.
Iron (Z=26): 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶4s²3d⁶ (note 4s fills before 3d)
Noble gas shorthand — iron: [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ (argon = 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶)

#### Exceptions: Cr and Cu
Chromium (Z=24) is [Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵ (not 4s² 3d⁴) — half-filled d sublevel is extra stable.
Copper (Z=29) is [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰ (not 4s² 3d⁹) — fully-filled d sublevel is extra stable.
These are the two most-tested exceptions at the high school level.

#### Review Questions
1. Write the full electron configuration for phosphorus (Z=15) and for iron (Z=26).
2. State Hund's rule and draw an orbital diagram showing how the three 2p electrons of nitrogen are arranged.
3. Why is chromium's electron configuration [Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵ rather than [Ar] 4s² 3d⁴?

### Electron Configuration: Application Problems
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** electron-configuration-application
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** ion electron configuration · paramagnetic vs. diamagnetic · valence electrons
**Summary:** Apply electron configuration rules to ions, predict magnetic properties, and count valence electrons.

#### Ion Configurations
When atoms form ions, electrons are added or removed. For cations, remove from the highest-energy sublevel first. Key rule: for transition metals, remove 4s electrons before 3d.
- Fe (Z=26): [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶
- Fe²⁺: remove 2 from 4s → [Ar] 3d⁶
- Fe³⁺: remove 2 from 4s and 1 from 3d → [Ar] 3d⁵
For anions, add to the next available orbital.
- O (Z=8): 1s²2s²2p⁴ → O²⁻: 1s²2s²2p⁶

#### Paramagnetic vs. Diamagnetic
Paramagnetic: atoms/ions with unpaired electrons are attracted to magnetic fields.
Diamagnetic: all electrons paired — not attracted to magnetic fields.
Fe³⁺ has [Ar]3d⁵ — five 3d electrons, all unpaired (Hund's rule): strongly paramagnetic.
Zn²⁺ has [Ar]3d¹⁰ — all paired: diamagnetic.

#### Valence Electrons
Valence electrons occupy the outermost principal energy level. For main-group elements, count electrons in the highest n shell. Na: [Ne]3s¹ → 1 valence electron. Cl: [Ne]3s²3p⁵ → 7 valence electrons. For transition metals, valence electrons include both the outermost s and d electrons.

## Subtopic: Periodic Trends

### Periodic Trends: Atomic Radius
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** periodic-trends-atomic-radius
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** atomic radius · shielding effect · effective nuclear charge · period/group trends
**Summary:** Atomic radius decreases across a period (left to right) and increases down a group — driven by nuclear charge and electron shielding.

#### Defining Atomic Radius
Atomic radius is approximately half the distance between two adjacent same-element atoms in a bonded compound. It is not a hard boundary (electron clouds are probabilistic), but measured values give useful trends. Units: picometers (pm) or angstroms (Å).

#### Trend Across a Period
Left to right across a period, atomic radius *decreases*. Reason: as Z increases, more protons pull electrons closer. Electrons are added to the same shell, so shielding from inner shells stays roughly constant. The increasing effective nuclear charge (Z_eff = Z − shielding) pulls the electron cloud inward.

#### Trend Down a Group
Top to bottom in a group, atomic radius *increases*. Reason: each new period adds another electron shell. The valence electrons are farther from the nucleus and are shielded by more inner-shell electrons, reducing Z_eff. Distance dominates over nuclear charge.

#### Effective Nuclear Charge (Z_eff)
Z_eff ≈ Z − S, where S is the shielding constant (Slater's rules). Inner-shell electrons shield nearly completely; same-shell electrons shield only partially. Na (Z=11, 1 valence e⁻, ~10 inner e⁻): Z_eff ≈ 1. Cl (Z=17, 7 valence e⁻, ~10 inner e⁻): Z_eff ≈ 7. Cl is much smaller than Na.

#### Review Questions
1. Why does atomic radius decrease across a period even though more electrons are added?
2. Place these in order of increasing atomic radius: Na, K, Cl, Ar.
3. What is effective nuclear charge, and how does it change across period 3?

### Periodic Trends: Ionization Energy and Electronegativity
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** periodic-trends-ie-en
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** first ionization energy · successive ionization energies · electronegativity · Pauling scale
**Summary:** Ionization energy and electronegativity increase across periods and decrease down groups, following the same logic as atomic radius but in reverse.

#### First Ionization Energy (IE₁)
IE₁ is the energy required to remove the outermost electron from a gaseous neutral atom: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻. Units: kJ/mol or eV. IE₁ generally increases across a period (stronger nuclear pull on valence electrons) and decreases down a group (valence electrons farther, more shielded).

#### Exceptions in IE₁
Two notable exceptions in period 2: (1) Be (1s²2s²) → B (1s²2s²2p¹): IE₁ of B is lower because 2p electrons are higher in energy and easier to remove than 2s. (2) N (2p³) → O (2p⁴): IE₁ of O is lower because in oxygen, one 2p orbital is doubly occupied — electron-electron repulsion makes that electron easier to remove.

#### Successive Ionization Energies
IE₂ > IE₁ (removing a second electron from an already positive ion is harder). When a large jump occurs between IEₙ and IEₙ₊₁, the (n+1)th electron is from an inner shell — this reveals the element's group. Na: IE₁ = 496 kJ/mol, IE₂ = 4,562 kJ/mol → big jump after 1 → 1 valence electron → Group 1.

#### Electronegativity
Electronegativity is the ability of a bonded atom to attract electrons toward itself (Pauling scale). F is the most electronegative element (4.0). Trend: increases across period, decreases down group. Noble gases typically not assigned values. Used to predict bond polarity: |ΔEN| > 1.7 → ionic; 0.4–1.7 → polar covalent; < 0.4 → nonpolar covalent.

#### Review Questions
1. Why is the first ionization energy of oxygen lower than that of nitrogen, despite oxygen having a higher atomic number?
2. An element has ionization energies: IE₁ = 578, IE₂ = 1,817, IE₃ = 2,745, IE₄ = 11,578 kJ/mol. Which group does it belong to?
3. Arrange F, Cl, Br, I in order of decreasing electronegativity.

### Applying Periodic Trends to Chemistry
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** periodic-trends-application
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** bond polarity · reactivity trends · periodic trend prediction
**Summary:** Use periodic trends to predict chemical reactivity, bond character, and element behavior.

#### Predicting Bond Polarity
The more electronegative atom in a bond carries partial negative charge (δ−). HF: EN(F)=4.0, EN(H)=2.1, ΔEN=1.9 → polar covalent, nearly ionic. HCl: ΔEN=0.9 → polar covalent. H₂: ΔEN=0 → nonpolar covalent. Bond dipoles point toward the more electronegative atom.

#### Reactivity of Alkali Metals
Group 1 metals become more reactive down the group because: lower IE₁ (easier to lose electron), larger atomic radius, greater shielding. Li barely reacts with water; Na reacts vigorously; K reacts violently; Cs and Fr react explosively. This is directly explained by IE₁ trend.

#### Reactivity of Halogens
Group 17 nonmetals become less reactive down the group: F₂ is the most powerful oxidizing agent; I₂ is the weakest among halogens. Higher electronegativity and smaller size (F) drive stronger electron-attraction. Fluorine can oxidize even noble gas compounds; iodine is used as an antiseptic.

#### Worked Prediction
Q: Between Li and Cs, which is more reactive with water? Why? A: Cs. Lower IE₁ (543 vs. 520 kJ/mol is wrong — Cs: 376 kJ/mol), larger atom, valence electron farther and more shielded → easier to lose → more reactive.

#### Review Questions
1. Which is more polar: HF or HCl? Explain using electronegativity values.
2. Why does potassium react more vigorously with water than sodium?
3. Arrange in order of increasing reactivity: F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂. Explain your reasoning.

### Radioactive Decay: Alpha, Beta, Gamma
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** radioactive-decay-types
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** alpha decay · beta decay · gamma radiation · nuclear equations · penetrating power
**Summary:** The three main types of radioactive decay differ in particle emitted, penetrating power, and effect on the nucleus.

#### Alpha Decay (α)
Alpha particles = ⁴₂He (helium-4 nucleus: 2 protons, 2 neutrons). Alpha decay: A decreases by 4, Z decreases by 2.
Example: ²³⁸₉₂U → ⁴₂He + ²³⁴₉₀Th
Alpha particles have high mass and charge, travel only a few centimeters in air, and are stopped by paper or skin (external exposure low risk; internal ingestion very dangerous).

#### Beta Decay (β⁻)
Beta particles = high-energy electrons (⁰₋₁e). In beta decay, a neutron converts to a proton + electron (+ antineutrino): n → p + e⁻ + ν̄. A stays same, Z increases by 1.
Example: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e
Beta particles penetrate farther than alpha — stopped by a few mm of aluminum.

#### Gamma Radiation (γ)
Gamma rays = high-energy electromagnetic radiation (no particle, no mass, no charge). Often accompany alpha or beta decay as nucleus releases excess energy. A and Z unchanged. Gamma rays are the most penetrating — require several cm of lead or meters of concrete to block.

#### Nuclear Equation Balancing
In any nuclear equation: sum of mass numbers on each side must be equal, and sum of atomic numbers on each side must be equal. Check: ²³⁸₉₂U → ⁴₂He + ²³⁴₉₀Th: mass 238 = 4 + 234 ✓; atomic number 92 = 2 + 90 ✓.

#### Review Questions
1. Write the nuclear equation for alpha decay of ²²⁶₈₈Ra.
2. Rank alpha, beta, and gamma radiation from least to most penetrating.
3. In beta decay, what subatomic particle is converted to what other particles?

### Half-Life and Radioactive Dating
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** half-life-radioactive-dating
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** half-life · decay constant · radiocarbon dating · uranium-lead dating
**Summary:** Half-life is the time for half a radioactive sample to decay; it enables dating of geological and archaeological samples.

#### Half-Life Formula
After n half-lives, the remaining fraction is (½)ⁿ. Amount remaining = N₀ × (½)^(t/t₁/₂). The decay constant λ = ln2/t₁/₂ ≈ 0.693/t₁/₂. Activity (decays per second, Becquerel) = λN.

#### Worked Example
Carbon-14 half-life = 5,730 years. A sample initially has 8,000 atoms of ¹⁴C. How many remain after 17,190 years?
t/t₁/₂ = 17,190/5,730 = 3 half-lives. Remaining = 8,000 × (½)³ = 8,000/8 = 1,000 atoms.

#### Radiocarbon Dating
Living organisms continuously exchange carbon with the environment, maintaining a constant ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio. At death, ¹⁴C is no longer replenished; it decays. Measuring the remaining ¹⁴C ratio gives age. Accurate to ~50,000 years (beyond that, ¹⁴C signal too small to measure reliably).

#### Uranium-Lead Dating
²³⁸U → ²⁰⁶Pb through a decay chain; half-life 4.47 billion years. Used to date ancient rocks and meteorites. The age of Earth (~4.54 billion years) was established using U-Pb dating of meteorites.

#### Review Questions
1. A radioactive isotope has a half-life of 24 days. What fraction remains after 96 days?
2. Why can radiocarbon dating not be used for samples older than ~50,000 years?
3. If a uranium-lead sample has equal numbers of ²³⁸U and ²⁰⁶Pb atoms, how old is the sample? (U-238 half-life = 4.47 × 10⁹ yr)

### Nuclear Fission, Fusion, and Binding Energy
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** nuclear-fission-fusion-binding-energy
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** mass defect · binding energy · E=mc² · fission · fusion
**Summary:** Nuclear reactions release energy through mass-energy conversion; fission splits heavy nuclei while fusion joins light ones.

#### Mass Defect and Binding Energy
The mass of a nucleus is always less than the sum of its constituent nucleons — this mass difference (Δm, "mass defect") is converted to binding energy: E = Δm × c². The binding energy per nucleon peaks at iron-56 (most stable nucleus). Elements lighter than Fe can release energy by fusion; elements heavier than Fe can release energy by fission.

#### Nuclear Fission
Fission splits a heavy nucleus (usually U-235 or Pu-239) when struck by a neutron, releasing energy + 2–3 neutrons + fission fragments. Chain reaction: those neutrons trigger more fissions. Controlled in nuclear reactors; uncontrolled in nuclear weapons. U.S. first achieved controlled fission in the Chicago Pile-1 reactor (Fermi, 1942).

#### Nuclear Fusion
Fusion joins light nuclei (typically isotopes of hydrogen — deuterium ²H and tritium ³H) at extremely high temperatures and pressures, releasing more energy per gram than fission. The sun is powered by the proton-proton chain fusion cycle. Fusion produces ⁴He + neutron. Commercial fusion power remains experimental (ITER; NIF achieved ignition in 2022).

#### E = mc² in Context
The energy released in nuclear reactions comes from mass converted to energy: a small Δm produces enormous E because c² = (3×10⁸ m/s)² = 9×10¹⁶ J/kg. Even a microgram of mass converts to ~9×10¹⁰ J — roughly the energy in 20 tons of TNT.

#### Review Questions
1. Define mass defect and explain its relationship to binding energy using E = mc².
2. Why does fission of uranium release energy, while fusion of hydrogen also releases energy? (Hint: consider the binding energy curve.)
3. Write the nuclear equation for the fission of U-235 struck by a neutron, producing Ba-141, Kr-92, and additional neutrons. Balance the equation.

#### Medical Applications of Radioisotopes
Technetium-99m (t₁/₂ = 6 hours, gamma emitter) is the most widely used medical radioisotope for diagnostic imaging — short half-life minimizes patient dose; gamma penetrates tissue for external detection. I-131 (beta emitter, t₁/₂ = 8 days) treats thyroid cancer by concentrating in thyroid tissue. PET scans use positron-emitting isotopes (¹⁸F in FDG glucose analog).

#### Decay Series
Heavy radioactive isotopes undergo chains of decays until reaching a stable isotope. U-238 decays through 14 steps (alpha and beta decays) to reach stable Pb-206. Each step has its own characteristic half-life, allowing radiometric dating using intermediate products.
