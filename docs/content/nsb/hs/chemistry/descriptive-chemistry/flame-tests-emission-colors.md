#### Electron Transitions and Photon Emission

When a metal salt is placed in a flame, thermal energy excites valence electrons to higher **energy levels**. As electrons fall back to lower orbitals, they release energy as **photons** with a wavelength given by E = hf = hc/lambda. Because energy levels are quantized and unique to each element, the emitted wavelengths — and thus colors — are **characteristic fingerprints** for each metal ion.

#### Key Flame Test Colors

Competition questions require memorizing these standard results:
- **Lithium (Li+):** crimson/carmine red
- **Sodium (Na+):** bright/persistent yellow (even trace amounts dominate)
- **Potassium (K+):** lilac/violet (often masked by Na+ contamination; observe through cobalt blue glass)
- **Calcium (Ca2+):** brick red / orange-red
- **Strontium (Sr2+):** bright red / crimson
- **Barium (Ba2+):** pale/apple green
- **Copper (Cu2+):** blue-green / verdigris

#### Connection to Atomic Emission Spectra

The colors observed in a flame test correspond to specific **atomic emission spectral lines**. Sodium's yellow results from the **sodium D-lines** at ~589 nm (3p → 3s transition). Hydrogen's visible series (the **Balmer series**) produces red (656 nm, n=3→2), blue-green (486 nm, n=4→2), and violet lines. High-energy (short wavelength) photons correspond to transitions dropping to lower principal quantum numbers with a larger energy gap: delta_E = -13.6 eV × (1/n_f^2 - 1/n_i^2) for hydrogen.

#### Identifying Metal Ions in Practice

In a flame test, a clean wire (platinum or nichrome) is dipped in the sample solution and held in the hottest part of a Bunsen burner flame. **Contamination by sodium is the most common error** — always clean the wire between tests and use a cobalt blue filter to distinguish potassium's lilac from sodium's yellow. Multiple ions can be confirmed by cross-referencing flame color with other qualitative tests (e.g., precipitate formation).

#### Review Questions
1. A metal chloride solution produces a bright yellow flame. Identify the metal ion and describe the specific electron transition responsible for the color.
2. Explain why potassium's flame color is sometimes observed through a cobalt blue glass filter, and what interference the filter removes.
3. Using the hydrogen energy-level equation delta_E = -13.6 eV × (1/n_f^2 - 1/n_i^2), calculate the energy (in eV) of the photon emitted in the n=3 to n=2 transition (Balmer H-alpha line).
