## Subtopic: Kinetic Molecular Theory

### The Kinetic Molecular Theory of Gases
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** kinetic-molecular-theory-gases
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** KMT postulates · ideal gas · Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution
**Summary:** The Kinetic Molecular Theory explains gas behavior using five postulates about the nature and motion of gas particles.

#### The Five Postulates
1. **Gases consist of tiny particles** (atoms or molecules) with negligible volume compared to the total volume occupied.
2. **No intermolecular forces** — gas particles neither attract nor repel each other between collisions.
3. **Particles are in constant, random motion** in straight lines until they collide.
4. **Collisions are perfectly elastic** — kinetic energy is conserved; no energy lost to friction.
5. **Average kinetic energy is proportional to absolute temperature**: KE_avg = (3/2)kT, where k is Boltzmann's constant and T is in Kelvin.

#### What These Postulates Explain
- **Pressure:** Results from particles colliding with container walls. More collisions or harder collisions = higher pressure.
- **Temperature:** A macroscopic measure of average molecular kinetic energy. Raise temperature → particles move faster → more forceful collisions.
- **Volume:** Gases expand to fill any container because particles move randomly and freely.

#### Ideal vs. Real Gases
KMT describes an **ideal gas** — a theoretical model. Real gases deviate at high pressure (volume of particles matters) and low temperature (intermolecular attractions matter). At room temperature and atmospheric pressure, most gases behave nearly ideally.

#### Maxwell-Boltzmann Distribution
Not all gas particles move at the same speed. The **Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution** shows a bell-shaped curve of speeds. The peak is the most probable speed; the average speed is slightly higher; the root-mean-square (rms) speed is highest of the three. Increasing temperature shifts the entire curve to higher speeds and broadens it.

#### Review Questions
1. State the postulate of KMT that explains why gas pressure increases when temperature increases.
2. Why do real gases deviate from ideal behavior at high pressure?
3. Two gases at the same temperature have the same average kinetic energy. If gas A has a higher molar mass than gas B, which moves faster on average?

### Effusion and Graham's Law
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** effusion-grahams-law
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** effusion · diffusion · Graham's Law
**Summary:** Graham's Law states that the rate of effusion of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its molar mass.

#### Effusion vs. Diffusion
**Effusion** is the escape of gas through a tiny pinhole into vacuum. **Diffusion** is the spreading of gas through another gas. Both are slower for heavier gas molecules because heavier molecules move more slowly at the same temperature.

#### Graham's Law
At constant temperature and pressure:
**rate₁/rate₂ = √(M₂/M₁)**

Where M is molar mass in g/mol and rate can be measured in time, distance, or relative amounts.

**Example:** How much faster does H₂ (M = 2 g/mol) effuse than O₂ (M = 32 g/mol)?
rate_H₂/rate_O₂ = √(32/2) = √16 = **4 times faster**

#### Identifying Unknown Gases
If an unknown gas effuses 1.5 times slower than N₂ (M = 28):
rate_N₂/rate_x = 1.5 = √(M_x/28)
1.5² = M_x/28 → M_x = 2.25 × 28 = **63 g/mol** (likely CuO, MW = 63.5 — or SO₂, MW = 64)

#### Practical Applications
- **Uranium enrichment:** U-235 and U-238 are separated as UF₆ gas. Graham's Law gives slightly different effusion rates: M(²³⁵UF₆) = 349, M(²³⁸UF₆) = 352. Thousands of effusion stages concentrate U-235.
- **Helium vs. air balloons:** Helium (M=4) effuses much faster than N₂ (M=28) through balloon rubber — why helium balloons deflate quickly.

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate the ratio of effusion rates of CO₂ (M=44) to H₂O (M=18).
2. A gas effuses at half the rate of O₂. What is its molar mass?
3. Explain why the effusion process is slower than the average speed of molecules might suggest.

### Temperature, Pressure, and Volume: KMT Predictions
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** kmt-predictions-tpv
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** KMT and gas laws · pressure-volume-temperature relationships · molecular interpretation
**Summary:** Using KMT to predict and explain how temperature, pressure, and volume changes affect gas behavior.

#### Pressure from KMT
Pressure = force per unit area = sum of all molecular collisions with walls. **Increasing temperature** → faster molecules → harder, more frequent wall collisions → higher pressure (if volume fixed). **Decreasing volume** → molecules reach walls more quickly → more collisions per second → higher pressure (if temperature fixed).

#### Worked Scenario 1: Container Compression
A sealed container of N₂ at 300 K and 1 atm is compressed to half its volume while held at 300 K.
- KMT prediction: same number of molecules in half the space → collision frequency doubles
- Gas law prediction: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ → (1)(V) = P₂(V/2) → P₂ = **2 atm** ✓

#### Worked Scenario 2: Heating at Constant Volume
The same container is now heated from 300 K to 600 K at constant volume.
- KMT: same number of molecules, same volume, but average speed increases by √(T₂/T₁) = √2 → force per collision and frequency both increase
- Gas law: P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂ → (2 atm)/(300 K) = P₂/(600 K) → P₂ = **4 atm** ✓

#### Real Gas Correction: Van der Waals
Real gases require corrections: **(P + a/V²)(V − b) = nRT**
- *a* corrects for intermolecular attractions (reduces pressure below ideal)
- *b* corrects for finite particle volume (reduces available volume)
High-*a* gases (NH₃, H₂O) deviate most at low temperature; high-pressure gases deviate most.

#### Review Questions
1. Using KMT, explain why the pressure of a gas in a rigid container increases when temperature increases.
2. Why does a real gas at high pressure occupy more volume than predicted by PV = nRT?
3. At very low temperatures, which postulate of KMT breaks down for water vapor? Why?

## Subtopic: Boyle's Law

### Boyle's Law: Pressure-Volume Inverse Relationship
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** boyles-law
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** Boyle's Law · P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ · inverse proportionality
**Summary:** Boyle's Law states that at constant temperature, pressure and volume of a gas are inversely proportional.

#### The Law
**P₁V₁ = P₂V₂** (at constant n and T)
Or equivalently: P ∝ 1/V → PV = constant. As pressure doubles, volume halves. A PV graph is a hyperbola; a P vs. 1/V graph is a straight line through the origin.

#### Historical Context
Robert Boyle (1662) discovered this using a J-shaped tube with trapped mercury. He measured pressure (height of mercury) and volume of trapped air and established the inverse relationship — one of the first quantitative gas laws.

#### Standard Calculations
**Example 1:** A gas occupies 2.00 L at 1.50 atm. Volume at 3.00 atm (T constant)?
P₁V₁ = P₂V₂: (1.50)(2.00) = (3.00)(V₂) → V₂ = **1.00 L** (makes sense — pressure doubled, volume halved)

**Example 2:** A balloon holds 5.0 L at sea level (1.0 atm). It rises to an altitude where P = 0.5 atm. New volume?
(1.0)(5.0) = (0.5)(V₂) → V₂ = **10.0 L** (pressure halved, volume doubled)

#### Graphical Interpretation
On a P-V graph (pressure on y-axis, volume on x-axis), isotherms (constant temperature lines) are hyperbolas. Higher temperature isotherms lie farther from the origin (same P → larger V at higher T).

#### Units — Consistency
Pressure and volume units must be consistent on both sides. Common units: atm, mmHg (torr), kPa (1 atm = 101.325 kPa = 760 mmHg). Volume: L, mL, m³. Never mix units within one calculation.

#### Review Questions
1. A gas at 4.0 atm occupies 3.5 L. What volume does it occupy at 1.0 atm, same temperature?
2. Explain in KMT terms why compressing a gas (reducing volume) increases its pressure.
3. A syringe holds 50 mL of air at 1 atm. The plunger is pushed until the pressure is 2.5 atm. What is the new volume?

### Boyle's Law Applications and Breathing
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** boyles-law-breathing
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** Boyle's Law in physiology · inhalation mechanism · pressure-volume breathing cycle
**Summary:** Boyle's Law explains how the diaphragm drives inhalation and exhalation through pressure-volume changes in the lungs.

#### The Breathing Mechanism
Your lungs do not expand on their own — they are pulled open by the diaphragm. When the diaphragm contracts and flattens, chest volume increases. By Boyle's Law, increased volume → decreased pressure. The lung pressure drops below atmospheric pressure, and air flows in (inhalation). When the diaphragm relaxes, chest volume decreases → pressure increases above atmospheric → air flows out (exhalation).

#### Numbers
At rest, lung volume changes by about 0.5 L per breath. Inhalation pressure drops approximately 1–3 mmHg below atmospheric (760 mmHg). This small pressure gradient is sufficient because the airway resistance is low.

#### Scuba Diving and Boyle's Law
At 10 m depth, water pressure = 2 atm total. Air breathed at depth is compressed to match. If a diver holds their breath while ascending, lung volume doubles as pressure halves (Boyle's Law) — potentially causing a **pulmonary embolism** (lung rupture). This is why divers are taught to breathe continuously and never hold their breath while ascending.

#### Pressure Conversions Used in Physiology
Blood pressure is measured in mmHg; atmospheric is 760 mmHg; partial pressures of O₂ (~160 mmHg) and CO₂ (~0.3 mmHg) drive gas exchange via Fick's Law. Boyle's Law underlies all ventilation; partial pressures govern diffusion across membranes.

#### Review Questions
1. Explain why the chest must expand for inhalation to occur, using Boyle's Law.
2. A diver breathes air at 3 atm (30 m depth) when the air in their lungs is 2.0 L. If they hold their breath and ascend to 1 atm, what volume would their lungs have to hold? Why is this dangerous?
3. A patient on a ventilator has tidal volume 600 mL. How must pressure change to deliver this volume if lungs resist expansion?

## Subtopic: Charles's Law

### Charles's Law: Volume-Temperature Direct Relationship
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** charles-law
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** Charles's Law · V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ · absolute zero
**Summary:** Charles's Law states that at constant pressure, volume and absolute temperature of a gas are directly proportional.

#### The Law
**V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂** (at constant n and P), where T is in **Kelvin** (always!). Volume is directly proportional to absolute temperature: V ∝ T. Converting to Kelvin: K = °C + 273.15 (use 273 for most calculations).

#### Why Kelvin Matters
Using Celsius leads to wrong answers. At −273°C, volume would theoretically reach zero (absolute zero). Celsius temperature is offset; Kelvin starts at true zero of molecular motion. A gas at 0°C is NOT at zero molecular motion — it's at 273 K.

#### Standard Calculations
**Example 1:** A gas occupies 5.00 L at 27°C. Volume at 127°C, constant pressure?
T₁ = 300 K, T₂ = 400 K
V₂ = V₁ × T₂/T₁ = 5.00 × (400/300) = **6.67 L**

**Example 2:** A balloon is 10.0 L at 25°C. It's placed in liquid nitrogen (−196°C). New volume?
T₁ = 298 K, T₂ = 77 K
V₂ = 10.0 × (77/298) = **2.59 L**

#### Physical Interpretation (KMT)
Higher temperature → faster molecules → hit walls harder → walls must expand to maintain constant pressure. Lower temperature → slower molecules → weaker wall force → volume decreases.

#### Extrapolation to Absolute Zero
If you plot V vs. T(K) for any ideal gas, you get a straight line that extrapolates to V = 0 at T = 0 K (−273.15°C). All gases would converge at absolute zero — this is how Kelvin was defined. Real gases liquefy before reaching 0 K.

#### Review Questions
1. A gas is heated from 200 K to 400 K at constant pressure. By what factor does volume change?
2. Why must temperature be in Kelvin for Charles's Law, but either Kelvin or Celsius works for Boyle's Law?
3. A gas occupies 3.0 L at 0°C. Volume at 546°C at constant pressure?

### Gay-Lussac's Law and Combined Pressure-Temperature
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** gay-lussac-law
**Estimated time:** 11 min
**Key concepts:** Gay-Lussac's Law · P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂ · sealed containers
**Summary:** Gay-Lussac's Law states that at constant volume, pressure and absolute temperature are directly proportional.

#### The Law
**P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂** (at constant n and V). Pressure is directly proportional to absolute temperature when volume is fixed. This applies to sealed rigid containers.

**Example:** A tire at 20°C reads 32 psi. After a long drive at 60°C, what is the pressure?
T₁ = 293 K, T₂ = 333 K
P₂ = 32 × (333/293) = **36.4 psi**

This is why tire pressure recommendations specify cold inflation — warm tires read higher.

#### Safety Applications
Sealed containers (aerosol cans, propane tanks) carry warnings not to heat above certain temperatures. By Gay-Lussac's Law, the pressure inside increases proportionally with temperature. A propane tank at 20°C (293 K) with 200 psi reaches 273 psi at 80°C (353 K) — still safe. At 500°C (773 K), pressure would be ~530 psi — potentially catastrophic.

#### Autoclave Sterilization
Medical autoclaves use steam at 121°C and 15 psi above atmospheric (29.7 psia) to sterilize equipment. The elevated pressure keeps water liquid above 100°C and allows higher temperature — more effective sterilization.

#### Review Questions
1. A sealed cylinder holds gas at 1.5 atm and 300 K. What is the pressure at 450 K?
2. Explain in KMT terms why pressure increases when temperature increases in a rigid container.
3. Why do aerosol cans carry warnings against incineration or puncturing when warm?

## Subtopic: Combined Gas Law

### The Combined Gas Law: Unifying Boyle and Charles
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** combined-gas-law
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** combined gas law · P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ · STP
**Summary:** The Combined Gas Law merges Boyle's, Charles's, and Gay-Lussac's Laws into one equation covering simultaneous changes in P, V, and T.

#### The Equation
**P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂** (at constant n)

This equation contains all three two-variable laws as special cases:
- Hold T constant → P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ (Boyle's)
- Hold P constant → V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ (Charles's)
- Hold V constant → P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂ (Gay-Lussac's)

#### Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP)
**STP = 0°C (273 K) and 1 atm.** At STP, 1 mole of ideal gas occupies **22.4 L** (the molar volume). This is a useful conversion factor: 1 mol gas ↔ 22.4 L at STP.

**Note:** An older definition used 0°C and 1 atm; IUPAC's current STP is 0°C and 100 kPa (giving 22.7 L/mol). Most textbooks still use the 22.4 L/mol version.

#### Worked Example
A gas occupies 4.00 L at 25°C and 1.50 atm. What volume does it occupy at 0°C and 3.00 atm?
- P₁ = 1.50, V₁ = 4.00, T₁ = 298 K
- P₂ = 3.00, T₂ = 273 K
- V₂ = (1.50 × 4.00 × 273) / (298 × 3.00) = 1638/894 = **1.83 L**

Check: pressure doubled → volume halved (1.83 vs. 2.0 is close); temperature decreased → volume decreased further → both effects reduce volume. ✓

#### Review Questions
1. A gas at 2.0 atm, 10.0 L, 300 K is changed to 1.0 atm and 400 K. Find the new volume.
2. Convert 5.00 L of gas at 100°C and 0.500 atm to STP conditions. What volume?
3. A diver's 12 L tank at 200 atm and 20°C is used to 1 atm at 37°C. How many liters of gas were released (at final conditions)?

### Combined Gas Law Applications: Weather and Industry
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** combined-gas-law-applications
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** weather balloons · gas cylinders · combined gas law in practice
**Summary:** Real-world applications of the Combined Gas Law in meteorology, diving, and industrial gas handling.

#### Weather Balloons
A weather balloon is launched with 1.0 m³ of helium at ground level (1.0 atm, 20°C). At 30 km altitude, P = 0.010 atm and T = −45°C.
- T₁ = 293 K, T₂ = 228 K
- V₂ = V₁ × (P₁/P₂) × (T₂/T₁) = 1.0 × (1.0/0.010) × (228/293) = **77.8 m³**

The balloon expands 78-fold! Balloons are designed to burst at altitude (when expanded too large) and the instrument package floats down by parachute.

#### Scuba Tank Calculations
A 12 L scuba tank at 20°C contains air at 200 atm. How many liters of air (at 1 atm, 37°C body temperature) can a diver breathe?
Using combined law: V₂ = V₁ × (P₁/P₂) × (T₂/T₁) = 12 × (200/1) × (310/293) = **2469 L**

At a breathing rate of ~15 L/min, the tank lasts ~165 min at the surface — much less at depth (higher pressure means more molecules per breath needed).

#### Industrial Gas Cylinders
Oxygen cylinders used in hospitals are compressed to ~2000 psi (136 atm) at room temperature. If the cylinder is accidentally heated in a fire (say to 500°C), the pressure increases by Gay-Lussac's Law: P₂ = 136 × (773/293) = **358 atm** — why cylinders can become rockets if the valve fails in a fire.

#### Review Questions
1. A balloon holds 2.0 L at 1.0 atm and 25°C. It's placed in a freezer at 0°C and then squeezed to 0.5 atm. Find the new volume.
2. Why do scuba divers consume their air supply faster at greater depths?
3. A gas occupies 15.0 L at STP. Find its volume at 3.00 atm and 100°C.

## Subtopic: Ideal Gas Law

### The Ideal Gas Law: PV = nRT
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** ideal-gas-law
**Estimated time:** 13 min
**Key concepts:** PV = nRT · gas constant R · molar mass from ideal gas law
**Summary:** The Ideal Gas Law combines all gas variables (P, V, n, T) into one equation, PV = nRT, enabling calculation of any variable when the others are known.

#### The Equation
**PV = nRT**
- P = pressure (atm)
- V = volume (L)
- n = moles of gas
- R = 0.08206 L·atm/mol·K (the ideal gas constant)
- T = temperature (Kelvin, always)

When using kPa: R = 8.314 L·kPa/mol·K. When using mmHg: R = 62.36 L·mmHg/mol·K.

#### Derivation from Combined Gas Law
The Combined Gas Law (P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂) applies at constant moles. Adding that PV/T ∝ n gives PV/T = nR → PV = nRT.

#### Standard Calculations
**Example 1 — Find pressure:** 2.00 mol N₂ at 25°C in 10.0 L.
P = nRT/V = (2.00)(0.08206)(298)/(10.0) = **4.89 atm**

**Example 2 — Find moles:** 3.5 L of gas at 1.2 atm and 300 K.
n = PV/(RT) = (1.2)(3.5)/[(0.08206)(300)] = 4.2/24.62 = **0.171 mol**

**Example 3 — Find temperature:** 0.500 mol gas in 12.0 L at 2.00 atm.
T = PV/(nR) = (2.00)(12.0)/[(0.500)(0.08206)] = 24/0.0410 = **585 K**

#### Molar Volume at STP
At STP (0°C, 1 atm): V = nRT/P = (1)(0.08206)(273)/1 = **22.4 L/mol** — the molar volume.

#### Molar Mass from PV = nRT
n = PV/(RT); molar mass M = mass/n = m·RT/(PV). This allows determination of molar mass from gas density measurements.

#### Review Questions
1. Find the volume of 3.00 mol of gas at 2.00 atm and 127°C.
2. A gas has mass 1.25 g, occupies 0.500 L at 1.0 atm and 25°C. Find its molar mass.
3. How many molecules are in 5.00 L of gas at STP? (Use Avogadro's number: 6.022 × 10²³)

### Ideal Gas Law: Density and Molar Mass Applications
**Type:** Application
**Slug:** ideal-gas-law-density-molar-mass
**Estimated time:** 14 min
**Key concepts:** gas density · molar mass calculation · ideal gas law in analysis
**Summary:** Using the Ideal Gas Law to determine gas density and identify unknown gases by their molar mass.

#### Gas Density from Ideal Gas Law
Rearranging PV = nRT with n = m/M (mass/molar mass):
PV = (m/M)RT → **d = PM/(RT)** where d = m/V = density in g/L.

**Example:** Density of O₂ at STP?
d = (1.00 atm)(32.00 g/mol) / [(0.08206)(273)] = 32.00/22.4 = **1.43 g/L**

Notice: denser (heavier) gases have higher density at same conditions. Helium (M=4): d = 4/22.4 = 0.178 g/L — much less dense than air (M_air ≈ 29: d ≈ 1.29 g/L).

#### Identifying an Unknown Gas
A gas has density 1.96 g/L at STP. What is it?
M = dRT/P = 1.96 × 22.4 = **43.9 g/mol** ≈ 44 g/mol → **CO₂** or **propane (C₃H₈)** — need additional info to distinguish.

#### Real Lab: Dumas Method
In the Dumas method, a small amount of volatile liquid is vaporized in a flask of known volume at known T and P. The condensed gas is weighed. Using PV = nRT: n = PV/(RT), then M = mass/n.

**Example:** 0.150 g of unknown liquid vaporizes in a 250 mL flask at 1.00 atm and 100°C.
n = (1.00)(0.250)/[(0.08206)(373)] = 0.250/30.61 = 0.00817 mol
M = 0.150/0.00817 = **18.4 g/mol** → close to water (18.02) ✓

#### Stoichiometry with Ideal Gas Law
Gases produced in reactions can be quantified. 2H₂O₂(l) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g). If 0.100 mol H₂O₂ decomposes, moles O₂ = 0.0500 mol. Volume at 25°C, 1 atm: V = nRT/P = (0.0500)(0.08206)(298)/1.00 = **1.22 L**.

#### Review Questions
1. Calculate the density of CO₂ at 25°C and 1.00 atm.
2. An unknown gas has density 2.86 g/L at 0°C and 1 atm. Identify the gas.
3. 5.00 g of Na reacts with excess water. 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂. Find the volume of H₂ at 25°C and 1 atm.

### Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures
**Type:** Core Understanding
**Slug:** daltons-law-partial-pressures
**Estimated time:** 12 min
**Key concepts:** partial pressure · Dalton's Law · mole fraction
**Summary:** Dalton's Law states that total pressure of a gas mixture equals the sum of partial pressures of each component.

#### The Law
**P_total = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + …**
Each gas in a mixture behaves independently (consistent with KMT — no intermolecular forces between different gas molecules). The partial pressure of gas *i* is the pressure it would exert alone:
**P_i = x_i × P_total** where x_i = mole fraction = n_i/n_total.

#### Calculating Partial Pressures
**Example:** A container holds 2.00 mol N₂ and 0.500 mol O₂ at 5.00 atm total.
- x_N₂ = 2.00/2.50 = 0.800; P_N₂ = 0.800 × 5.00 = **4.00 atm**
- x_O₂ = 0.500/2.50 = 0.200; P_O₂ = 0.200 × 5.00 = **1.00 atm**

#### Collecting Gas Over Water
Gas collected by water displacement is always contaminated with water vapor. Total pressure = P_gas + P_H₂O. At 25°C, vapor pressure of water = 23.8 mmHg. If total P = 760 mmHg: P_gas = 760 − 23.8 = **736.2 mmHg**.

#### Breathing Gas Mixtures
Air: ~78% N₂, ~21% O₂, ~1% Ar, 0.04% CO₂. At 1 atm (760 mmHg):
- P_O₂ = 0.21 × 760 = **160 mmHg** (this drives O₂ into blood)
- P_CO₂ = 0.0004 × 760 = **0.3 mmHg** (drives CO₂ out of blood)

At 8848 m (Everest), P_total ≈ 253 mmHg, P_O₂ ≈ 53 mmHg — severely hypoxic without supplemental oxygen.

#### Review Questions
1. A mixture of 1.00 mol He, 2.00 mol Ne, and 1.00 mol Ar is in a 5.00 L container at 25°C. Find P_total and P_He.
2. O₂ is collected over water at 20°C (vapor pressure of water = 17.5 mmHg). Total pressure = 740 mmHg. Find P_O₂ and volume of dry O₂.
3. Why does the partial pressure of oxygen in air at high altitude decrease, even though the fraction of oxygen (21%) stays the same?

#### Toss-Up Patterns
**Pattern 1 — PV = nRT directly:**
"For 10 points — what is the volume in liters occupied by 1.00 mole of ideal gas at standard temperature and pressure?"
→ **22.4 liters**

**Pattern 2 — Identify the law:**
"For 10 points — this law states that the total pressure of a mixture of gases equals the sum of the partial pressures of each gas."
→ **Dalton's Law** (of Partial Pressures)

**Pattern 3 — Name the law for inverse P-V:**
"For 10 points — what law states that, at constant temperature, the pressure of a gas is inversely proportional to its volume?"
→ **Boyle's Law**

**Pattern 4 — Ideal gas constant:**
"For 10 points — in the ideal gas equation PV = nRT, what is the numerical value and units of R in liter-atmospheres per mole-Kelvin?"
→ **0.08206 L·atm/mol·K**

#### Speed Strategies
- Always check: is T in Kelvin? Is R consistent with pressure units?
- For ratios of two states, use combined law (cleaner algebra than plugging into PV=nRT twice)
- Mole fraction × total pressure = partial pressure (one step, no KT calculation needed)
- STP = 0°C, 1 atm, 22.4 L/mol — memorize cold
