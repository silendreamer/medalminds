#### Ideal Gas Assumptions and Their Limits

The **ideal gas law** (PV = nRT) assumes gas molecules have zero volume and exert no intermolecular forces. Real gases violate both assumptions, especially at **high pressure** (molecules are close together, volume matters) and **low temperature** (slow molecules are more susceptible to attractive forces). Under these conditions, measured pressures and volumes diverge noticeably from ideal predictions.

#### Intermolecular Attractions

**Attractive intermolecular forces** (London dispersion, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding) cause real gas molecules to pull on each other. This reduces the force with which molecules strike container walls, so the **measured pressure is lower** than the ideal prediction. Gases with stronger intermolecular forces (e.g., polar molecules or large, polarizable molecules) show larger deviations.

#### Molecular Volume

At high pressures, the finite **volume of the gas molecules themselves** becomes significant compared to the container volume. The space actually available for molecular motion is less than V, so the **effective volume is larger** than PV = nRT predicts. Larger molecules (e.g., CO2 vs. He) exhibit more pronounced volume corrections.

#### The van der Waals Equation

The **van der Waals equation** corrects both effects:

(P + a(n/V)^2)(V - nb) = nRT

- The term **a(n/V)^2** (where a > 0) adds back the pressure reduction due to attractions.
- The term **nb** subtracts the excluded volume occupied by the molecules themselves (b is the molar volume of the molecules).
- **a** is large for polar or easily polarized gases; **b** correlates with molecular size. For an ideal gas, a = 0 and b = 0 reduce the equation to PV = nRT.

#### Review Questions
1. A sample of CO2 at high pressure has a measured pressure lower than predicted by the ideal gas law. Which van der Waals correction term accounts for this, and why?
2. Why do real gases most closely approach ideal behavior at high temperature and low pressure?
3. Compare the expected values of the van der Waals constant a for He and NH3, and explain the difference in terms of intermolecular forces.
