#### Elementary Steps and Molecularity

A **reaction mechanism** is the sequence of **elementary steps** that collectively describe how reactants convert to products. Each elementary step is a single molecular event — a **unimolecular** step involves one reactant (e.g., isomerization or decomposition), while a **bimolecular** step involves a collision between two species. The **molecularity** of a step equals the number of molecules (or ions) that must collide simultaneously. Unlike the overall reaction, the rate law for each elementary step can be written directly from its stoichiometry.

#### Intermediates vs. Transition States

A **reaction intermediate** is a species produced in one elementary step and consumed in a later step; it appears in the mechanism but not in the overall balanced equation. Intermediates occupy local energy minima on the potential energy diagram. By contrast, a **transition state** is the highest-energy species along the reaction coordinate — it cannot be isolated. Identifying intermediates is a key step in verifying that a proposed mechanism is consistent with the observed stoichiometry of the overall reaction.

#### Rate-Determining Step

The **rate-determining step (RDS)** is the slowest elementary step in a mechanism and acts as the bottleneck for the entire reaction. The overall rate law is controlled by the RDS: the rate law for the overall reaction must match the rate law derived from the RDS. For example, if the RDS is bimolecular — A + B → products — the rate law is rate = k[A][B]. If a fast equilibrium precedes the RDS, the concentration of an intermediate can be expressed in terms of reactants using the equilibrium constant before substituting into the rate law.

#### Verifying a Mechanism

A valid mechanism must satisfy two criteria: (1) the elementary steps must sum to give the correct overall **balanced equation**, and (2) the rate law predicted by the RDS must agree with the experimentally determined rate law. Adding up the elementary steps means canceling species that appear on both sides (intermediates cancel; catalysts should reappear as products of a later step). If either criterion fails, the proposed mechanism must be revised.

#### Review Questions

1. In a two-step mechanism, the first step produces intermediate X and the second step is the rate-determining step that consumes X. What is the overall rate law if the first step is a fast equilibrium A ⇌ X with K_eq, and the RDS is X + B → products with rate constant k?
2. Distinguish between a reaction intermediate and a transition state in terms of energy and isolability.
3. A proposed mechanism has three elementary steps that sum to give the net equation 2A + B → C + D. What condition must the mechanism also satisfy regarding the experimentally observed rate law?
