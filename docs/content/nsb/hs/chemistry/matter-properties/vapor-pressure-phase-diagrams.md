#### Vapor Pressure
Vapor pressure is the partial pressure of a substance's vapor in equilibrium with its liquid at a given temperature. More precisely: in a closed container, liquid molecules constantly evaporate while vapor molecules condense. Equilibrium is reached when evaporation rate = condensation rate. The pressure of vapor at this equilibrium = vapor pressure. Key relationships: (1) VP increases with temperature (more particles have enough KE to escape). (2) VP depends on IMF strength — weaker IMFs → higher VP (more volatile). (3) Boiling occurs when VP = atmospheric pressure.

#### The Phase Diagram
A phase diagram shows which state of matter is stable at any combination of temperature and pressure. Key features: **Triple point**: the one unique temperature and pressure where all three phases coexist. For water: 273.16 K, 611.7 Pa. **Critical point**: the temperature and pressure above which liquid and gas become indistinguishable (supercritical fluid). For water: 374°C, 218 atm. **Solid-liquid boundary**: for most substances, slopes positively (higher pressure favors solid). For water, slopes negatively (higher pressure favors liquid — explains pressure melting of ice).

#### Water's Unique Phase Diagram
Water's solid-liquid boundary has a negative slope (unusual). This means applying pressure at 0°C converts ice to water — explaining ice skating (though the primary mechanism is actually frictional heat). Carbon dioxide's phase diagram is more typical, with a positive solid-liquid slope. At 1 atm pressure, CO₂ has no stable liquid phase — dry ice sublimes directly to gas (sublimation point: −78.5°C).

#### Supercritical Fluids
Above the critical point, a substance is a supercritical fluid — it has the density of a liquid but diffuses like a gas. Supercritical CO₂ is used industrially as a solvent for coffee decaffeination and pharmaceutical extraction. It's environmentally friendly (no organic solvent residue) and the properties can be tuned by adjusting P and T.

#### Review Questions
1. On a water phase diagram, mark the triple point, critical point, and normal boiling point. Explain what happens when you increase pressure at 200°C.
2. Why does CO₂ sublimate at 1 atm rather than melting?
3. A liquid has a higher vapor pressure than water at 25°C. What can you conclude about its intermolecular forces and boiling point compared to water?
