#### Scenario 1: Wine Production — Fractional Distillation
Fermented grape juice contains water (bp 100°C), ethanol (bp 78.4°C), and hundreds of other compounds. To concentrate ethanol, winemakers use distillation (commercial spirits use fractional distillation). A fractionating column allows vapors to repeatedly condense and re-evaporate, enriching the ethanol fraction. The vapor exiting the column is richer in ethanol than the original mixture because ethanol has the lower boiling point.

Pure ethanol cannot be obtained by simple distillation from water due to azeotrope formation at 95.6% ethanol (the azeotrope boils at 78.1°C, lower than pure ethanol, and co-distills). Obtaining 100% ethanol requires molecular sieves or chemical dehydration.

#### Scenario 2: Drug Identification via Chromatography
A forensic chemist suspects a white powder contains three substances. She dissolves it and runs thin-layer chromatography (TLC). Four spots appear; three match known standards. The Rf values: aspirin = 0.45, caffeine = 0.25, unknown compound = 0.67. The unknown's Rf doesn't match the standards in this solvent system. She switches to a different solvent (changes the mobile phase polarity) and reruns. This changes all Rf values, but their relative relationships can still reveal the compound's identity by comparison to a reference database.

#### Scenario 3: Separating a Ternary Mixture
Given: a mixture of iron filings, sand (SiO₂), and salt (NaCl). Optimal separation sequence: (1) Magnetic separation — magnet removes iron (exploits magnetic property). (2) Add water — salt dissolves, sand doesn't (exploits differential solubility). (3) Filter — separates sand from saltwater (exploits particle size). (4) Evaporate water from filtrate — recovers salt. Four pure components from four physical techniques; no chemical reactions needed.

2. Design a separation procedure for a mixture of large silica beads, sugar, and cooking oil.
